# 3 Days in Paris: The Perfect First-Timer's Itinerary

Paris rewards people who walk slowly and resist the urge to check off lists. The city's greatest moments tend to come between attractions — a perfect croque-monsieur at a zinc bar, a courtyard you ducked into to check a map, the smell of a boulangerie at 7 AM. Three days is enough to see the essential Paris and start to feel it.

## Day 1: Right Bank Classics — Louvre, Marais, and a Seine Walk

### Morning (8:30 AM – 1:00 PM)

Arrive at the **Louvre** before it opens at 9 AM and you'll be near the front of the queue rather than in the middle of it. Buy tickets online (€22) in advance — the queue without them is brutal. The museum contains 35,000 works; trying to see everything is what ruins most Louvre visits. Pick three rooms and go deep: the **Winged Victory of Samothrace** at the top of the Daru staircase (one of the great sculptures in the world, incomprehensibly underrated), the **Vermeer rooms** in the Dutch Masters section, and the **Italian paintings** — yes, see the Mona Lisa, but stand in front of **The Wedding at Cana** (the painting opposite) which is 15 times larger and arguably more beautiful. Budget 2.5 hours.

Walk through the Tuileries Garden toward **Place de la Concorde** and east along the Seine embankment — the booksellers (*bouquinistes*) have been selling secondhand books, prints, and postcards from their green metal boxes along the quai since the 16th century. Browse for 20 minutes.

**Morning fuel:** Skip the café inside the Louvre (expensive, mediocre). Instead, buy a croissant from any boulangerie you pass on the way — in Paris, any random boulangerie is better than a destination one elsewhere. A proper croissant should shatter when you bite it. Cost: €1.20–1.50.

### Afternoon (1:00 PM – 7:00 PM)

Cross to the **Île de la Cité** — the island in the Seine where Paris began. Walk through the ancient flower market (open daily except Monday) and arrive at **Notre-Dame Cathedral** — which, depending on when you read this, may be in the final stages of its post-2019-fire reconstruction and restoration. The exterior alone is worth studying: the three rose windows, the flying buttresses that were revolutionary in the 12th century, the carved portal tympana depicting Last Judgment scenes.

Cross to the **Île Saint-Louis** — the smaller island immediately behind Île de la Cité — and walk the length of Rue Saint-Louis-en-l'Île, stopping for ice cream at **Berthillon** (€3–4 per scoop, the city's most famous glacier since 1954 — the caramel salted butter flavour is the one).

Spend the afternoon in the **Marais** district on the Right Bank: the city's former Jewish quarter, now an elegant mix of old hôtels particuliers (private mansions turned museums), contemporary galleries, falafel shops on Rue des Rosiers, and some of the city's best independent fashion.

Key stops in the Marais:
- **Place des Vosges** — the oldest planned square in Paris (1612), arcaded in stone, with symmetrical red-brick buildings. Sit on the lawn.
- **Musée Picasso** (€14) — 5,000 of Picasso's works housed in a 17th-century mansion. More intimate and enjoyable than any Picasso show you've seen elsewhere.
- **Merci** concept store on Boulevard Beaumarchais — architecture, design, and clothing with a ground-floor café

### Evening (7:00 PM – late)

Dinner at a **classic Parisian bistro** in the Marais or along Rue de Bretagne: *steak frites* (€18–24), *confit de canard* (€20), a carafe of house Bordeaux (€8–12). The formula is perfect and has been perfect for 150 years. **Chez Janou** near Place des Vosges does Provençal food with an enormous pastis selection; **Le Gamin de Paris** on Rue Vieille du Temple is for classic bistro in the most classic Marais setting.

Walk to the **Centre Pompidou** after dinner — it stays lit at night, the exterior escalators running through clear tubes are visible from the plaza, and the square in front (Place Georges-Pompidou) is alive with street performers and musicians until late.

## Day 2: Left Bank and Eiffel Tower

### Morning (8:00 AM – 1:00 PM)

Cross the Seine to the **Left Bank** and start in **Saint-Germain-des-Prés** — Paris's literary quarter. The **Café de Flore** and **Les Deux Magots** are famous (Sartre and de Beauvoir drank here, Hemingway drank everywhere) and worth a coffee (€5–7) for the architecture and the people-watching, even if the service is occasionally snooty.

Walk south through the **Luxembourg Gardens** — one of Paris's finest parks, with a formal fountain basin where children race model sailboats on Wednesday afternoons and the *pétanque* players are there most mornings. Continue to the **Panthéon** (€13) — the neoclassical temple housing France's greatest citizens: Victor Hugo, Marie and Pierre Curie, Voltaire, Rousseau. The Foucault Pendulum hanging from the dome demonstrates Earth's rotation.

**Left Bank market:** **Marché Raspail** (Sundays only) or **Marché d'Aligre** (daily except Monday) for the city's most authentic produce and food market experience — olives, cheese, charcuterie, bread. Budget €10–15 for lunch provisions.

### Afternoon (12:30 PM – 7:00 PM)

The **Musée d'Orsay** (€16, book ahead) is the better art museum. Housed in a converted Belle Époque railway station, it contains the world's most complete collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist works — every major Monet, Renoir, Degas, Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin. The building itself is extraordinary: the old station clock faces now frame galleries of colour. Budget 2 hours minimum.

Walk west along the Seine embankment to the **Champ de Mars** — the park in front of the Eiffel Tower. Approach slowly from the east end and let the tower reveal itself gradually. The Eiffel Tower is one of those structures that photographs have ruined because the photographs are so ubiquitous — and then you see it in real life at the end of a long green park and it's somehow still magical. Buy a baguette sandwich from any nearby boulangerie (€4–6) and have a picnic on the lawn beneath it.

Book tickets online for the **Eiffel Tower** summit (€29.40) — the queue without tickets takes 2–3 hours. At 276 metres, the second floor has arguably the better view — clear visibility, closer to the city's architecture. The summit feels more abstract. Go to the summit.

### Evening (7:00 PM – midnight)

Dinner at **Café du Commerce** near Trocadéro — a multi-level 1920s brasserie on three floors surrounding a glass atrium, serving classic Parisian food in a room that's barely changed in a century. Affordable by Paris standards (€20–30 for a main).

Take the Métro to **Montmartre** for the evening. Climb the hill to **Sacré-Cœur Basilica** after dinner — the white stone dome is lit from within and visible across the entire city at night. The **place du Tertre** behind it is genuinely beautiful without the day's portrait painters, and the narrow streets descending the hill in the direction of **Abbesses** Métro station — particularly **Rue Lepic** — are the Paris of film and fantasy, still largely intact.

## Day 3: Versailles Morning, Montmartre Afternoon

### Morning (8:00 AM – 2:00 PM)

Take the RER C from any Left Bank station to **Versailles** (€7.20 each way, 40 minutes). Arrive at the palace at 9 AM when it opens. Skip the audio guide and walk directly to the **Hall of Mirrors** first — 357 mirrors facing 357 arched windows along 73 metres of gilded gallery. It doesn't photograph. It has to be walked.

The **gardens** (free when the fountains are off, €9.50 on fountain days) are a separate 800-hectare world that most visitors spend 30 minutes in and should spend 3 hours in. Rent a bicycle at the garden entrance (€7/hour) and ride to the **Grand Trianon**, Marie Antoinette's private estate at the far end of the gardens — a pink marble pavilion surrounded by formal French gardens, quieter and more beautiful than the main palace.

Return to Paris by 2 PM.

### Afternoon (2:00 PM – 7:00 PM)

**Montmartre** in daylight: the neighbourhood has two personalities. The tourist-facing **Place du Tertre** with portrait painters and overpriced cafés is one. The back streets — **Rue Lepic**, **Rue des Abbesses**, **Rue Caulaincourt** — are the other, where locals live in apartments with flower boxes, the boulangeries are exceptional, and the city view from the cemetery garden is better than from Sacré-Cœur.

**Musée de Montmartre** (€15) documents the neighbourhood's bohemian period when Renoir, Utrillo, and Toulouse-Lautrec all had studios there. Small, personal, worth an hour.

For the best Paris view that isn't the Eiffel Tower: **La Crypte de la Basilique** — the Sacré-Cœur's crypt — has a narrow staircase to the roof of the dome that most visitors don't know exists. On a clear afternoon, the city stretches south as far as you can see.

### Evening (7:00 PM onwards)

Farewell dinner at **Le Baratin** in Belleville — a small, legendary wine bar and restaurant where the handwritten menu changes daily and the natural wine list is one of the best in Paris. Book ahead; the 12 tables fill weeks in advance. €40–60 per person for the full experience.

Or: **Septime** in Bastille, one of the city's most talked-about bistros — modernist French cuisine, outstanding value relative to quality (€50–60 menu), perpetually difficult reservation.

End the evening walking the **Canal Saint-Martin** — the covered/open canal in the 10th arrondissement where the city's young professionals have dinner on the iron footbridges in warm months. Less famous than the Seine, more alive.

## Getting There & Around

Fly into Charles de Gaulle (CDG) — the RER B train connects the airport to central Paris in 35 minutes (€12.10). Get a **Navigo Easy** card (€2) at any station and load passes onto it: a 24-hour all-zones pass is €8.65 and covers unlimited Métro, RER, and bus. For the city itself, the Métro is excellent and a taxi from anywhere to anywhere is €15–25.
