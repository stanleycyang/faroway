# 3 Days in Tokyo: The Perfect Weekend Itinerary

Where ancient temples meet neon skyscrapers — and somehow, impossibly, it works. Tokyo is the world's largest city and one of its most navigable, with a subway system so logical it'll make you angry at every city you've ever lived in. Three days is enough to fall in love. It's not enough to finish. Here's how to spend them.

## Day 1: Foundations — Old Tokyo and the Icons

### Morning (8:00 AM – 12:00 PM)

Start where Tokyo started. Take the subway to Asakusa and arrive at **Senso-ji Temple** before 9 AM, when tour groups are still eating breakfast. Walk through Kaminarimon Gate — the giant red lantern will be in soft morning light, and the incense smoke from the main hall drifts thick enough to feel it in your clothes. Spend 30 minutes exploring the temple grounds, then turn around and wander the narrow lanes of **Nakamise-dori** for the best souvenir shopping in the city: ningyo dolls, matcha Kit-Kats, hand-painted fans, yukata fabric.

From Asakusa, walk ten minutes northeast to the **Tokyo Skytree** (634m, the world's tallest broadcast tower). The views from the Tembo Deck on a clear day stretch to Mt. Fuji. Budget 1.5 hours including the queue; arrive before 10 AM on weekdays to skip the worst of it. Cost: ¥2,100 for the main deck.

**Morning fuel:** Grab a steaming cup of sweet amazake (fermented rice drink) from a street stall near Senso-ji before you go — ¥300, unforgettable in cold weather.

### Afternoon (12:00 PM – 6:00 PM)

Cross the city to **Shibuya** for lunch. The options within a 10-minute walk of the crossing are overwhelming, but **Ichiran Ramen** on Dogenzaka offers private ramen booths where you fill out a flavour checklist — broth richness, spice level, green onions yes or no — and a bowl appears through a small curtain. Solo dining perfected. Cost: ¥1,000.

After lunch, walk to the **Shibuya Crossing** and cross it. Then cross it again from a different corner. Then go upstairs to the Starbucks on the second floor of Shibuya Scramble Square and watch from above — it's genuinely one of the great spectacles of modern city life, 3,000 people flowing in every direction like a murmuration of starlings.

Spend the late afternoon in **Harajuku**: walk the tree-lined Omotesando Avenue for an architecture tour of flagship stores (Prada, Dior, and Tod's all have Pritzker-worthy buildings), then duck into the backstreets of **Ura-Harajuku** for vintage clothing, independent designers, and the creative street style the neighbourhood is actually famous for. Takeshita Street is Instagram gold but commercially hollow — skip it or do five minutes.

### Evening (6:00 PM – 10:00 PM)

Take the Yamanote Line three stops south to **Ebisu** and walk to **Ebisu Garden Place** for the golden hour view over the city from the plaza. Then settle into the neighbourhood: Ebisu and adjacent **Daikanyama** have Tokyo's best concentration of wine bars, craft beer spots, and small-plate restaurants. Try **Afuri** in Daikanyama for yuzu-salt ramen — the broth is lighter and more citrus-fragrant than anything you've had before. Expect a short queue; worth it.

**Where to stay (Day 1 base):** Shinjuku is the best hub for 3 nights — central, dense with convenience stores, and on every major train line.
- *Budget:* Khaosan World Asakusa (¥3,500/night)
- *Mid-range:* Hotel Gracery Shinjuku (¥12,000/night, Godzilla head on the roof)
- *Splurge:* Park Hyatt Tokyo, Shinjuku (from ¥80,000/night — the Lost in Translation hotel)

## Day 2: Neighbourhoods — Modern Tokyo and Local Life

### Morning (8:00 AM – 12:30 PM)

Start at **Meiji Shrine** in Harajuku, Tokyo's most serene Shinto shrine, set inside an 83-hectare forested park that feels entirely removed from the city pressing against its borders. Arrive by 8:30 AM. Walk the long gravel path through towering torii gates, stop at the purification fountain before entering the main hall, and take your time. Sundays often feature traditional wedding processions. Free admission; the entire visit takes about 45 minutes.

From Meiji Shrine, walk 15 minutes east to **Omotesando** for brunch at **Bread, Espresso &** — sourdough toast with varietal butters, exceptional espresso, perpetually busy but quick turnover. Then spend 90 minutes exploring **Aoyama**: the Nezu Museum's Japanese garden is one of the city's best-kept secrets (¥1,100 entry), and the surrounding streets contain Tokyo's most innovative independent boutiques.

### Afternoon (12:30 PM – 6:00 PM)

Take the subway to **Akihabara** — Tokyo's electronics and anime district — not because you necessarily care about either, but because there's nowhere else on Earth like it. The main street is lined with 8-story buildings selling every piece of consumer electronics ever manufactured, and the side streets get progressively stranger in the best possible way: maid cafes, retro game stores, doujinshi comic shops, floor-to-ceiling Gundam figurines. Allow 2 hours and buy at least one thing absurd.

From Akihabara, walk 15 minutes northwest to **Kanda** and the dense used-bookshop neighbourhood of **Jimbocho** — 170+ bookstores packed into a few city blocks, many with English-language sections, all worth browsing.

Afternoon coffee at a **third-wave coffee** shop in Kanda — **Koffee Mameya** in Omotesando is the most famous, but Fuglen Tokyo (Norwegian-owned, double-function as a cocktail bar after 6 PM) in Yoyogi is worth the detour.

### Evening (6:00 PM – 11:00 PM)

Head to **Shinjuku** for the evening — specifically the area around **Golden Gai**: a warren of 200+ tiny bars, each seating 6-10 people, each with its own micro-personality. Some play jazz. Some have cats. Some are regulars-only but most welcome tourists. A beer costs ¥700-900; the conversation is free. Spend 2-3 hours drinking your way through 2 or 3 of them.

For dinner before or between bars, **Omoide Yokocho** (Memory Lane) — a smoke-filled alley of yakitori stalls behind Shinjuku Station's west exit — is the classic Tokyo cheap-eat experience. Grilled chicken skin on skewers, ¥150 each, Asahi beer, ¥500. You'll smell like smoke for the rest of the night.

## Day 3: Depth — Hidden Tokyo and Farewell Dinner

### Morning (8:30 AM – 1:00 PM)

Escape the tourist trail completely. Take the Chiyoda Line to **Yanaka** — a neighbourhood in the northeast that survived the 1923 earthquake and the WWII firebombing and looks the way Tokyo might have looked a hundred years ago. Wooden temple gates, narrow lanes, cats sleeping on walls, a cemetery that doubles as the city's best cherry-blossom spot. Walk **Yanaka Ginza** shopping street for craft goods and traditional snacks. Buy a piece of pottery. Have coffee at a machiya townhouse café. Allow 2 hours.

From Yanaka, take the subway to **Tsukiji Outer Market** (the inner wholesale market moved to Toyosu, but the outer market — the street-facing stalls — is still there and still extraordinary). The morning rush slows by 11 AM making it easier to browse: fresh tamagoyaki egg omelets being made to order, tuna on rice for ¥1,200, the world's densest concentration of kitchen knife shops.

### Afternoon (1:00 PM – 6:00 PM)

Cross Hamarikyu Gardens (¥300, one of Tokyo's finest Japanese gardens, directly adjacent to Tsukiji) to the bay. Take the water bus from Hamarikyu to **Odaiba** — a futuristic artificial island in Tokyo Bay — and spend two hours exploring the beachfront, the teamLab digital art borderless experience (book in advance, ¥3,200), and the unobstructed views of Rainbow Bridge and the city skyline.

Final afternoon option for shoppers: take the Yurikamome elevated train back toward central Tokyo and stop at **Ginza** — Tokyo's Mayfair — for window shopping and the best depachika (department store basement food halls) in the world. The basement of Mitsukoshi or Isetan contains more beautiful food than most cities have in their entirety.

### Evening (6:00 PM – last call)

One last dinner. If you haven't yet, **Uobei** in Shibuya for high-speed conveyor belt sushi at ¥110–220 per plate is the most Tokyo thing you can do with your last meal. If you want to spend properly, book a counter seat at any **tempura** restaurant in Ginza — watching a chef fry each piece to order and hand it to you directly is a full sensory experience, not just a meal. Budget ¥8,000–12,000 per person.

Walk the **Imperial Palace East Gardens** at dusk if you finish dinner early — free, and the stone walls lit against the darkening sky are beautiful in a way most tourists never see because they only come during the day.

## Getting There & Around

Fly into Narita (NRT) or Haneda (HND) — Haneda is much closer to the city (30 min by monorail, ¥550) and worth choosing if fares are similar. Buy a **Suica IC card** at any station machine — top it up with ¥3,000-5,000 and it covers all metro, JR, and bus rides across the city, plus convenience store purchases. A single metro ride averages ¥200–250.
