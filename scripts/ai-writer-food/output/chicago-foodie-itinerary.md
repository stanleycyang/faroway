Three days in Chicago taught me one thing: this city doesn't do food halfway. From the first bite of Italian beef dripping onto wax paper to the final forkful of layer cake at a white-tablecloth institution, Chicago earns every calorie. Here's the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I started — a route that makes geographic sense, respects your stomach's recovery time, and hits the dishes worth crossing town for.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Chicagoans eat dinner earlier than New Yorkers — prime time is 6:30-8pm, and popular spots fill up. Book 3-5 days ahead for any restaurant you'd be heartbroken to miss. Tipping runs 18-22% at sit-down restaurants; counter service tip jars expect $1-2. Cards work everywhere, but some legendary cash-only spots remain. The L train connects most food neighborhoods — download the Ventra app and spare yourself the fumbling.

## Day 1: The Classics — Downtown & The Loop

**Morning: The Diner Start You Need**

Begin in the City Center at Lou Mitchell's, a diner that's been feeding the Loop since 1923. The double-yolk eggs and thick-cut bacon are exactly what you'd expect, but the move here is the apple pancakes — caramelized edges, fluffy centers, served with powdered sugar and a side of history. They hand out donut holes and Milk Duds to everyone waiting in line, which tells you everything about Chicago hospitality. Get there by 8am on weekdays, 7:30am on weekends, or wait 45 minutes.

*What to order*: Apple pancakes, double-yolk eggs over-easy, fresh-squeezed orange juice.
*Cost*: $18-24 per person including coffee and tip.

**Afternoon: The Italian Beef Education**

Walk off breakfast through Millennium Park, admire the Bean like a tourist (you should, it's genuinely impressive), then take the L to the Near West Side for the meal that defines Chicago more than any other: Italian beef.

At Al's #1 Italian Beef on Taylor Street, order it "dipped" — the entire sandwich dunked in jus — with hot giardiniera peppers. You'll need napkins. Plural. This is a $10 sandwich that will stay with you for hours, which is good because you're walking to Pilsen next.

Pilsen's 18th Street corridor is where you want to spend your afternoon. This historically Mexican neighborhood has incredible taquerias and bakeries. Stop at any panaderías (I counted four excellent ones in two blocks) and grab a concha or two for pocket snacks. The street murals make this a food walk and art walk in one.

*What to order*: Italian beef dipped with hot peppers, then tacos al pastor at any taqueria that has a trompo (vertical spit) spinning in the window.
*Cost*: $22-30 for beef, tacos, and pastries.

**Evening: Steakhouse, The Chicago Way**

Chicago invented the steakhouse format that every city now copies. Tonight, you experience the original. Head to the Near North for Gene & Georgetti, a 1941 institution where the walls are dark, the martinis are cold, and the garbage salad (their name, not mine) is a pile of vegetables topped with shrimp that somehow works.

The bone-in ribeye here doesn't need explanation. Order it medium-rare, get the cottage fries, and understand why Chicago beef built a city.

*What to order*: Garbage salad to start, bone-in ribeye medium-rare, cottage fries, one martini.
*Cost*: $85-140 per person depending on wine choices.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$125 | Mid ~$180 | Splurge ~$195

## Day 2: Neighborhood Crawl — Wicker Park to Logan Square

**Morning: The Brunch That Earned Its Reputation**

Start in Wicker Park at Giant, a restaurant that takes eggs more seriously than most places take their entire menu. The space is bright, the coffee comes from local roasters, and the shakshuka or soft scrambled eggs with bottarga will make you question every brunch you've had before. This isn't a greasy spoon morning — it's fuel for a day of eating.

*What to order*: Soft scrambled eggs, whatever seasonal vegetable side they're running, coffee.
*Cost*: $28-38 with coffee and tip.

**Afternoon: The Hot Dog Argument & Market Snacking**

No Chicago trip is complete without a Chicago-style hot dog, which means: yellow mustard, chopped white onion, neon green relish, tomato wedges, pickle spear, sport peppers, celery salt, on a poppy seed bun. No ketchup. Ever. This is law.

Take the L toward the Old Town area and find the nearest Vienna Beef-supplied stand (they're everywhere, ask any local). The dog costs $4-6 and is perfect. Eat it standing up.

Then continue to Chicago French Market in the West Loop, a covered market with everything from oysters to crepes to empanadas. This is your grazing lunch — grab a banh mi from Saigon Sisters, oysters from whatever looks freshest, maybe a cheese plate to share. Move slowly. Try things. Talk to vendors.

*What to order*: One Chicago dog, then $20-25 worth of market wandering.
*Cost*: $28-35 including the hot dog and market snacks.

**Evening: The Hidden Spot Worth Finding**

Logan Square has a BYOB Filipino spot called Isla that most tourists will never find. It's small — maybe 30 seats — and the kare-kare (oxtail peanut stew) is transcendent. The bistek tagalog comes with enough garlic rice to make tomorrow's plans irrelevant. Bring wine from the bottle shop two doors down.

This is the kind of meal you remember differently than restaurant meals. It's personal. Someone's grandmother's recipes, scaled up just enough. Reserve ahead.

*What to order*: Kare-kare, bistek tagalog, pancit, ube dessert if available.
*Cost*: $35-50 per person plus whatever you spend on wine.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$90 | Mid ~$120 | Splurge ~$150

## Day 3: The Farewell Tour — South Side to Waterfront Finale

**Morning: Soul Food Breakfast**

Head south to Bronzeville for a breakfast that puts everything in perspective. Chicago's South Side has a soul food tradition that rivals any Southern city. At Pearl's Place, the smothered chicken and waffles arrive on a plate the size of your ambitions. The grits are creamy. The hot sauce is on the table. This is breakfast as a full-body experience.

*What to order*: Chicken and waffles, cheese grits, hot coffee.
*Cost*: $22-28 with tip.

**Afternoon: Deep-Dish Debate & Waterfront Walk**

You can't leave Chicago without trying deep-dish pizza, even though Chicagoans actually eat thin-crust tavern-style more often. The deep-dish is ritual, not daily bread.

Go to Pequod's in Lincoln Park. Not because it's the oldest, but because the caramelized cheese crust — blackened and crispy around the edges — is what everyone talks about for a reason. Order a small (it's enough, trust me), and accept that you need 45 minutes for it to arrive. This is not fast food.

After, walk the Waterfront along the lakefront trail to settle your stomach. The views of the skyline at golden hour will make you understand why Chicagoans tolerate the winters.

*What to order*: Small deep-dish, either sausage or mushroom-pepper, one beer.
*Cost*: $25-35 with beer.

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

For your final dinner, head to the Arts Quarter (West Loop's Restaurant Row) for something that shows what Chicago fine dining can be. At Girl & the Goat, Stephanie Izard's flagship, the goat empanadas and wood-oven pig face are dishes that exist nowhere else. It's loud, energetic, and intensely Chicago — ambitious cooking served without pretension.

Book this one two weeks out if you can. It's worth it.

*What to order*: Goat empanadas, pig face, whatever vegetable preparation sounds weird (it will be excellent), escabeche if available.
*Cost*: $75-120 per person with drinks.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$125 | Mid ~$175 | Splurge ~$185

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Italian beef protocol**: Order "dipped" your first time — wet sandwich, maximum flavor. Say "hot" for giardiniera (spicy) or "sweet" for roasted peppers. You can always ask for both.

- **Deep-dish timing**: Call ahead and pre-order if you're on a schedule. These pizzas take 35-50 minutes. Pequod's and Lou Malnati's both allow this.

- **Tipping at counters**: Chicago counter-service spots increasingly suggest 20% on the screen. $2-3 on orders under $15 is standard; don't let the tablet guilt you.

- **Neighborhood safety after dark**: Stick to well-lit main streets in Pilsen and the South Side. Use rideshare after 10pm if you're unfamiliar with the area. The food neighborhoods are generally fine, but ask your server if unsure about walking routes.
