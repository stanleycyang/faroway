# Buenos Aires Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Buenos Aires

Three days. One mission: to eat Buenos Aires the way porteños actually eat it — late dinners over smoky grills, medialunas dunked in coffee at 11 AM, and enough dulce de leche to recalibrate your entire relationship with sweetness. This is the itinerary I wish I'd had before I arrived, built from three days of obsessive eating, wrong turns that led to great empanadas, and parrillas where nobody spoke English but everyone understood what I wanted when I pointed at the grill.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Dinner doesn't exist before 9 PM here — arrive at 8 and you'll eat alone with the waiters. Bring US dollars in cash and exchange at the "blue dollar" rate (ask your hotel or a cueva) for roughly 30% more spending power than official rates. Most casual spots are cash-only; nicer restaurants take cards but prefer cash. Tip 10% at sit-down restaurants. Reservations matter for popular parrillas on weekends — book a day ahead. And accept that mate if someone offers it to you; refusing is almost rude.

## Day 1: Palermo — Where Buenos Aires Eats Now

Palermo is where the city's food scene has exploded over the past decade. Leafy streets, sidewalk cafes, and the highest concentration of interesting restaurants in the city. Start here to get your bearings and your appetite calibrated.

### Morning: Medialunas and Café con Leche

Begin at a traditional confitería — the Argentine café-bakery hybrid that runs on butter and nostalgia. Order a trio of *medialunas de manteca* (the buttery, slightly sweet croissants that define Buenos Aires mornings) with a *café con leche*. These aren't fancy; they're perfect. The medialunas should be warm, glossy, and soft enough to pull apart. Sit at the counter if you can, watch the morning rush of suits and grandmothers ordering the same thing, and accept that this is breakfast now.

**Where**: Any old-school confitería in Palermo or Recoleta — look for the ones with Formica counters and men in bow ties.
**Cost**: $4–$6 for coffee and pastries.

### Afternoon: Empanadas and Market Wandering

For lunch, empanadas. Not at a tourist spot, but at a no-frills empanadería where locals grab a half-dozen standing up. Order *empanadas de carne* (ground beef with cumin, olives, and egg), *pollo* (chicken), and one *jamón y queso* (ham and cheese) because you need to compare. The crust should shatter, the filling should be juicy without being wet, and the whole thing should cost less than a dollar.

After, take the Subte to San Telmo and slip into **Mercado de San Telmo** — the indoor market that hides behind an unassuming facade on Defensa street. This isn't a tourist market; it's where porteños buy cheese, olives, fresh pasta, and coffee beans. Wander the aisles, sample aged provolone, grab a glass of wine at one of the tiny wine bars tucked between stalls, and note the places you'll return to later in the trip.

**Where**: Any neighborhood empanadería in Palermo, then Mercado de San Telmo.
**Cost**: Empanadas $5–$8 for a filling lunch; market grazing $8–$15.

### Evening: Your First Real Asado

Tonight, you learn what Argentine beef actually tastes like. Find a proper *parrilla* — not a steakhouse for tourists, but the kind of place with a guy tending a wood-fired grill visible from the street. Order *provoleta* to start (grilled provolone cheese with oregano, served bubbling and slightly charred), then *bife de chorizo* — the classic sirloin strip that's thicker than your fist and needs nothing but salt. Medium-rare here means pink throughout; ask for *jugoso* if you want it properly red.

The meal will take two hours minimum. Wine is Malbec, obviously. Don't rush. This is dinner as it's meant to be eaten.

**Where**: A parrilla in Palermo Hollywood or Palermo Soho — look for smoke and locals.
**Cost**: Budget $25–$35 with wine; splurge $60–$80 at a top-tier parrilla.

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$110

## Day 2: San Telmo — Old Buenos Aires, Deep Flavors

San Telmo is cobblestones, antique shops, tango bars, and the kind of places that have been serving the same dishes for fifty years. Today we eat history.

### Morning: Coffee and Facturas at the Market

Return to Mercado de San Telmo for a proper breakfast. Find **Coffee Town** or one of the specialty coffee stalls that's appeared alongside the traditional vendors. Pair a well-pulled espresso with *facturas* — the category of Argentine pastries that includes medialunas but extends to *vigilantes* (cream-filled), *cañoncitos* (dulce de leche tubes), and *bolas de fraile* (like a filled donut). One of each. You're researching.

**Where**: Mercado de San Telmo coffee stalls.
**Cost**: $5–$8.

### Afternoon: Choripán and Street Food Walking

If it's Sunday, the San Telmo fair stretches for blocks along Defensa street — antiques, crafts, tango dancers, and critically, street food vendors. Find a *choripán*: a grilled chorizo sausage split and served in crusty bread with chimichurri and salsa criolla. This is Argentina's hot dog, except better in every way. The sausage should snap when you bite it, the bread should soak up the fat without falling apart, and the chimichurri should be heavy on oregano and garlic.

Walk it off through the fair, then duck into a bodegón — the old-school neighborhood restaurants that serve enormous portions to working-class crowds. Order whatever the table next to you is having, or ask for *milanesa napolitana* — the breaded beef cutlet topped with ham, tomato sauce, and melted cheese that's basically schnitzel gone magnificently wrong.

**Where**: Street vendors along Defensa; any bodegón in San Telmo.
**Cost**: Choripán $3–$5; milanesa lunch $10–$15.

### Evening: Closed-Door Restaurant or Tango Dinner

Tonight, try a *puerta cerrada* — the closed-door restaurants where chefs cook multi-course meals in their homes for a small group of strangers. These need to be booked days in advance and usually cost a flat rate including wine. It's the most intimate way to eat in Buenos Aires, and the food often pushes beyond traditional parrilla into modern Argentine cooking.

Alternatively, find a tango dinner show in San Telmo — skip the touristy Palermo versions and look for a smaller venue where the dancing is real and the food is secondary but still respectable. Either way, you're eating late. This is Buenos Aires.

**Where**: Book a puerta cerrada through Casa SaltShaker or similar; or find a San Telmo tango venue.
**Cost**: Puerta cerrada $80–$120 including wine; tango dinner $50–$70.

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~$70 | Mid ~$95 | Splurge ~$150

## Day 3: Recoleta and Beyond — The Farewell Feast

Final day. You know how to eat here now. Time to refine and indulge.

### Morning: Café Tortoni or Classic Confitería

Go full tourist, but on purpose: **Café Tortoni**, the 1858 institution that's served everyone from Borges to tango legends. Yes, there might be a line. Yes, it's worth it once. Order a *submarino* — hot milk with a bar of chocolate to stir in — and more medialunas. Sit in the wood-paneled room and watch the waiters who've been doing this for decades.

If you want to skip the line, find any old confitería in Recoleta near the cemetery. The vibe is the same; the pastries are identical.

**Where**: Café Tortoni (Microcentro) or Recoleta confiterías.
**Cost**: $8–$12.

### Afternoon: Dulce de Leche Everything

It's time to confront dulce de leche directly. Head to a traditional *alfajor* shop — **Havanna** is the chain version, but smaller bakeries make them better. Alfajores are two soft cookies sandwiching dulce de leche, often coated in chocolate or meringue. Buy three different kinds, eat them on a park bench in the Recoleta area, and accept that you now understand why Argentines put this stuff on everything.

For a late lunch, find a café serving *tostadas* — Argentine toast points with various toppings including, yes, more dulce de leche, but also fresh cheese and tomato. Simple, cheap, satisfying before the final dinner.

**Where**: Bakeries near Recoleta Cemetery; any Palermo café.
**Cost**: Alfajores $5–$8; light lunch $8–$12.

### Evening: The Meal Worth Flying For

Final dinner. Go to the best parrilla you can afford. Order *morcilla* (blood sausage) and *sweetbreads* to start, because you've earned the adventurous cuts. Then *entraña* (skirt steak) or *ojo de bife* (ribeye) — whichever the waiter recommends. A proper bottle of Malbec from Mendoza. *Ensalada mixta* because your body needs something green after three days of meat.

End with *flan con dulce de leche* — the wobbly caramel custard that brings everything full circle. Take your time. You're not eating dinner; you're saying goodbye.

**Where**: Don Julio, La Cabrera, or Parilla Peña in Palermo — book ahead.
**Cost**: Budget $35–$45; splurge $100–$150 with excellent wine.

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~$60 | Mid ~$80 | Splurge ~$180

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Lunch is the value meal**: Many parrillas and restaurants offer *menú ejecutivo* (business lunch) for half the dinner price. Eat your biggest meat at 1 PM, graze at night.
- **Bring small bills**: USD $100 bills get a slightly worse exchange rate than $20s at some cuevas. Break your cash before arrival if possible.
- **Skip the appetizers at parrillas**: Provoleta is essential, but the bread baskets and extra sides add up. The meat is the point.
- **Mercado de San Telmo closes early**: Don't arrive after 5 PM expecting a full experience. Go mid-morning when the stalls are busiest and the produce is freshest.
