Three days in Kotor taught me that Montenegrin food isn't trying to impress anyone — it's too busy being deeply, stubbornly satisfying. The bay provides seafood that tastes like it was pulled from the water an hour ago, the mountains send down lamb and cheese with centuries of tradition behind them, and the local wine flows cheap enough that you'll wonder why you ever paid €15 for a glass back home. This is an itinerary built on scraped plates and genuine discoveries.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Lunch runs 12:30–3pm, dinner rarely before 7:30pm — don't fight it. Cash is king in Old Town's smaller spots, though cards work in waterfront restaurants. Tipping 10% is generous and appreciated. Reservations matter only for weekend dinners at popular spots. Learn "hvala" (thank you) and "molim" (please) — it changes how people treat you. The euro is your currency here, which keeps things simple.

## Day 1: Old Town — Stone Walls and Sea Salt

**Morning**

Start where Kotor started: inside the medieval walls. Walk through the main gate before the cruise crowds arrive (get there by 8:30am) and find a table at one of the cafes tucked into the squares behind the Cathedral of Saint Tryphon. Order burek — the flaky phyllo pastry stuffed with cheese or meat that Montenegro inherited from Ottoman times. The cheese version (sa sirom) comes hot and grease-stained through its paper wrapping. Pair it with a thick, unfiltered Montenegrin coffee that'll recalibrate your morning. The Old Town cafe owners have been serving this same breakfast for decades, and the routine is perfect.

*Cost: €4–6*

**Afternoon**

By noon, work your way toward the waterfront for lunch. The restaurants lining the promenade look touristy, and some are, but the ones at the far end near the Maritime Museum serve genuinely good Adriatic seafood. Order grilled squid — not calamari rings, but whole squid scored and charred over wood fire, served with nothing but olive oil, garlic, and blitva (chard with potatoes). It's simple because it doesn't need to be complicated. The squid should be tender enough to cut with a fork. If it bounces, send it back.

After lunch, walk the perimeter of Old Town through the quieter residential squares. You'll pass small windows selling local cheese and smoked ham — grab a few pieces of njeguški pršut, Montenegro's answer to prosciutto, dry-cured in mountain air. This is your afternoon snack, eaten standing up, watching cats navigate the medieval alleyways.

*Cost: €18–28 for lunch, €5–8 for snacking*

**Evening**

Dinner stays inside the walls but moves upscale. Find a table in the Arts Quarter — the area around the old town's smaller churches where restaurants have more room to breathe. This is the night for a seafood platter: mussels from the bay, grilled fish of the day (ask what's fresh — brancin/sea bass or orada/sea bream are reliable), and maybe some scampi if they have it. Order a bottle of Vranac, Montenegro's signature red wine — it's earthier than you'd expect with seafood, but locals drink it with everything and they're not wrong. Finish with priganice, little fried dough balls drizzled with honey and sometimes stuffed with cheese.

*Cost: €35–55*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€60 | Mid ~€75 | Splurge ~€120

## Day 2: Waterfront and Beyond — The Bay's Bounty

**Morning**

Leave Old Town today. Take the waterfront promenade north toward the less-trafficked parts of the bay and find a local konoba (tavern) for a proper Montenegrin breakfast. You want eggs scrambled with kajmak — a cream cheese so rich it borders on butter, made in the mountain villages and spooned generously over everything. Some places serve this with sausage and fresh bread; accept all of it. The Waterfront outside the tourist center has working boats and working appetites, and breakfast here costs half what you'd pay inside the walls.

*Cost: €6–10*

**Afternoon**

Return toward the City Center for lunch at a restaurant that takes its grill seriously. Today is meat day — specifically, ćevapi or pljeskavica (grilled minced meat, either in small fingers or as a patty), served in somun bread with raw onions and ajvar, the roasted red pepper spread that makes everything better. This is Balkan fast food elevated: the meat should be charred outside, juicy inside, and the bread soft enough to soak up the juices. You'll find the best versions at places with smoke-blackened kitchens and no English menus.

After lunch, hunt down the hidden cafe that every local knows but doesn't advertise. Ask around for the spot that serves homemade rakija — fruit brandy distilled in-house or sourced from family connections in the villages. The grape version is standard, but honey rakija (medovača) will change your understanding of digestifs. One shot, maybe two, then walk it off.

*Cost: €10–15 for lunch, €5–8 for drinks*

**Evening**

Tonight earns a splurge. Book ahead for a restaurant in the Business District or slightly outside Old Town where the chefs are doing something more ambitious. The play here is lamb — specifically, jagnjetina ispod sača, lamb slow-cooked under a metal dome covered in embers. It takes hours to prepare, so call ahead or order when you sit down and let the appetizers carry you. The meat should fall apart without resistance, perfumed with rosemary and potatoes that have absorbed an unreasonable amount of lamb fat. This is the dish that Montenegrins serve at celebrations, and eating it feels ceremonial.

*Cost: €45–70*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€65 | Mid ~€90 | Splurge ~€140

## Day 3: The Best of What Remains

**Morning**

Final morning, stay loyal to Old Town one last time. Return to the square near the Main Square and order palačinke — thin crepes filled with walnuts and drizzled with chocolate, or stuffed with jam if you're feeling traditional. Montenegrins eat these any time of day, but they hit different with morning light filtering through stone archways. Linger over another Montenegrin coffee. You've earned slow.

*Cost: €5–8*

**Afternoon**

Dedicate lunch to filling the gaps. That fish soup you've seen on every menu but haven't tried? Order it — brodetto or riblja čorba, depending on the house style. The good versions are thick with tomato, garlic, and whatever the boats brought in, served with toasted bread for soaking. Hit the Central Market if it's a market day (mornings are best, but some vendors stay until early afternoon). Buy olives cured by someone's grandmother, more of that mountain cheese, a jar of fig jam to take home.

Wander through a neighborhood you haven't explored yet — maybe the residential blocks where locals actually live, away from the postcard streets. Find a bakery selling krofne (Montenegrin doughnuts, often filled with chocolate or apricot) and eat one while walking.

*Cost: €15–22 for lunch, €8–12 for market snacks*

**Evening**

The last meal should be definitive. Return to the waterfront for a sunset dinner where you can watch the bay turn gold, then order the thing Kotor does better than anywhere: perfectly fresh fish, simply prepared. Get the whole fish grilled (orada or brancin, head and tail on — this is how you know it's fresh), with olive oil and lemon, a side of seasonal vegetables, and bread to mop the plate. Order another bottle of Vranac or try Krstač, the local white. Don't rush. The bay isn't going anywhere, and neither are you for the next two hours.

Finish with something sweet — maybe rožata, the Montenegrin cousin of crème caramel, or just more rakija. The digestif version.

*Cost: €40–65*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€70 | Mid ~€95 | Splurge ~€150

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The cruise ship schedule is your enemy.** Check when ships dock (usually 9am–5pm) and eat inside Old Town before or after. The quality doesn't change, but the chaos does.
- **"Fish of the day" means ask which fish.** Servers respect you more when you care what you're eating, and the answer tells you whether they're serving fresh catch or frozen inventory.
- **Njeguški pršut and cheese make the best cheap meal.** Buy both at the market, grab bread from a bakery, and you have a €10 picnic that rivals any restaurant plate.
- **Rakija isn't optional.** Refusing a offered shot is rude; accepting it opens doors. Sip slowly — this isn't vodka culture, and no one's counting.
