Three days in Thessaloniki taught me that this city doesn't just feed you — it overwhelms you with generosity, then hands you another plate. Greece's second city has been a crossroads of Ottoman, Sephardic, and Byzantine food traditions for centuries, and that layered history shows up in every bite. Here's the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I loosened my belt and got to work.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Greeks eat late — lunch rarely before 2pm, dinner often at 10pm. Most places accept cards, but carry cash for market stalls and small ouzeries. Tipping is simple: round up or leave 5-10% for good service. Reservations matter for weekend dinners but weekday lunches are walk-in friendly. Learn to say "efcharistó" (thank you) — it opens doors. And yes, they'll bring you bread you didn't order. You're paying for it. Eat it.

## Day 1: The City Center Fundamentals

**Morning: Koulouri and Coffee at the Waterfront**

Start where Thessalonians start: with a koulouri in hand. These sesame-crusted bread rings cost about €0.80 from any street cart, and the best ones are still warm, the sesame toasted but not burnt. Grab one from a vendor near the White Tower and walk the waterfront promenade. Pair it with a freddo espresso (iced espresso, shaken until frothy) from any of the cafés lining the water — this is the city's unofficial morning drink. Find a bench, watch the fishing boats, let the day start slow.

**Estimated cost:** €4–6 for koulouri and coffee

**Afternoon: Modiano Market and a Proper Lunch**

By early afternoon, make your way to Modiano Market, the covered market that reopened after renovations and remains the city's gastronomic heart. This isn't a tourist market — it's where locals buy fish, olives, spices, and the sheep's milk cheese that shows up on every table. Walk the aisles first. Let vendors hand you samples of graviera cheese and cured sardines.

For lunch, find a stall or small taverna inside or just outside the market serving bougatsa — but the savory version. Most tourists know the sweet custard-filled bougatsa, but the cheese-filled variety, with its shatteringly crisp phyllo and molten filling, is what locals eat for lunch. One slice runs about €3. Add a plate of grilled octopus from a seafood counter (around €12–15) and you've assembled the kind of lunch that makes you cancel dinner plans.

Spend an hour wandering after, buying olives and halva to bring home.

**Estimated cost:** €18–25 for lunch and market snacking

**Evening: Waterfront Dinner Done Right**

For your first proper dinner, head to the waterfront area where the locals go — past the tourist-heavy strip and into the neighborhoods where the menus aren't in English. Order what Thessaloniki does better than anywhere: grilled fish. The waiter will show you what came in that morning. Point at something, ask them to grill it simply with olive oil and lemon. Add a horiatiki (village salad) — tomatoes, cucumber, onion, feta, olives, no lettuce — and a plate of tzatziki for the bread.

You'll pay by the kilo for fish, so expect €20–35 for a portion, but this is the meal. A half-liter of house wine runs €6–8. Finish with a complimentary plate of fruit or a tiny pour of raki — they almost always bring something.

**Estimated cost:** €35–55 for dinner with wine

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€55 | Mid-range ~€75 | Splurge ~€120

## Day 2: The Old Town and Hidden Corners

**Morning: Bougatsa in Ano Poli**

Today, start uphill. The Old Town (Ano Poli) sits above the city center, a maze of Ottoman-era houses, Byzantine walls, and quiet squares that feel like a different century. Before the climb, get the sweet bougatsa you skipped yesterday. The best versions use a semolina custard dusted with powdered sugar and cinnamon. Order it "me krema" (with cream) and accept that your shirt will get messy. Eaten warm, standing at a counter, it's about €4 and one of the best €4 you'll spend.

Walk it off by climbing the winding streets toward the Byzantine walls. The views of the city and Thermaic Gulf are your reward.

**Estimated cost:** €4–6 for bougatsa and Greek coffee

**Afternoon: Meze Lunch in a Neighborhood Taverna**

Come back down toward the Arts Quarter area — specifically, the quieter streets where students and artists eat. Look for a small meze taverna, the kind with six tables and a handwritten menu that changes daily. This is where you'll find the city's soul food: gigantes (giant beans in tomato sauce), melitzanosalata (smoky eggplant dip), keftedes (fried meatballs), and whatever greens they're serving that day — often wild chicory braised in olive oil.

Order four or five small plates to share, a basket of bread, and a small carafe of tsipouro (the local spirit, served with ice and often with a small meze included). This is not a rushed meal. Expect it to last two hours. You'll leave having spent around €25–30 per person, feeling like you've eaten at a grandmother's table.

After, walk through the Arts Quarter, ducking into any shop that catches your eye. There's a small café hidden down one of the narrower streets — the kind with no sign, just a door and a few chairs outside — that serves Greek coffee brewed over sand. Ask around. Finding it is part of the experience.

**Estimated cost:** €25–35 for lunch, €3–5 for afternoon coffee

**Evening: A Serious Seafood Dinner**

Tonight, go somewhere that takes seafood seriously. Head to a neighborhood restaurant where the fish case is the first thing you see when you walk in. This meal is about technique: fish baked in salt crust, shrimp saganaki (prawns in tomato sauce with feta), sea bass carpaccio if they have it.

This is your mid-range splurge meal — expect €45–60 per person with wine, but the quality justifies it. Order the house's specialty; in Thessaloniki, trust the kitchen. Finish with a small plate of loukoumades if they have them — fried dough balls drizzled with honey and cinnamon, served warm.

**Estimated cost:** €45–65 for dinner

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€75 | Mid-range ~€100 | Splurge ~€150

## Day 3: The Best of Everything

**Morning: The Greek Breakfast You've Earned**

Your final morning demands a proper sit-down breakfast — not the grab-and-go of Day 1. Find a café in the City Center that serves a full Greek breakfast: eggs (scrambled or fried with tomatoes), thick-cut bacon, feta, olives, fresh bread, honey, and Greek yogurt so thick you could stand a spoon in it. Order it with a pot of mountain tea (tsai tou vounou), the herbal tea that Greeks swear cures everything.

This is a slow morning. Linger. Watch the city wake up. This meal runs €12–18 depending on where you sit, and it's worth paying for a good seat.

**Estimated cost:** €12–18 for breakfast

**Afternoon: A Final Market Walk**

Return to the market area — not to repeat yourself, but to buy what you've learned to want. Olives from the vendor whose samples you liked best. A block of halva, sesame-studded and crumbly. A jar of thyme honey. Dried oregano from a spice stall that sells it by the bagful for almost nothing.

For lunch, eat light: a single gyro, eaten standing. The Thessaloniki-style gyro is distinct — pita, pork or chicken off the spit, tomatoes, onions, and mustard (not tzatziki, though you can ask). It costs about €3.50 and it's messily perfect. Follow it with a trigono panoramatos from any bakery — a triangle of phyllo filled with sweet cream, soaked in syrup. About €2.50.

Spend the afternoon in a café near the waterfront, drinking freddo cappuccino and watching boats.

**Estimated cost:** €10–15 for gyro, sweets, and coffee

**Evening: The Farewell Feast**

Your last dinner should be your longest. Head back to the Waterfront for a restaurant that does the full Thessaloniki experience: meze to start (taramosalata, fava, grilled peppers with feta), then grilled lamb chops or paidakia — charred on the outside, pink within, seasoned with oregano and lemon. Add a side of patates tiganites (fried potatoes, the Greek version of fries, crisped in olive oil) and whatever seasonal vegetables they're serving.

Drink local wine, something from the Naoussa region if they have it. When you think you're done, order a baklava — the real thing, layered with nuts and soaked in honey syrup until it goes soft in the middle. Finish with a small glass of Metaxa or tsipouro.

This is a €60–90 meal, and it's supposed to be. You didn't come here to hold back.

**Estimated cost:** €60–90 for dinner

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€80 | Mid-range ~€110 | Splurge ~€180

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Lunch is your value meal.** Many restaurants that charge €50+ for dinner offer the same quality at lunch for half the price. Greeks eat their big meal midday anyway.

- **Don't order tap water.** It's safe to drink, but restaurants expect you to buy bottled. Ask for "nero" and they'll bring a liter for €1–2.

- **Weekend dinner reservations are essential.** Greeks go out in groups on Friday and Saturday nights. A table at 10pm requires booking by Thursday. Weeknights are more forgiving.

- **The bread basket isn't free.** That bread and olive oil they set down when you arrive? It's on your bill, usually €1–2. It's also usually excellent, so eat it without guilt.
