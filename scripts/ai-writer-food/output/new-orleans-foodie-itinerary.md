Three days in New Orleans isn't enough time. It never is. But with strategic planning and a willingness to eat past the point of comfort, you can cover the essential ground — from the dark-roux gumbos of the French Quarter to the po'boys that have been made the same way for generations in quieter neighborhoods. This is the itinerary I wish I'd had before my first bite.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

New Orleans runs late. Breakfast spots open around 8am, but the city doesn't fully wake until 10. Dinner reservations at popular spots book 2–3 weeks out — don't wing it for anywhere you actually care about. Cash helps at older establishments and food stands. Tip 20% at sit-down restaurants; that's standard here, not generous. Credit cards work almost everywhere now, but some neighborhood joints still prefer cash.

## Day 1: French Quarter Foundations

**Morning**

Start in the French Quarter with beignets at Café Du Monde. Yes, it's the obvious choice. Yes, there's usually a line. And yes, you should still go — ideally before 8:30am when the crowds haven't fully materialized. Order the chicory coffee dark, the beignets hot. The powdered sugar will get everywhere; this is unavoidable and part of the experience. You'll pay around $8–10 for coffee and a plate of three, and you'll leave dusted in white like you walked through a flour mill. Find a bench at Jackson Square afterward to watch the city wake up.

**Afternoon**

Walk to the French Market, about ten minutes from the café. This is where you start understanding the range — produce vendors, hot sauce tastings, praline samples, prepared food stalls serving everything from jambalaya to alligator sausage. Don't fill up; graze strategically. A cup of crawfish étouffée from one of the stalls runs about $9–12 and gives you a baseline for the dish you'll encounter all week.

For proper lunch, head to a po'boy shop nearby. The roast beef version — gravy-soaked, dressed with lettuce, tomato, and mayo on French bread that crackles when you bite — is the move. This sandwich should cost $13–16 and should be too big to finish in one sitting. Get it "dressed" unless you have strong opinions otherwise. Budget ~$25–35 for lunch including the market grazing.

**Evening**

Dinner in the French Quarter means making a choice: historic Creole institution or something more contemporary. For the foundational experience, a restaurant serving classic gumbo and shrimp Creole will run $45–75 per person with drinks. The gumbo should be dark, the roux cooked to the edge of burnt, the trinity (onion, celery, bell pepper) soft but present. Order it with andouille sausage if you're choosing one protein. Red beans and rice appears on many menus — traditionally a Monday dish, but available daily at most spots now.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$70 | Mid ~$110 | Splurge ~$160

## Day 2: Garden District & Uptown Exploration

**Morning**

Take the streetcar up St. Charles Avenue — this isn't just transit, it's context. Watch the architecture shift from French Quarter density to the sprawling oaks and columned houses of the Garden District. Get off around Washington Avenue and find breakfast at a neighborhood spot. Eggs and grits with debris (the bits of roast beef that fall into the gravy) is the local play. French toast made with local French bread soaked properly is another strong option. Expect $15–22 with coffee.

**Afternoon**

The Garden District rewards walking, and you should do it hungry. The area around Magazine Street has bakeries and sandwich shops worth your attention. This is the time for a muffuletta — the olive salad-topped Italian sandwich that rivals the po'boy for local loyalty. A half is plenty; a whole will defeat you. Budget $14–18 for the sandwich, more if you add a slice of king cake (available seasonally, but bakeries often have versions year-round).

If you're interested in understanding how the food works rather than just eating it, this afternoon is your window for a cooking class. Several spots in Uptown teach small-group sessions on gumbo, jambalaya, and praline-making. Classes run $120–180 and typically include the meal you cook. Book these well in advance.

**Evening**

Dinner Uptown should feel different from the Quarter — slightly quieter, more neighborhood energy. This is where you find updated takes on Louisiana classics at restaurants tucked into converted houses. Look for Gulf fish prepared simply with butter and herbs, oysters chargrilled with garlic and Parmesan, and brisket that's been slow-smoked local style. A good dinner here with wine runs $60–90 per person. Skip the trendy spots without deep local roots; look for places where half the tables are regulars.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$90 | Mid ~$140 | Splurge ~$220

## Day 3: Tremé, Markets & The Farewell Feast

**Morning**

Walk to Tremé, the oldest African American neighborhood in the country and the birthplace of jazz. Breakfast here is straightforward and soul-deep: hot sausage and eggs at a counter, maybe a sweet potato biscuit if you can find one. This is also where you encounter the real beignet alternative — calas, the rice fritters that were sold by free women of color in the 1700s. Few places still make them; when you find them, order more than you think you need. Breakfast runs $12–18 depending on where you land.

**Afternoon**

Return to the Central Market area for a final food walk. This is your chance to fill gaps — if you haven't had proper boudin yet, find a stall selling it. If you missed crawfish (seasonal, but often available frozen and prepared), this is the time. Snack through the market with intention: a stuffed artichoke here, a few boiled shrimp there, maybe a cup of turtle soup for around $8. This isn't lunch — it's a collection of punctuation marks. Total grazing: $20–35.

Then find a spot that's been doing one thing well for decades. These are the places that don't make "best of" lists anymore because everyone assumes you already know about them. A bowl of yakamein (the beef noodle soup sometimes called "Old Sober" for its hangover-curing properties) or a plate of red beans from someone's grandmother's recipe — these meals cost $10–15 and matter more than they should.

**Evening**

Your final dinner should be the one you remember. Return to the French Quarter or Warehouse District for this — somewhere with history in the walls and a kitchen that treats every ingredient like it's the last of its kind. This is when you order the tasting menu or simply let the server tell you what's good tonight. Start with oysters — raw, chargrilled, or Rockefeller. Move to a gumbo z'herbes if available (the green gumbo, traditionally eaten during Lent). For the main, whatever swims in the Gulf that morning. Finish with bread pudding soaked in whiskey sauce or a simple praline with coffee.

This meal will cost $80–150 per person with drinks. It's supposed to.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$110 | Mid ~$170 | Splurge ~$280

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Make reservations for dinner, wing it for lunch.** The reservation culture here is real but limited to evening — lunch spots rarely require booking, and walking in works fine for breakfast.

- **Ask servers what they actually eat.** Not what's popular, not what's "traditional" — what they order on their day off. The answers are usually better than the menu descriptions.

- **The best food often comes from the ugliest buildings.** Avoid places that look renovated specifically to attract tourists. The fluorescent-lit corner joint with the hand-painted sign has survived because the food demands it.

- **Hydrate strategically.** Between the heat, the salt, and the alcohol, New Orleans will dehydrate you faster than you expect. Drink water between meals, not instead of coffee.
