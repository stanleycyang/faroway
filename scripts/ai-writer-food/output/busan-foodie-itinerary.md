Three days in Busan with one mission: eat everything worth eating. This is a city where grandmothers have been perfecting the same pork soup for forty years, where fish markets operate on a scale that borders on overwhelming, and where a bowl of noodles at 2 AM feels less like a late-night snack and more like a civic duty. I ate seventeen meals in seventy-two hours. Here's exactly where you should put yours.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Most Koreans eat dinner between 6–8 PM, but Busan's markets keep their own hours — some stalls open at dawn, others don't hit their stride until midnight. Tipping isn't expected anywhere; don't do it. Card acceptance is nearly universal, but smaller market vendors prefer cash (₩10,000 and ₩50,000 notes work fine). No reservations needed except for higher-end seafood restaurants on weekends. Learn to say "mashisoyo" (delicious) — vendors light up when you use it.

## Day 1: Jagalchi and the Sea

Busan is a port city first, everything else second. Start where the fishing boats come in.

### Morning

**Jagalchi Market, 7:30 AM** — Skip the hotel breakfast. You want *jeonbok-juk* (abalone porridge) while the market is still waking up. The ajummas on the second floor of Jagalchi Market's main building ladle out bowls of this pale green, impossibly silky rice porridge studded with chunks of fresh abalone. It's gentle, restorative, and exactly what you need before a day of aggressive eating. Find a stall where the woman looks like she's been doing this since before you were born. She has. The market sprawls along the Waterfront in Nampo-dong, and at this hour, you'll share counter space with fishermen finishing night shifts. 

*Estimated cost: ₩12,000–15,000 ($9–11)*

### Afternoon

**Jagalchi Market (continued), 11:30 AM** — Now the market is fully alive. Work your way through the ground floor, where tanks of everything that swims, crawls, or clings to rocks line both sides. This is lunch, but it's also theater. Pick your fish — *gwangeo* (halibut) or *ureok* (rockfish) are excellent choices — and they'll slice it for *hoe* (Korean sashimi) while you watch. You'll get the fish three ways: raw slices with ssamjang and garlic, a plate of lightly blanched organ bits, and finally *maeuntang*, a spicy soup made from the bones and head. This is a full experience that takes ninety minutes. Sit at the upstairs restaurants where your chosen vendor sends your fish.

Walk it off along the Waterfront toward BIFF Square in Nampo-dong. The *ssiat hotteok* (seed-filled sweet pancakes) here are Busan's definitive street snack — crispy, molten with brown sugar and sunflower seeds, and absolutely mandatory. Grab one from any vendor with a long line.

*Estimated cost: ₩35,000–50,000 for hoe lunch ($26–37), ₩3,000 for hotteok ($2)*

### Evening

**Nampodong Dwaeji-gukbap, 7:00 PM** — Still in Nampo-dong, end the day with Busan's soul food: *dwaeji-gukbap*, pork soup with rice. This milky, long-simmered broth with tender pork slices exists all over Korea, but Busan's version is the original. The soup comes plain; you season it yourself with salted shrimp paste, green onions, and chili flakes from the banchan spread. Add rice directly to the soup or eat it on the side — there's no wrong answer. The restaurants around Nampo-dong's main streets have been serving this for decades; look for places with handwritten menus and no English. 

*Estimated cost: ₩9,000–12,000 ($7–9)*

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~₩80,000 ($60) · Mid ~₩110,000 ($82) · Splurge ~₩180,000 ($135)

## Day 2: Seomyeon and the Meat

Leave the sea behind. Today is about the interior neighborhoods and the things that sizzle.

### Morning

**Seomyeon's Breakfast Alley, 8:30 AM** — Seomyeon is Busan's commercial heart, a dense grid of restaurants, bars, and coffee shops that operates on its own schedule. Breakfast here means *gukbap* again, but this time *soondae-gukbap* — blood sausage soup. The Seomyeon Sundae Gukbap Alley (yes, it has a name) holds a dozen or so restaurants all serving variations. The soup is earthier than yesterday's pork version, with sliced blood sausage, intestines, and pork in a cloudy broth. Add plenty of *saeujeot* (salted shrimp) and chopped chilies. Koreans consider this hangover food, but it's really just deeply nourishing.

*Estimated cost: ₩9,000–11,000 ($7–8)*

### Afternoon

**Samnak Gopchang Alley, 12:30 PM** — Take the subway to Samnak, a neighborhood tourists mostly skip. This is where Busan eats *gopchang* (grilled intestines). The alley specializes in beef and pork intestines grilled over charcoal, served with soju and ssam (lettuce wraps). The texture is chewy, the flavor deeply beefy, and the whole experience feels like being let in on a secret. Restaurants here are no-frills — metal tables, soju by the bottle, smoke everywhere. Embrace it.

After lunch, walk to the nearby Sasang Traditional Market. This isn't in the guidebooks. It's a working market where locals buy produce, dried goods, and *tteok* (rice cakes). Graze through vendors selling *hotteok*, *eomuk* (fish cake skewers), and seasonal *jeon* (savory pancakes). Buy a bag of fresh *tteok* for later.

*Estimated cost: ₩25,000–35,000 for gopchang ($19–26), ₩8,000 for market snacks ($6)*

### Evening

**Haeundae for KBBQ, 7:30 PM** — Time to go upscale. Head to Haeundae, Busan's glossy beach district, for *hanwoo* Korean beef. The beef restaurants along Haeundae's main streets serve prime-graded Korean beef that's marbled to absurdity. Order *kkotsal* (boneless short rib) and *chadolbaegi* (beef brisket). The servers will grill it for you; let them. Pair with soju and *doenjang-jjigae* (fermented soybean stew) on the side.

If you want to extend the night, Haeundae's beach bars and rooftop spots are within walking distance. Or do what locals do: end with *naengmyeon* (cold buckwheat noodles) — the traditional chaser after heavy meat.

*Estimated cost: ₩50,000–80,000 ($37–60)*

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~₩90,000 ($67) · Mid ~₩130,000 ($97) · Splurge ~₩200,000 ($150)

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

Last day. Time for the dishes you've been circling back to, and the ones you almost missed.

### Morning

**Nampo-dong for Milmyeon, 9:00 AM** — *Milmyeon* is Busan's cold wheat noodle dish — chewy, served in an icy broth or with spicy sauce, and utterly refreshing. It was invented here by North Korean refugees after the war and remains a Busan specialty. The restaurants in Nampo-dong near the Waterfront have the longest pedigrees. Order *mul-milmyeon* (in broth) and add mustard and vinegar to taste. The noodles have a bounce you won't find elsewhere.

*Estimated cost: ₩8,000–10,000 ($6–7)*

### Afternoon

**Gukje Market, 12:00 PM** — Return to where markets began. Gukje ("International") Market is the sprawling labyrinth connecting to Jagalchi, and it's where you eat the things you missed. *Bindaetteok* (mung bean pancakes), eaten standing at a pojangmacha stall. *Kalguksu* (knife-cut noodle soup), hot and simple. *Sundae* (Korean blood sausage) sliced fresh and dipped in salt. Wander for two hours. Buy everything that looks good. Sit on plastic stools next to office workers on lunch break.

This is also where you'll find Busan's famous *eomuk* (fish cake) shops — Samjin Eomuk has the widest selection. Buy some for the train ride out.

*Estimated cost: ₩20,000–30,000 for grazing ($15–22)*

### Evening

**Haeundae for the Final Seafood, 7:00 PM** — End where Busan shines brightest. Splurge on a proper seafood dinner at one of Haeundae's established restaurants. This is where you order the *haemul-tang* (spicy seafood stew) loaded with crab, shrimp, and octopus, or the full *modeum-hoe* (assorted sashimi platter) with everything the kitchen has. The restaurants near Haeundae Market will negotiate prices; the ones with ocean views charge a premium that's mostly worth it. Order soju. Order too much food. You're leaving tomorrow.

*Estimated cost: ₩60,000–120,000 ($45–90)*

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~₩95,000 ($71) · Mid ~₩140,000 ($105) · Splurge ~₩220,000 ($165)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Market timing matters**: Jagalchi is best before 10 AM for calm exploration or after 5 PM for dinner service. Midday is tourist crush.
- **Learn the banchan rotation**: When banchan (side dishes) run low, you can ask for refills. Say "banchan deo juseyo" and point. It's free and expected.
- **Soju protocol**: If someone older pours you soju, turn your head slightly away while drinking. It's a respect thing, and Koreans notice when you do it.
- **Pack tissues**: Many smaller restaurants don't provide napkins at the table. Convenience stores sell pocket tissue packs everywhere.
