Three days in Chengdu taught me that eating here isn't a casual pursuit — it's a full-contact sport requiring stamina, heat tolerance, and a willingness to rearrange your entire understanding of spice. This itinerary is what I wish someone had handed me before I landed: the specific spots, the right dishes, the neighborhoods that reward wandering, and the meals worth building your day around.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Chengdu runs on cash more than you'd expect — smaller noodle shops and market stalls often don't take cards, so keep ¥200-300 on you daily. Tipping isn't customary and can cause confusion. Lunch peaks between 11:30am-1pm; dinner crowds arrive by 6pm. For hotpot restaurants and popular spots, booking ahead via WeChat or Dianping saves you from long waits, especially on weekends. Learn to say "wēi là" (mild spice) — you'll need it.

## Day 1: The Old Town Initiation

**Morning — Breakfast in the Old Town**

Start where Chengdu starts its mornings: with a bowl of dandan noodles and the chaos of an early market. Head to the Old Town neighborhood before 9am, when the air is still cool and the noodle shops are full of locals eating fast. You want a bowl of 担担面 (dandan mian) — wheat noodles tossed in chili oil, ground pork, preserved vegetables, and Sichuan peppercorn that leaves your lips tingling for the next hour. The portions are small because they're meant to be eaten alongside other things: a couple of 龙抄手 (dragon wontons) in red oil, maybe a bowl of sweet soy milk to cut the heat.

The best bowls come from shops that have been doing one thing for decades. Look for the places with laminated menus, plastic stools, and a crowd of office workers eating in silence. The whole meal should run you about $4-6, and you'll leave wondering why breakfast anywhere else bothers existing.

*Cost: $4-8*

**Afternoon — Lunch and Market Walking**

By noon, make your way deeper into the Old Town toward the central market district for lunch and grazing. This is where Chengdu's street food culture reveals itself — not in curated food halls, but in the narrow alleys where vendors have been frying the same thing since before you were born.

Lunch should be 串串香 (chuanchuan xiang) — skewered meats and vegetables that you select from refrigerated cases and have cooked in a communal spicy broth. You'll find clusters of these shops near the market areas, identifiable by the forest of bamboo skewers in customers' hands. Pick lotus root, duck intestine, beef tripe, potato slices, and whatever mushrooms look good. Skewers run ¥0.5-3 each; a proper lunch means 20-30 of them, plus a bowl of rice. The broth is molten and numbing — pace yourself with the peppers.

After lunch, walk the market stalls. This is where you buy 花椒 (Sichuan peppercorns) to take home, watch vendors make fresh 豆花 (tofu pudding), and sample things you can't identify. Grab a cup of sweet rice wine from a street vendor. Eat a few 锅盔 (stuffed flatbreads) hot from the griddle. This is research.

*Cost: $12-18*

**Evening — Dinner in the Old Town**

For dinner, stay in the Old Town but elevate. Find a proper Sichuan restaurant — the kind with private rooms and servers who bring dishes in waves. This is where you eat 麻婆豆腐 (mapo tofu) made correctly: silken tofu in a sauce so red it looks dangerous, topped with ground beef, fermented black beans, and enough peppercorn to make your entire mouth go electric. Order 回锅肉 (twice-cooked pork belly), 水煮鱼 (fish in fiery oil), and a cold dish like 夫妻肺片 (husband and wife lung slices — actually beef and offal in chili oil).

Eating at this level means paying $25-50 per person depending on how many dishes you order. Get too many. Share with anyone you've met. Drink cold beer to survive.

*Cost: $25-50*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend: Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$120**

## Day 2: Arts Quarter and the Hidden Side

**Morning — Breakfast in the Arts Quarter**

Cross town to the Arts Quarter, where the vibe shifts from market chaos to tree-lined streets and converted warehouses. Breakfast here is more leisurely — find a spot serving 抄手 (Sichuan wontons) in clear broth with a side of chili oil for dipping. This is the gentler side of Sichuan breakfast, the kind you eat while reading or watching the street wake up.

Pair it with 蛋烘糕 (egg pancakes), a Chengdu specialty you'll find from street carts: sweet or savory fillings tucked into a crispy-edged pancake cooked in small round molds. The sweet red bean version is traditional; the savory ground pork version is better.

*Cost: $5-10*

**Afternoon — Cooking Class and Local Neighborhood Lunch**

Mid-morning, take a cooking class. Several spots in the Arts Quarter run 3-hour sessions where you'll visit a market, buy ingredients, and learn to make 3-4 dishes. This is where Sichuan cooking starts to make sense — you'll understand why the peppercorns are toasted before grinding, how to build layers of heat, and exactly how much chili oil is "enough" (more than you think). Classes run $40-70 per person and include lunch, since you eat what you make.

If you skip the class, head to the local neighborhood just north of the Arts Quarter — streets that don't appear in guides, where the lunch crowd is entirely people who live within walking distance. Find 肥肠粉 (intestine noodles), a bowl of glass noodles in a thick, spicy soup with sliced pig intestine. It sounds challenging; it tastes like the best thing you've eaten in days. About $3 a bowl.

*Cost: $40-70 (with class) or $10-15 (without)*

**Evening — Hotpot in the City Center**

Tonight is hotpot night. You cannot come to Chengdu and skip this. Head to the City Center where the serious hotpot restaurants cluster. You'll get a table with a divided pot — one side blazing red with chilies and tallow, the other side a mild mushroom broth for recovery. You order plates of raw ingredients: sliced beef and lamb, various organ meats, leafy greens, noodles, tofu, mushrooms. You cook them in the bubbling broth, dip them in sesame oil and garlic, and eat until you can't move.

The ritual takes 2-3 hours minimum. This is not a meal you rush. By the end, your clothes will smell like smoke, your lips will be swollen from spice, and you'll understand why Chengdu's social life revolves around this pot. Mid-range hotpot runs $20-35 per person; the famous spots with hour-long waits cost about the same but require advance booking.

*Cost: $25-45*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend: Budget ~$75 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$180**

## Day 3: The Business District and Final Feast

**Morning — Breakfast in the Business District**

For your last full day, head to the Business District for a different Chengdu: one of gleaming towers and professionals grabbing breakfast on the way to work. Find a 豆浆油条 (soy milk and fried dough) spot — the classic Chinese breakfast that transcends regions. Fresh soy milk, hot and barely sweetened, with fried crullers for dipping. Simple, perfect, and about $2 total.

Then walk to find 甜水面 (sweet water noodles), thick chewy noodles in a sauce of soy, sugar, chili oil, and sesame paste. It's the dish that confuses tourists — sweet and spicy together, served cold, eaten as a breakfast snack. You'll either love it immediately or need two tries. Either way, you'll remember it.

*Cost: $4-8*

**Afternoon — Market Farewell and Snacking**

Return to the markets for one last circuit. This time you're not exploring — you're executing. Buy the things you wished you'd tried earlier: 兔头 (rabbit heads), if you're brave, eaten cold with numbing spice. 凉粉 (cold mung bean jelly) doused in chili oil. 蒸蒸糕 (steamed rice cakes) from a vendor with a bamboo steamer. This is grazing as a goodbye, hitting the street food stalls with purpose.

Find a tea house near the market for a midday break. Chengdu's tea culture runs deep — order a covered cup of jasmine tea, sit in a bamboo chair, and let the morning's eating settle. This costs almost nothing ($2-4) and gives you the energy for dinner.

*Cost: $10-20*

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your last dinner should be at a restaurant that treats Sichuan cuisine as craft. Head to the Waterfront area, where several spots combine traditional techniques with better ingredients and calmer rooms. This is where you eat 口水鸡 (saliva chicken) — poached chicken in a complex sauce of chili oil, sesame, and Sichuan peppercorn that's sweet, spicy, numbing, and savory all at once. Order 樟茶鸭 (tea-smoked duck), crispy-skinned and fragrant from camphor and tea leaves. Get one more mapo tofu because you can. Finish with 甜烧白 (sweet pork with glutinous rice), a surprisingly delicate dessert.

This meal costs more — $40-75 per person — but you're leaving tomorrow. You've earned this.

*Cost: $40-75*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend: Budget ~$60 | Mid ~$90 | Splurge ~$150**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Order by pointing.** Most small restaurants have photos on the wall or other diners' tables to gesture toward. The language barrier dissolves when you can point at exactly what you want.
- **Pace your spice exposure.** Day one enthusiasm leads to day two regret. Carry tissues, drink hot tea instead of cold water (it helps, somehow), and don't be ashamed to ask for mild heat.
- **Eat early.** The best street food vendors sell out. The best restaurants fill up. If you're hungry at 10:30am, eat. If you want dinner at 5:30pm, go. Waiting until "normal" hours means competing with everyone else.
- **Download Dianping.** It's China's Yelp, and even without reading Chinese, you can sort by rating and look at photos. Saved me from several mediocre meals.
