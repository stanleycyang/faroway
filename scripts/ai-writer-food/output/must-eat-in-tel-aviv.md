The shakshuka arrives still bubbling, the cast iron pan so hot the edges of the eggs continue to cook at the table. It's 10 AM on a Friday at a café on Rothschild Boulevard, and the waiter slides a basket of bread toward me with the kind of urgency that says: *dip now, before the yolks set*. I tear off a chunk, drag it through the spiced tomato slick, and watch the orange yolk bleed into the red. This is how Tel Aviv wakes up.

I spent three weeks eating through this city — through its markets and corner hummusiyot, its Jaffa bakeries and Florentin hole-in-the-walls — and what I discovered is a cuisine that refuses to sit still. Every dish carries the DNA of somewhere else: Morocco, Yemen, Iraq, Poland, the Levant. But what emerges is something entirely Tel Avivian — bold, sunlit, a little aggressive in the best way, and utterly impossible to eat alone.

Here's what you need to eat before you leave.

---

#### Shakshuka

The brunch king wears a tomato crown. Shakshuka is deceptively simple — eggs poached in a sauce of tomatoes, peppers, onions, and enough cumin and paprika to stain your fingers — but the execution separates the ordinary from the transcendent. The best versions arrive in a blackened pan, the sauce reduced to something jammy and sweet-tart, the eggs still trembling. You don't use a fork. You use bread as a utensil, scooping sauce and egg together in messy, dripping bites.

The dish traveled to Israel with North African Jews, and it found its spiritual home in the café culture of Tel Aviv, where breakfast is a religion and nobody rushes. Order it at any café along Rothschild or in Neve Tzedek, usually for ₪45–55 (around $12–15), and it will come with a spread of salads, labneh, and more bread than you can finish. The vegetable version is classic, but look for variations with feta, merguez sausage, or green shakshuka made with spinach and herbs.

#### Hummus

There are hummusiyot in Tel Aviv that open at 6 AM and close when the hummus runs out, often by noon. This is not a condiment here — it's a main course, served warm in a wide bowl with a crater of olive oil in the center, and you eat it with fresh pita and little else. The texture should be impossibly smooth, almost fluffy, with a nuttiness that coats your mouth and a garlic presence that lingers.

The hummus wars in this city are real. Ask five locals for the best, and you'll get five different answers, all delivered with the conviction of someone defending family honor. But one name appears on nearly every list: Abu Hassan in Jaffa. The tiny shop on HaDolphin Street has been serving hummus since 1959, and the line often stretches out the door. They offer three things: hummus, hummus with ful (stewed fava beans), and msabbaha (a chunkier, warmer version with whole chickpeas). Order the msabbaha. Watch the tahini pool into the warm chickpeas. A plate runs about ₪30–40 ($8–11), and you'll be back the next day.

---

#### Sabich

If hummus is Tel Aviv's classic, sabich is its sleeper hit — the sandwich that locals argue is better than falafel, if only tourists would pay attention. It's an Iraqi-Jewish creation: warm pita stuffed with slices of fried eggplant (silky, almost creamy from the oil), a hard-boiled egg, hummus, Israeli salad, and the secret weapon — amba, a tangy, bright-yellow mango pickle sauce that pulls everything into focus.

The original sabich was Shabbat food, made with ingredients that could be prepared the day before. Now it's Tuesday lunch, Friday morning, whenever hunger strikes. Find it at any sabich stand, but the original Sabich Frishman near the beach still does it right. Around ₪35–45 ($9–12) gets you a sandwich so overstuffed it requires two hands and complete surrender to the drip. The amba will get on your shirt. You will not care.

#### Knafeh

In the Arab bakeries of Jaffa, there's a dessert that doesn't care about your restraint. Knafeh is built from shredded phyllo dough (or sometimes semolina) layered over a stretchy, mild white cheese, then baked until the top shatters and the cheese melts into strings. They drench it in orange blossom syrup while it's still hot, finish it with a snowfall of crushed pistachios, and slice it in front of you.

The first bite is about texture: the crunch gives way to the pull of warm cheese, and then the syrup hits — floral, not cloying, just sweet enough to make you close your eyes. Knafeh is Palestinian in origin, and the best versions come from the bakeries around Jaffa's clock tower, where they've been making it for generations. A slice costs ₪20–30 ($5–8), and you eat it standing at the counter, still warm, watching the next tray come out of the oven.

#### Bourekas

Every morning, before the beach fills up and the cafés get crowded, Tel Aviv runs on bourekas. These flaky, golden pastries — originally Ottoman, now thoroughly Israeli — come stuffed with salty cheese, mashed potato, spinach, or mushroom. The dough should shatter when you bite, leaving flakes on your shirt and a buttery residue on your fingers.

Find them at any bakery or market stall, especially at Carmel Market in the early hours when the vendors are still setting up. The protocol: order one or two, get them handed to you in wax paper, and eat while walking. A hard-boiled egg often comes on the side, along with pickles and a smear of zhoug (spicy Yemeni relish) if you're lucky. Bourekas run ₪8–15 ($2–4), making them the cheapest breakfast in the city and one of the best.

---

#### Israeli Breakfast

Not a dish, technically — more like a philosophy. The Israeli breakfast is the most ambitious first meal you'll encounter: eggs prepared to order, baskets of fresh bread, labneh drizzled with olive oil, multiple salads (tomato-cucumber, beet, carrot), olives, white cheeses, smoked fish, and always more food than you expected. It exists because Israelis treat morning eating as a serious affair, not something to be rushed.

Order it at any café, and expect to spend ₪60–90 ($16–25) for a spread that feeds two. The best approach: go slowly, try everything, and accept that you will not need lunch.

---

#### Israeli Wine

The surprise on this list. Wine from Israel — particularly reds from the Golan Heights and Galilee — has quietly become world-class over the past two decades, winning international competitions and showing up on sommelier lists in New York and London. The climate is Mediterranean, the winemakers are often trained in France or California, and the results are bold Cabernets, elegant Syrahs, and some genuinely interesting blends.

Most serious restaurants in Tel Aviv carry Israeli wines, and a good glass runs ₪40–70 ($11–19). Ask your server for something from Yarden, Golan Heights Winery, or the smaller boutique producers. For a tasting experience, visit the wine bars in Neve Tzedek or around the Port, where flights let you sample across the regions.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Tel Aviv eats late — lunch happens at 2 PM, dinner at 9 PM, and the shakshuka crowd appears at 11 AM. But the hummusiyot run on the opposite schedule: arrive early or miss out. If a place closes when the food runs out, believe it.

Sharing is expected. Order for the table, not for yourself — especially at sit-down restaurants where mezze-style eating is the norm. Don't hoard. Don't over-order until you've seen how much food actually arrives.

And learn the rhythm of Shabbat. From Friday sundown to Saturday night, much of the city goes quiet. Jaffa stays open, Arab restaurants keep serving, and some bars emerge after sunset on Saturday. But your Thursday and Friday morning should include stocking up at Carmel Market, when the energy peaks and the vendors are shouting prices at full volume.
