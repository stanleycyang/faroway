Three days in Los Angeles isn't enough. It's never enough. This is a city where you could spend a week just eating tacos across different neighborhoods, another week on Korean food alone, and still miss half of what matters. But three days of strategic, obsessive eating? That's enough to understand why LA might be the most exciting food city in America right now — a sprawling, traffic-choked, deeply delicious place where immigrant communities have been perfecting dishes for generations while young chefs remix everything. Here's how to eat it properly.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

LA restaurants generally don't take reservations for parties under four, but the places that do require booking — especially for dinner — fill up fast. Tip 20% at sit-down spots, $1-2 per drink at bars. Card works everywhere except some cash-only taco stands and markets. Lunch runs 11:30am-2pm, dinner starts late by American standards (7pm reservations are normal). Bring comfortable shoes and a car, or make peace with ride-shares — this city's geography will test you.

## Day 1: Downtown and the Eastside

**Morning**

Start where the city's current energy lives: the Arts District on the eastern edge of Downtown. Before the galleries and coffee roasters colonized these warehouses, this was industrial LA. Now it's where you'll find Gjusta's sister operation and some of the best morning eating in the city.

Head to the Original Pantry Cafe if you want the old-school experience — a 24-hour diner that's been serving since 1924 and has literally never closed, not once. Get the sourdough hotcakes with a side of their house-made sausage. The portions are absurd, the coffee is fine, and the crowd at 8am includes overnight workers, hungover twenty-somethings, and businessmen in suits. It costs $18-22 and sets a foundation you'll need.

For something more modern, walk to Bavel's neighborhood and find Tartine Bianco, where the bread alone justifies the trip. A morning bun and a coffee runs $12-15, but you'll think about that morning bun for weeks.

**Afternoon**

Now you earn your lunch. Walk or drive to Grand Central Market, the 1917 food hall that anchors Downtown's food identity. This isn't a curated Instagram market — it's chaotic, multilingual, and delicious. The vendors have turned over through the decades, but the bones remain.

Get one thing only for lunch, and make it count: Tacos Tumbras a Tomas for *carnitas*. The owner, Tomas Martinez, has been cooking here since 1993. His carnitas are braised, then crisped on the flat-top, served on doubled corn tortillas with nothing but salsa verde and raw onion. Two tacos run $8. They're perfect.

Wander the market after. Get a horchata at Villa Moreliana. Stop at Valeria's for a quick *pupusa* if you're still hungry. Buy dried chiles at Torres Produce — they're better and cheaper than anything you'll find at home. Budget $15-25 for grazing.

By late afternoon, drive east to Boyle Heights — historically Mexican-American, still the center of that community despite pressures. Get a *raspado* (shaved ice with fresh fruit and chamoy) from a street vendor if you spot one. Walk off lunch. This is LA at its most itself.

**Evening**

Dinner in the Arts District at Bavel, if you planned ahead and booked. This is the restaurant that made everyone pay attention to Middle Eastern food in LA, run by the team behind Bestia. The lamb neck shawarma with hummus is essential — tender, deeply spiced meat dragged through tahini-rich hummus on warm laffa bread. The duck 'nduja with date jam sounds strange and tastes incredible. A proper dinner here runs $75-95 per person with a drink. Worth every cent.

No reservation? Walk-ins sometimes work at the bar. Alternatively, Bavel's lunch service is easier to get into, and you could pivot dinner to Bestia next door (book that one weeks ahead) or keep it casual with a late-night bowl at Howlin' Ray's if you can handle the Nashville hot chicken.

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~$80 | Mid ~$120 | Splurge ~$180

## Day 2: Koreatown and Thai Town

**Morning**

Today you head west, then north, through neighborhoods where the food reflects decades of immigrant community building. Start in Koreatown, the dense, neon-lit district west of Downtown that holds the largest Korean population outside Korea.

Breakfast here means *juk* (rice porridge) at Jook Hyang or a full Korean breakfast spread, but ease in with something simpler: head to Cafe Mak for a pastry and coffee, Korean bakery style. The *soboro* bread (Korean streusel) and an Americano cost $8-10 and give you fuel for what's coming.

Alternatively, if you're ready for intensity, walk into any of the 24-hour Korean restaurants — Koreatown doesn't really sleep — and order *galbi-jjim* (braised short ribs) with banchan. Breakfast meat sweats are real. Budget $15-25.

**Afternoon**

Lunch is why you came to Koreatown. You have options:

For Korean BBQ, go to Park's BBQ. Yes, it's the famous one. Yes, there's usually a wait. Yes, it's worth it. The *chadolbaegi* (thin-sliced brisket) and prime *galbi* cook fast on the tabletop grill. Let the servers manage the meat — they're better at it than you. Lunch runs $45-60 per person.

For something faster and cheaper, get cold noodles (*naengmyeon*) at Yu Chun Chic Naeng Myun. The buckwheat noodles come in ice-cold beef broth with mustard and vinegar on the side. It's $15, it's refreshing, and it's one of the best versions outside Seoul.

After lunch, drive north to Thai Town — the stretch of Hollywood that's home to LA's Thai community. This isn't a polished neighborhood; it's strip malls and signage you can't read and some of the best Thai food in America.

Stop at Silom Market, a small grocery store with a hot food counter in back. Get a bag of *moo ping* (grilled pork skewers) for $6. Get sticky rice. Sit outside on whatever surface you can find and eat pork that's been marinating since morning, smoky and sweet and impossibly tender.

**Evening**

Dinner stays in Thai Town. Jitlada is the restaurant that put this neighborhood on the food map — a family-run spot known for southern Thai cooking that most Thai restaurants in America won't attempt. This food is spicy. Actually spicy. The *crying tiger* beef and the *crab yellow curry* are both famous. The menu is overwhelming; ask your server what's best tonight. Dinner runs $35-50 per person.

If you want something more modern, Night + Market Song offers Thai drinking food — *larb*, *nam tok*, dishes designed to go with beer and friends. It's loud, it's fun, and dinner costs $30-45.

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~$75 | Mid ~$130 | Splurge ~$200

## Day 3: The Westside and the Grand Finale

**Morning**

Your final day moves west toward the ocean. Start at Gjusta in Venice — a bakery, deli, and California-everything spot that's earned its reputation through sheer quality. Get the smoked fish plate with everything: house-cured salmon, crème fraîche, pickled onions, and bread that's worth the $24 price tag. Or go simple with a twice-baked almond croissant ($7) and coffee on the patio. Either way, arrive early — the weekend line builds fast.

Walk off breakfast on Abbot Kinney or down to the Venice boardwalk. The morning light in this neighborhood makes everything look good, even the tourists.

**Afternoon**

Lunch takes you to Santa Monica. Skip the pier (overpriced, overcrowded) and head to the Santa Monica Farmers Market if you're here Wednesday or Saturday. This is where LA chefs shop, and you'll understand why California produce has a reputation. The stone fruit in summer, the citrus in winter — buy something, eat it immediately.

For a proper lunch, walk to Cassia, a Southeast Asian brasserie on Santa Monica Boulevard. The Vietnamese pot au feu is meant for two and features braised short rib, bone marrow, and noodles in a fragrant broth. It's $65 and it's a meal you'll remember. The *kaya toast* as a starter — coconut jam, soft eggs, soy — bridges breakfast into lunch beautifully.

Late afternoon, drive back toward the city. Stop in Culver City if you want a coffee break at Cognoscenti, or just rest. You've been eating hard.

**Evening**

Your final dinner should be the best thing you eat all trip. Two options:

For the splurge, book Providence — a seafood tasting menu that ranks among the best in the country. Chef Michael Cimarusti's approach is obsessive, seasonal, and quietly stunning. The tasting runs $250+ per person. It's a special occasion meal, and this is a special occasion.

For something excellent but more accessible, go to Republique, the all-day restaurant in a historic building on La Brea. The roasted chicken for two is what everyone orders — crackling skin, perfect jus, worth the $65. The bone marrow appetizer with snails is LA fine dining without pretension. Dinner runs $70-100 per person.

Either way, end the night properly. Walk out full, happy, and already planning when you'll come back.

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~$85 | Mid ~$160 | Splurge ~$400

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Book dinner reservations 2-3 weeks ahead** for places like Bavel, Bestia, and Providence. Walk-ins work for lunch, but dinner at the popular spots requires planning.
- **Koreatown and Thai Town are better on weekdays** — locals know this, tourists don't. You'll wait less and get better service when the weekend crowds thin.
- **Cash still matters at markets and taco stands.** Keep $40-60 in small bills for street food, *raspados*, and the vendors who haven't (and won't) install card readers.
- **LA's best food often looks like nothing from outside.** Strip mall exteriors, minimal signage, no Instagram presence — trust the neighborhoods more than the aesthetics.
