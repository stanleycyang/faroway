Three days in Zanzibar taught me that this island eats differently than anywhere else on the East African coast. The Swahili kitchen here absorbed Arab traders, Indian merchants, Portuguese colonizers, and British spice hunters — then made something entirely its own. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Zanzibaris eat late — lunch rarely before 1pm, dinner after 8pm. Most local spots are cash-only; carry Tanzanian shillings for street food and small restaurants, though tourist-facing places accept US dollars (post-2006 bills only — older ones get refused). Tipping isn't expected at local joints but appreciated at restaurants (10% is generous). Dress modestly in Stone Town, especially around meal times when you're walking the old quarter. No reservations needed except for hotel fine dining.

## Day 1: Stone Town — The Old Quarter Awakening

### Morning

Start in the labyrinthine alleys of Stone Town before the heat sets in. Find a *mandazi* vendor near Darajani Market — these fried dough balls are Zanzibar's answer to breakfast, pillowy and slightly sweet, perfect for dunking into *spice tea* brewed dark with cloves, cinnamon, cardamom, and ginger. The best mandazi come from women frying them fresh in blackened pans on street corners, usually sold in bags of five or six. Don't overthink it — follow the smell of hot oil and cardamom.

The tea vendors near the market serve chai in small glasses for almost nothing. Sit on a plastic stool, watch the morning commerce, and let the spices wake you up properly.

**Cost**: $2–4 for mandazi and two teas.

### Afternoon

By 1pm, Darajani Market reaches full chaos — fishmongers shouting over tables of red snapper and octopus, spice sellers with burlap sacks of cloves and vanilla, fruit stalls stacked with jackfruit and soursop. Walk through slowly, but save your appetite for *urojo* — Zanzibar's signature soup and the island's most underrated dish.

Urojo is sold from carts and small shops around Stone Town's central streets. It's a bowl of contradictions: tangy tamarind broth, crispy *bhajia* (spiced potato fritters), chunks of boiled potato, shredded mango, crunchy cassava chips, and enough chili to make you sweat. Each vendor makes it slightly different. The versions near the old fort tend to be spicier; the ones deeper in the residential alleys are tangier. Get it with extra bhajia.

Eat standing up or perched on the vendor's bench. This is a $3 lunch that will recalibrate your entire understanding of Swahili street food.

**Cost**: $3–5 for urojo with extras.

### Evening

Forodhani Gardens transforms at sunset. The waterfront fills with vendors setting up grills and gas burners, smoke rising over the harbor as dhow boats drift past. This is Zanzibar's most famous food market, and despite the tourist crowds, it delivers.

Start with *Zanzibar pizza* — not pizza at all, but a thin crepe folded around minced meat, egg, vegetables, and sometimes cheese or Nutella, cooked on a flat griddle until crispy. The vendors with the longest lines make the best ones. Follow it with grilled octopus on a stick, brushed with lime and chili. Add sugarcane juice pressed fresh from a hand-crank machine.

The mistake tourists make is eating at the first stall. Walk the entire row first, watch who's cooking with confidence, then commit. Budget eaters can fill up here for $8–10; those wanting to sample everything might spend $20.

For a proper sit-down dinner, walk five minutes to Mercury's Bar on the waterfront. The food is secondary to the Freddie Mercury memorabilia (he was born here), but the grilled fish is reliable and the setting — Stone Town's seafront at night — justifies the markup.

**Cost**: Forodhani $8–20 · Mercury's dinner $25–40.

**Day 1 total**: Budget ~$15 | Mid ~$45 | Splurge ~$65

## Day 2: Spice Farm to Coast — The Source of Everything

### Morning

Book a spice tour that leaves early from Stone Town. Most farms are 30–45 minutes northeast, and the morning light through vanilla vines and cinnamon trees is worth the early start. The tours themselves involve guides cracking open nutmeg, handing you fresh lemongrass to smell, and climbing trees to cut down jackfruit.

But you're here for the meal at the end. Good spice tours conclude with a lunch cooked on-site: *pilau* rice fragrant with cardamom and cumin, served with slow-cooked beef or chicken, fresh fruit, and spice tea. This is home cooking, the kind Zanzibari families make for celebrations. The rice should be golden, each grain separate, the meat falling apart. If your tour doesn't include a meal, you booked the wrong tour.

**Cost**: Spice tour with lunch $25–40 per person (negotiate beforehand; this should include transport from Stone Town).

### Afternoon

Return to Stone Town and find shade. The afternoon heat flattens the island, and locals disappear until 4pm. Use this time to explore Kizimkazi in the southeast if you have a driver — it's where the oldest mosque in East Africa still stands, and the village remains a working fishing community untouched by beach tourism.

Late afternoon, find a local restaurant near the old fort for *octopus curry* — tender pieces of octopus simmered in coconut milk with tomatoes, turmeric, and green chilies. The sauce should be thin enough to soak into rice but rich with coconut fat. This dish requires patience; the octopus needs long cooking to avoid rubber. Ask your hotel staff where they eat octopus curry, not where tourists should go.

**Cost**: $8–15 for octopus curry with rice.

### Evening

For dinner, head slightly upscale. Emerson Spice or one of Stone Town's rooftop restaurants offers the same Swahili flavors with better presentation and harbor views. Order the seafood platter if available — grilled prawns, fish in coconut sauce, octopus — and finish with Zanzibar coffee, which carries the island's clove and cinnamon into the roast.

This is the night to spend more if your budget allows. The rooftop restaurants in Stone Town aren't pretentious; they're just the same food, served slower, with someone else doing the dishes.

**Cost**: $40–80 for a full dinner with drinks.

**Day 2 total**: Budget ~$50 | Mid ~$80 | Splurge ~$135

## Day 3: Beach Coast and the Farewell Feast

### Morning

Leave Stone Town for the east coast — Jambiani or Paje. The drive takes about an hour, and the landscape shifts from Stone Town's density to palm groves and wide tidal flats.

Breakfast in Jambiani is quieter than anywhere else on the island. This fishing village hasn't fully converted to tourism, and the morning meal might be simple: more mandazi, fresh mango, instant coffee. Seek out a guesthouse or small café run by locals rather than a resort. The food won't be elaborate, but sitting on a terrace watching fishermen drag their boats onto the sand is the right way to start the day.

**Cost**: $5–10 for a simple breakfast.

### Afternoon

The east coast has dramatic tides — check times before planning any beach activity. At low tide, women from Jambiani wade into the flats to harvest seaweed, which gets exported for cosmetics but also eaten locally in salads and soups.

Lunch should be at a beach shack in Paje or Jambiani: grilled fish caught that morning, served with coconut rice and a simple tomato-onion salad. The fish will be whole — head, tail, bones — cooked over charcoal and served with lime wedges and pili pili sauce. Ask for whatever they caught today. Expect to pay more than Stone Town prices but less than resort rates.

**Cost**: $12–20 for grilled fish lunch.

### Evening

Your last dinner should be memorable. Return to Stone Town or stay on the coast for a seafood blowout — either works. If you're back in Stone Town, spend more time at Forodhani, ordering the things you missed the first night. If you're on the coast, book a sunset dhow cruise that includes dinner on board: grilled lobster, prawns, and whatever the crew caught, eaten while anchored off a sandbar as the sun drops.

This is where splurge money pays off. A private dhow dinner for two runs $100–150 but includes the boat, the crew, the sunset, and more seafood than you can finish. Budget travelers can achieve the same flavors at Forodhani for $15, just without the water.

End the night with one last spice tea, wherever you find it.

**Cost**: Beach dinner $20–35 · Dhow dinner $100–150 per person · Forodhani farewell $15–25.

**Day 3 total**: Budget ~$40 | Mid ~$70 | Splurge ~$200

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Urojo peaks at lunch.** The vendors who make it fresh in the morning sell out by mid-afternoon. By evening, what's left has been sitting too long. Go between noon and 2pm.

- **Spice tour meals vary wildly.** Ask specifically if lunch is included and cooked on-site. Some tours just hand you packaged snacks. The ones with a proper kitchen and family cooking are worth paying more for.

- **The east coast eats simply but honestly.** Don't expect Stone Town's variety in Jambiani or Paje. What you will get is the freshest fish of your trip, cooked by people who have been doing it the same way for generations.

- **Forodhani works best on an empty stomach.** Go hungry, eat slowly, and sample from multiple vendors rather than committing to one stall immediately. The grilled octopus varies in quality — watch for vendors who grill to order rather than from a pre-cooked pile.
