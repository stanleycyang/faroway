Three days in Seoul, one mission: eat everything worth eating. I landed with a list of 47 dishes, a backup phone battery, and the kind of hunger that comes from months of research. What follows is the itinerary I built meal by meal, tracking down the versions worth crossing the city for — and the practical details that actually matter when you're standing on a street corner at 7 AM wondering where breakfast happens in this town.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Seoul runs late. Breakfast spots often don't open until 8 or 9 AM, lunch crowds peak around 1 PM, and dinner rarely starts before 7 PM. Tipping doesn't exist here — don't leave money on the table. Cards work almost everywhere, but markets prefer cash. For Korean BBQ and popular restaurants, walk-ins work for lunch but dinner requires reservations, especially on weekends. Pour drinks for others before yourself, receive with two hands from elders, and remove your shoes when the floor is raised.

## Day 1: Myeongdong and the Markets

### Morning

Start in **Myeongdong** before the shopping crowds descend. By 9 AM, the street food vendors are firing up griddles, and this is when you eat **hotteok** — those crispy-chewy pancakes filled with brown sugar, cinnamon, and crushed nuts that ooze when you bite through. The version at the corner of Myeongdong 8-gil has the right ratio of caramelized shell to molten center. Grab one standing up, burn your tongue slightly, and accept that breakfast in Seoul often happens on your feet. Follow it with a paper cup of sweet red bean porridge from any vendor with a line.

*Estimated cost: $4–$6*

### Afternoon

Subway to **Gwangjang Market** for the lunch that defines this trip. This is Seoul's oldest market, and the food stalls in the center aisle haven't changed their recipes in decades. Find a spot at one of the long counters and order **bibimbap** — but here it comes in a hot stone bowl, the rice crackling against the sides, topped with raw beef that cooks from the residual heat. Mix in the gochujang yourself, scraping up the crispy rice from the bottom. The ajummas running these stalls have been doing this longer than you've been alive. Trust them.

Stay for the market walk: towers of **tteokbokki** in bubbling red sauce, blood sausage (sundae) sliced thick, and mung bean pancakes (bindaetteok) fried to order. Graze through the aisles. Point at what looks good. Everything costs a few thousand won.

*Estimated cost: $15–$22*

### Evening

Dinner moves to **Hongdae**, the university district where the energy shifts from traditional to young and loud. This is where you eat Korean BBQ properly — not at a tourist spot, but at a neighborhood joint where the exhaust fans roar and the soju flows. Order **samgyeopsal** (pork belly) and **chadolbaegi** (thinly sliced beef brisket). The meat comes raw; you grill it yourself on the tabletop, wrap it in lettuce with garlic, ssamjang, and whatever banchan catches your eye. The sides refill for free — don't be shy about asking.

Budget spots run around $15–$18 per person for unlimited pork. Mid-range beef BBQ pushes $35–$45. The splurge version involves aged hanwoo beef at a reservation-only spot in Gangnam, and that's $80–$120 before soju.

*Estimated cost: $25–$80*

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$200

## Day 2: Ikseon-dong to Insadong — Old Seoul Eating

### Morning

The hanok alleys of **Ikseon-dong** feel like a secret the city almost forgot to mention. These renovated traditional houses now hold tiny cafes and restaurants, and morning here is quiet — locals shuffling to work, coffee roasters just opening their doors. Find a spot serving **juk** (rice porridge) — the abalone version is silky, savory, and gentle on a stomach that's been working hard. This is how Koreans eat breakfast when they actually sit down for it. The portions are larger than expected, the banchan minimal, and the bill around $10–$12 for a bowl that feeds you for hours.

*Estimated cost: $10–$14*

### Afternoon

Walk ten minutes to **Insadong**, the traditional art and tea district. Lunch here should be **japchae** at a proper sit-down restaurant — not the sad side-dish version you get at BBQ joints, but the main event: sweet potato glass noodles stir-fried with spinach, carrots, mushrooms, and slivers of beef, finished with sesame oil that coats everything. Order it with a stone pot of rice and banchan. The combination restaurants along the main alley serve reliable versions for $12–$18.

After eating, duck into a traditional teahouse. Not for caffeine — for **sujeonggwa**, the cinnamon-ginger punch served cold with floating pine nuts, or **sikhye**, sweet rice drink. These places charge $6–$8 for a pot of tea and unlimited time. Sit on floor cushions. Slow down. You've earned it.

*Estimated cost: $18–$28*

### Evening

Tonight you eat **kimchi jjigae** — the fermented kimchi stew that tastes like Korea's collective grandmother made it. The versions worth seeking use aged kimchi, pork belly, and tofu, bubbling in individual stone pots that stay hot through the last bite. **Seongsu-dong**, Seoul's converted warehouse district sometimes called the city's Brooklyn, has emerged as a dinner destination with restaurants serving traditional stews alongside industrial-chic coffee roasters. The contrast works. Budget jjigae runs $8–$10. Add grilled mackerel or more banchan and you're at $15–$25. The high-end play is a full **hanjeongsik** spread — a traditional Korean course meal with dozens of small dishes — and that's $50–$100 per person at the right place.

*Estimated cost: $20–$80*

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~$48 | Mid ~$120 | Splurge ~$220

## Day 3: Gangnam, Tradition, and the Farewell Feast

### Morning

Your last morning starts in **Gangnam**, the district that launched a thousand memes but also houses serious restaurants. Seek out a **gukbap** spot — rice soup, served scalding hot, with your choice of beef, pork, or blood sausage. The Busan-style dwaeji gukbap (pork rice soup) versions are white, porky, and deeply satisfying. You season it yourself at the table with salted shrimp, chili flakes, and chopped green onions. This is workingman's breakfast, and it costs $8–$11 at places packed with people in suits.

*Estimated cost: $8–$12*

### Afternoon

Return to the markets for your final food walk, but this time hit **Namdaemun Market** near Myeongdong. It's more chaotic than Gwangjang, more local, less polished. The **kalguksu** stalls here serve hand-cut knife noodles in anchovy broth — slurp-worthy, cheap, and filling. Wander the food alleys for whatever catches your eye: fish cakes on sticks, fried dumplings, sweet pancakes with seeds. This is snack lunch, eaten standing up, maybe on a plastic stool if you're lucky.

Take the afternoon to walk off the food. The streets around **Bukchon Hanok Village** reward wandering, and you'll burn enough calories to justify dinner.

*Estimated cost: $12–$18*

### Evening

The last meal matters. Make it **samgyetang** — whole young chicken stuffed with glutinous rice, ginseng, jujubes, and garlic, simmered until the meat falls off the bone. This is restorative cooking, the dish Koreans eat for stamina and health, and finishing your trip with it feels right. The original restaurants near Gyeongbokgung Palace have been serving it for fifty years. Budget versions run $15–$18; mid-range places with better birds charge $25–$35. The splurge is finding a place that ages their ginseng and uses free-range chicken — $50–$70, and you'll taste the difference.

Finish with soju. Maybe two bottles. Toast the city, the vendors, the ajummas who refilled your banchan without asking. You ate Seoul properly.

*Estimated cost: $25–$80*

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$200

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Install Naver Map before landing.** Google Maps barely functions for Korean navigation. Naver shows restaurant hours, photos of menus, and real-time transit. It's essential.
- **Lunch specials are your secret weapon.** Many restaurants serving $40 dinners offer the same quality at lunch for $15–$20. Go big midday, graze at night.
- **Point and eat.** Language barriers dissolve when you point confidently at what the next table is having. Koreans respect enthusiastic eating.
- **Markets close earlier than restaurants.** Gwangjang and Namdaemun start shutting down by 6 PM. Hit them at lunch, not dinner.
