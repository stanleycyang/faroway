The salmon arrives at my table still sizzling, its skin blackened and crackling, the flesh beneath so deeply coral it looks like a sunset trapped in protein. It's 7 PM at a waterfront restaurant in Kitsilano, and the mountains across the inlet are turning pink in the fading light. I pull the first flake apart with my fork—it separates like pages of a book, glistening with its own oils—and somewhere between my mouth and my brain, I understand why people built a city here.

Vancouver eats like a port city should: voraciously, internationally, with the sea and the mountains showing up on every plate. After three weeks of eating my way through its neighborhoods, I've come to understand this place through its food—the Indigenous salmon traditions, the century-old Chinatown, the Japanese influence that runs so deep the sushi here rivals Tokyo, the Canadian comfort food that sticks to your ribs through rain-soaked winters. This is everything you need to eat.

#### Wild Pacific Salmon

Let's start with the fish that built this coast. Wild Pacific salmon isn't just food here—it's culture, economy, and ceremony stretching back thousands of years. The flesh is denser than Atlantic farmed salmon, more muscular from fighting currents, with a clean ocean flavor that needs almost nothing to shine.

You'll find it everywhere: cedar-planked at upscale restaurants, candy-smoked at Indigenous-owned shops, raw and gleaming in sushi rolls, or grilled simply with butter at no-frills fish counters. The sockeye runs from June through September, when the fish are at their fattest and most flavorful—this is the salmon you want, its flesh the color of ripe persimmons. In the off-season, look for flash-frozen wild salmon; it's still superior to anything farmed.

At Granville Island Public Market, you can buy a cedar-wrapped fillet for around $18–22 per pound, ready to throw on a hotel barbecue. For a sit-down meal, expect to pay $28–40 for a salmon entrée at waterfront restaurants—worth every dollar when you're watching the seaplanes land while you eat.

#### Sushi

I'll say what sushi chefs in Tokyo have admitted to me: Vancouver's sushi might be the best outside Japan. It's not hyperbole. The city sits on the Pacific Rim, receives the same cold-water fish that Japan prizes, and has absorbed Japanese culinary immigration since the early 1900s. The result is a sushi culture so developed that even mid-range spots serve fish that would cost triple in New York.

The tells of a good Vancouver sushi bar: the rice is warm and loose, seasoned with a whisper of vinegar. The fish is cut thick—none of that paper-thin sadness. The omakase menus trust you with unfamiliar textures: geoduck clam that snaps between your teeth, sea urchin that tastes like the cleanest tide pool imaginable, wild sablefish belly that melts before you can chew.

Head to the blocks around Robson and Denman for accessible excellence, or venture to Richmond for the spots where menus are Japanese-first, English-second. Budget $15–25 for a solid lunch set, $40–80 for dinner omakase. Go at 5:30 PM—before the dinner rush—for the best seat at the bar.

#### Japadog

Now for the beautiful absurdity of Vancouver street food: a hot dog topped with teriyaki sauce, Japanese mayo, nori seaweed, and sometimes bonito flakes that wave at you from the heat. Japadog started as a single street cart downtown in 2005, a Japanese immigrant couple's audacious fusion of North American fast food with Tokyo convenience-store flavors. It shouldn't work. It works completely.

The Terimayo is the gateway dog: smoky pork sausage, sweet-savory teriyaki glaze, that distinctive Kewpie mayo richness. The Okonomi upgrades you with bonito, seaweed, and okonomiyaki sauce. They're $7–10 each, eaten standing on the sidewalk with your hands getting sticky, watching the Burrard Street crowds part around you. Find the carts downtown near the Art Gallery, or hit the brick-and-mortar location on Robson Street.

#### Dim Sum

Chinatown in Vancouver is one of the oldest in North America, and the Cantonese food traditions here run deep—but for the full dim sum experience, you need to take the Canada Line twenty minutes south to Richmond. This suburban city is majority Chinese-Canadian, and its dim sum restaurants are the real thing: carts wheeling between tables, grandmothers pointing decisively at bamboo steamers, chaos and tea and the most perfect har gow you've ever tasted.

Those har gow—shrimp dumplings—are the test. The wrapper should be translucent, almost glassy, with exactly seven pleats. The shrimp inside should snap audibly when you bite. Siu mai come next: open-topped pork and shrimp dumplings crowned with orange roe. Then char siu bao, those fluffy white buns hiding sticky-sweet barbecue pork. Then chicken feet, if you're brave—all collagen and sauce.

Go on a weekday morning around 10 AM to avoid the weekend waits. Budget $15–25 per person if you're sharing with a table; order too much, always. The best spots require a wait even then—look for the parking lots full of Mercedes and BMWs, the universal signal of excellent Chinese food.

#### Poutine

Canada's gift to drunk eating, hangover curing, and cold-weather survival. Poutine is simple: crispy fries, fresh cheese curds, hot gravy. The magic happens when you eat it fast, before the curds lose their squeak, while the gravy is still pooling and the fries still have structure. It's salty, rich, and deeply satisfying in a way that makes you understand why Canadians endure winter.

Vancouver puts its own spin on the classic—you'll find versions with pulled pork, butter chicken, even salmon (it works, somehow). But start traditional. The cheese curds must squeak when you bite them—that rubbery resistance means freshness. The gravy should be beefy and slightly peppery, not too thick.

Find solid poutine at late-night diners in Gastown, food trucks parked outside breweries, and casual spots on Commercial Drive. Expect to pay $10–16 for a loaded portion. Best consumed after midnight, ideally while it's raining, which in Vancouver means almost any night from October to April.

#### Nanaimo Bar

This no-bake bar from Vancouver Island has achieved cult status across Canada: a coconut-chocolate base, a thick layer of custard-yellow buttercream, and a dark chocolate shell on top. The textures are everything—crumbly bottom, creamy middle, crisp chocolate snap. It's aggressively sweet, unabashedly retro, and somehow perfect with a black coffee.

Named for the city of Nanaimo, a ferry ride away on Vancouver Island, the bar has been a Canadian staple since the 1950s. Every bakery in Vancouver makes their own version—some add mint, espresso, or peanut butter to the filling. The classic remains best: pure vanilla custard, no distractions.

Pick one up at Granville Island bakeries for $3–5, or grab a whole pan to bring home. They keep in the fridge for a week, though they never last that long.

#### Spot Prawns

For six weeks in May and June, Vancouver loses its mind over spot prawns. These wild Pacific crustaceans are sweeter than lobster, with a snap to the flesh that farmed shrimp can only dream of. Restaurants run special menus, fishermen sell directly from boats at the dock, and locals queue before sunrise at the Fisherman's Wharf.

Eat them simply: steamed or grilled, with butter and lemon. The heads are edible—suck out the brains if you're committed; they taste like concentrated ocean. Some restaurants serve them live, still twitching, flash-cooked at your table. It's primal, delicious, and fleeting.

During the short season, expect to pay $25–35 per pound at dockside sales, $40–60 for a prepared plate at restaurants. The rest of the year, they're frozen—still good, but not transcendent.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Vancouver diners are casual but intentional. Reservations matter at popular spots, especially on weekends—book a few days ahead for dinner. Lunch is often the better value; many high-end places offer abbreviated menus at friendlier prices.

Sharing is encouraged, especially at Asian restaurants—order for the table, not for yourself. Lunch rushes hit around noon; dinner peaks at 7:30 PM. Off-hours reward you with better service and the kitchen's full attention.

Tap water here is pristine mountain runoff—order it proudly. Tip 15–20 percent at sit-down restaurants. And if you're exploring Richmond for dim sum or Chinese food, bring cash; some old-school spots don't take cards.
