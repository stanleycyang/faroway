Three days in Santorini taught me that the island's food is as dramatic as its cliffs — volcanic soil produces tomatoes sweeter than anywhere else in Greece, and the local fava beans have their own protected designation. I came to eat everything, properly, and what follows is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I arrived.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Greeks eat late. Lunch happens between 2–4pm, dinner rarely before 9pm. Most restaurants accept cards, but small tavernas and bakeries often prefer cash (euros). Tip 5–10% at sit-down places — it's appreciated but not expected to American levels. Book any caldera-view dinner at least a week ahead in summer, or resign yourself to eating at 6pm like the cruise ship crowd. Bring comfortable shoes — you'll walk more than you expect between meals.

## Day 1: Oia — Volcanic Elegance and the Island's Best Produce

### Morning

Start in Oia before the tour buses arrive. By 8:30am, the village belongs to you and a handful of photographers. Walk the marble paths to **Karma Café** on the main walkway, where they serve thick Greek yogurt with Santorini thyme honey and walnuts alongside proper Greek coffee. The honey here is darker than mainland varieties, almost savory, pulled from bees that feed on volcanic wildflowers.

Order a plate of fresh fruit — the cherry tomatoes are what put Santorini on the food map. They're tiny, intensely sweet, grown in volcanic ash without irrigation. Even at breakfast, you'll understand why.

*Estimated cost: €12–18*

### Afternoon

Walk down the 300 steps to **Amoudi Bay** for lunch. The descent takes twenty minutes, and your legs will burn coming back up, but the reward is a table at the water's edge where fishing boats bob three feet away.

Order **grilled octopus** — this is non-negotiable. The tentacles come charred and tender, dressed with nothing but olive oil and lemon. The texture should be like butter with a smoky crust. If it's rubbery, you're at the wrong place. The tavernas at water level (look for locals, not laminated menus) grill their catch over charcoal, and you can often see your octopus hanging to dry on a line before it hits the flame.

Add a simple Greek salad — the tomatoes here are different, I promise. Skip the bread basket; save room.

*Estimated cost: €28–40*

After climbing back up (take your time, there's no shame in stopping), wander Oia's back alleys where you'll find small shops selling capers packed in sea salt, sun-dried cherry tomatoes, and local olive oil in ceramic bottles. These make better souvenirs than keychains.

### Evening

Dinner at a caldera-view restaurant in Oia is expensive and worth it once. **Book ahead** — I cannot stress this enough. You're paying for the sunset as much as the food, and that's honest.

Order **fava** as a starter, the creamy split pea puree that Santorini claims as its own. It comes drizzled with raw onion and olive oil, meant to be scooped with bread. Follow with grilled fish — ask what came in that morning. The mezedes (small plates) here are designed for sharing: taramasalata, tzatziki, stuffed grape leaves.

The bill will sting, but the sun dropping into the caldera while you eat fried zucchini flowers is the kind of expense you remember correctly.

*Estimated cost: €65–120*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€105 | Mid-range ~€150 | Splurge ~€180

## Day 2: Fira and the Wine Villages — The Island's Working Heart

### Morning

Fira wakes up slower than Oia and has more grit. The capital sprawls along the cliff edge with a mix of tourist shops and actual Greek life happening in the back streets.

Find a bakery for **bougatsa** (warm phyllo filled with semolina custard) and strong coffee. The bakeries on the pedestrian street near the cable car station serve workers heading to early shifts — follow them. A bougatsa costs €3 and will carry you to lunch.

Alternatively, the cafés along the caldera rim serve full Greek breakfasts: eggs, tomatoes, olives, feta, bread. Sit on a terrace and watch the cruise ships anchor far below.

*Estimated cost: €8–15*

### Afternoon

Drive (or ATV) twenty minutes south to **Megalochori**, a wine village where tourists rarely venture. The streets are quieter, the whitewash is a little dustier, and you'll find tavernas cooking for Greek families rather than Instagram.

This is where you eat **tomatokeftedes** done right. These cherry tomato fritters are the island's signature dish — crispy outside, sweet and herby inside, nothing like the dense, oily versions served at tourist traps. In Megalochori, they're made by hand, often by someone's grandmother, and they come with a squeeze of lemon.

Order them alongside more fava, a plate of fried capers, and whatever grilled meat looks good. The lamb here is excellent — raised on volcanic scrub, lean and intensely flavored.

*Estimated cost: €25–40*

After lunch, drive to **Venetsanos Winery** for a tasting with caldera views. Santorini's Assyrtiko wine is bone-dry and mineral, shaped by volcanic soil and sea wind. A tasting flight runs €15–25 and includes local cheese. Buy a bottle for your last night.

### Evening

Back to Fira for dinner, but skip the caldera-view places. Instead, find a taverna in the back streets where Greek is the primary language on the menu.

Order **moussaka** — the layered eggplant and meat casserole with béchamel that everyone thinks they know. A proper version is lighter than expected, the eggplant silky rather than oily, the meat sauce spiced with cinnamon and allspice. This is comfort food meant for sharing, ideally with a carafe of house wine and nowhere to be afterward.

Finish with Greek coffee and a small glass of rakomelo (honey-spiced raki) if offered.

*Estimated cost: €30–55*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€78 | Mid-range ~€120 | Splurge ~€160

## Day 3: The Southern Coast — Beaches, Fish, and Farewell

### Morning

Drive to **Pyrgos**, the hilltop village with Venetian castle ruins at its peak. This is Santorini's old capital, and it feels more authentically Greek than anywhere else on the island.

Find the bakery near the main square for **spanakopita** (spinach and feta in phyllo) still warm from the oven. Eat it while climbing to the castle ruins, where 360-degree views stretch across the entire island. The morning light here is soft and golden, and you'll have the place nearly to yourself.

*Estimated cost: €5–10*

### Afternoon

Head to **Vlychada Beach** on the southern coast, where lunar-white cliffs rise behind the sand like something from another planet. The beach has a few tavernas serving simple, perfect food.

This is **souvlaki** territory — grilled pork or chicken in warm pita with tzatziki, tomatoes, and onions. Order two (you'll want two), plus fried potatoes and a cold Mythos beer. Eat with your feet in volcanic black sand.

The fish restaurants near Vlychada's small marina serve the freshest catch on the island. If you're ready for a longer lunch, skip the souvlaki and order a whole grilled fish with horta (wild greens) and fried potatoes. Point at what looks good in the ice case.

*Estimated cost: €18–45*

### Evening

For your final dinner, return to where the island cooks best: the back streets of Fira or a quiet table in Imerovigli, the village between Fira and Oia that locals consider the most beautiful.

Order what you loved most over the past three days. For me, it was more tomatokeftedes, more fava, more grilled octopus. Add something new: **baklava** for dessert, the layered phyllo with honey and pistachios that every taverna serves but few make in-house. Ask before ordering — the homemade versions are denser, less sweet, and worth seeking out.

Open that Assyrtiko you bought yesterday. Watch the lights flicker on across the caldera.

*Estimated cost: €45–90*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€68 | Mid-range ~€115 | Splurge ~€145

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The tomatoes are seasonal.** Santorini cherry tomatoes peak June through September. Visit outside this window and you'll get mainland produce dressed up in local branding.
- **Lunch is the value meal.** Many restaurants offer identical dishes at lunch for 20–30% less than dinner prices, with better seating availability and softer light for photos.
- **Skip restaurants with photos on the menu.** This rule holds worldwide, but especially in tourist-heavy Oia. The best food hides behind handwritten Greek signs.
- **Water is expensive.** Santorini imports its drinking water. Bring a reusable bottle and refill at your hotel to save €3–5 per meal on bottled water.
