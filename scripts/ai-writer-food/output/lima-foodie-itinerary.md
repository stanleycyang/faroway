# Lima Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Lima

Three days. One city. Approximately seventeen meals if you count strategically. Lima didn't become South America's culinary capital by accident — it happened through centuries of Andean, Spanish, African, Chinese, and Japanese influences colliding on one Pacific coastline. This itinerary is what I wish someone had handed me before I landed: every dish worth tracking down, every neighborhood worth walking hungry through, and every practical detail that separates eating well from eating memorably.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Lunch is the main event here — most Peruvians eat their biggest meal between 1–3 PM, and "menú del día" set lunches offer incredible value ($4–8 for soup, main, drink). Cevicherías close by 4 PM because fresh fish is a morning affair. Top restaurants like Central or Maido require reservations weeks ahead. Tipping runs 10% at sit-down restaurants; cash in soles works everywhere, though nicer spots take cards. And please — skip the street ceviche. Your stomach will thank you.

## Day 1: Miraflores — Where Lima Feeds Its Visitors (For Good Reason)

Start where the ocean meets the cliffs. Miraflores is the neighborhood most travelers land in, and while it's polished compared to grittier Lima districts, the food here is genuinely excellent — this is where the city's best cevicherías compete for glory.

### Morning: The Proper Peruvian Breakfast

Forget the hotel buffet. Walk to any corner bakery or café along Avenida Larco and order **tamal verde** — a steamed corn masa pocket stuffed with chicken and wrapped in banana leaves, dyed green with cilantro and ají. Pair it with chicha morada, that deep purple corn drink that tastes like spiced plum juice. This is what Limeños actually eat in the morning: something warm, something filling, something that costs S/.8–12 ($2–3).

For a sit-down option, head to a café near Kennedy Park and order pan con chicharrón — a sandwich of slow-cooked pork, sweet potato, and salsa criolla that'll carry you deep into the afternoon.

**Estimated cost:** $3–8

### Afternoon: The Ceviche Pilgrimage

This is why you're here. By noon, find your way to a proper cevichería — not a tourist spot with laminated menus, but somewhere the locals queue. Order the **ceviche clásico**: cubes of corvina or lenguado cured in tiger's milk (that electric lime-chili-fish leche de tigre), piled with red onion slivers, and served with chunks of sweet potato and giant corn kernels called choclo.

The best ceviche doesn't sit in the lime juice waiting for you — it's assembled à la minute. The fish should taste clean, bright, almost floral. If it smells fishy, leave.

Afterward, walk the Malecón — that clifftop promenade stretching along the Pacific — and let the fog and sea air settle your first serious Lima meal.

**Estimated cost:** $12–25 (ceviche portions are meant to share, or not — no judgment)

### Evening: Lomo Saltado and the Chinese-Peruvian Connection

Dinner in Miraflores means **lomo saltado**, and understanding this dish means understanding Lima's chifa heritage — Chinese-Peruvian fusion born from Cantonese immigrants who arrived in the 1800s. The dish is stir-fried beef with tomatoes and onions, kissed with soy sauce, and served over (and with) french fries and rice. Yes, carbs on carbs. It works.

Find a chifa restaurant or a traditional criolla spot. The beef should be seared hard and fast, the tomatoes barely cooked, the fries crispy enough to hold up against the sauce. Order a pisco sour to start — you're in Peru, after all — and finish with a second one because you've earned it.

**Estimated cost:** $18–40

**Day 1 Total:** Budget ~$35 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$150

## Day 2: San Isidro and Surquillo — Where Limeños Actually Eat

Leave the tourist polish behind. Today you eat where Lima feeds itself: the upscale dining rooms of San Isidro and the gloriously chaotic stalls of Mercado de Surquillo.

### Morning: Market Breakfast at Mercado de Surquillo

Take an Uber to Mercado de Surquillo before 9 AM. This market isn't hidden, but it's not on most tourist loops either — it's where chefs shop and where neighborhood regulars eat standing at juice counters.

Start with a **jugo especial** — fresh-squeezed orange, papaya, and lucuma blended on the spot. Then find a stall serving caldo de gallina, that restorative hen soup that Peruvians swear cures everything from hangovers to heartbreak. The broth is golden, the noodles are thick, and there's usually a whole chicken leg submerged somewhere in there.

Wander the fruit stalls. Try cherimoya (Mark Twain called it "the most delicious fruit known to man"), camu camu, and granadilla. Buy a bag of canchita (toasted corn kernels) for later.

**Estimated cost:** $5–10

### Afternoon: The Causa and Ají de Gallina Test

For lunch, head into San Isidro — Lima's most upscale district, where corporate Lima takes two-hour lunches and expense accounts flow freely.

Order **causa limeña**: cold mashed potato layered with avocado and either chicken salad or crab, pressed into a terrine and served sliced. The potato should be silky, the ají amarillo paste giving it that signature yellow glow. This is elegant comfort food, the kind of dish that looks simple but falls apart when done wrong.

Pair it with **ají de gallina** — shredded chicken swimming in a creamy sauce made from yellow peppers, walnuts, and cheese. It comes over rice with black olives and a hard-boiled egg. This is the dish Peruvian grandmothers fight about.

If time allows, walk to Huaca Huallamarca — an ancient adobe pyramid sitting incongruously between apartment towers. Lima does this: buries pyramids between buildings, then goes back to lunch.

**Estimated cost:** $15–35

### Evening: A Tasting Menu Worth the Splurge

If you've booked ahead (and you should have), tonight is for one of Lima's world-class tasting menus. Central, Maido, Kjolle — these restaurants aren't just meals, they're arguments about what Peruvian cuisine can become.

At Maido, you'll experience Nikkei cuisine — the Japanese-Peruvian fusion that makes Lima unlike anywhere else on earth. Expect 12–15 courses: sashimi kissed with ají amarillo, miso-marinated fish, rice cooked to Japanese precision with Peruvian ingredients. The meal takes three hours. You won't want it to end.

**Estimated cost:** $150–250 (worth it, once)

**Day 2 Total:** Budget ~$50 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$295

## Day 3: Barranco and Centro Histórico — The Soul of Lima

Your final day splits between Barranco's bohemian energy and the colonial grandeur of Centro Histórico. Eat like you're leaving tomorrow — because you are.

### Morning: Breakfast in Barranco

Barranco wakes up slowly. Grab coffee at a café near the Bridge of Sighs, order butifarra — a sandwich of thinly sliced ham, onion salsa, and a smear of ají — and watch the neighborhood shake off the night before.

Walk through the bougainvillea-draped streets. This is Lima's artist quarter: galleries, murals, crumbling colonial houses painted in pastels. Let the morning stretch.

**Estimated cost:** $6–12

### Afternoon: Anticuchos and Colonial Lima

After noon, Uber to Centro Histórico. Lima's historic center is loud, chaotic, beautiful, and occasionally sketchy — stick to the main plazas and keep your phone pocketed.

Find a street cart or casual restaurant serving **anticuchos** — grilled beef heart skewers, marinated in vinegar and ají panca, charred over coals and served with potatoes. They're smoky, tender, and irrationally delicious. This is Lima's street food royalty, and the best anticucheras have been perfecting their recipe for decades.

Walk off the meat at Plaza Mayor. Admire the Cathedral. Visit the San Francisco Church catacombs if bones arranged by type appeal to you. Buy a bag of roasted habas (fava beans) from a street vendor.

**Estimated cost:** $8–15

### Evening: The Farewell Feast

Return to Barranco for your final dinner. Tonight is about criolla cooking — the Afro-Peruvian and coastal traditions that define Lima's soul food.

Order **arroz con mariscos** — rice loaded with clams, mussels, shrimp, and squid in a sauce fragrant with ají panca. Or go for **jalea**, a mountain of fried seafood and yuca that arrives looking like Neptune's revenge.

Finish with **picarones**: sweet potato and squash doughnuts, fried to order and drizzled with chancaca syrup — that dark, molasses-like sauce that makes them transcendent. Eat them warm, standing at a cart if you find a good one, or sitting in a restaurant watching Barranco come alive for the night.

End at a rooftop bar. Order a chilcano (pisco, ginger ale, lime, bitters). Look at the Pacific. You've eaten Lima properly.

**Estimated cost:** $25–60

**Day 3 Total:** Budget ~$40 | Mid ~$75 | Splurge ~$180

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Eat ceviche before 2 PM.** Cevicherías close early because fresh fish is a morning catch — if a place is serving ceviche at dinner, question their supply chain.
- **Menú del día is your budget weapon.** Most local restaurants offer a set lunch (soup, main, drink) for S/.12–20 ($3.50–6). It's often better than the à la carte options.
- **Book fine dining 3–4 weeks ahead.** Central, Maido, and Kjolle book up fast. Astrid & Gastón is slightly easier. Don't arrive expecting to walk in.
- **Cash in markets, cards in restaurants.** Soles work everywhere, but upscale spots take Visa. Surquillo and street vendors are cash-only.
