Three days in Warsaw taught me this: Polish food is not delicate. It's built for survival — dense, soulful, and unapologetically heavy. Your job is to pace yourself, walk between meals, and surrender to the rhythm of a city that takes its eating seriously. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I arrived hungry.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Poles eat late by Central European standards — lunch around 1-2pm, dinner rarely before 7pm. Tipping runs 10-15% at sit-down restaurants; round up at casual spots. Cards work almost everywhere, but market vendors and milk bars often prefer cash (złoty, not euros). Book dinner reservations for weekend evenings, especially in the Old Town. And learn *dzień dobry* (good day) — it opens doors.

## Day 1: Old Town and the Heart of Traditional Polish Cooking

### Morning

Start in the Old Town with a breakfast that sets intentions. Find a corner table at a traditional cafe near the reconstructed market square — rebuilt brick by brick after the war, now humming with morning energy. Order *pierogi* stuffed with farmer's cheese and potato, pan-fried until the edges crisp and served with a crown of sour cream. Yes, dumplings for breakfast. In Warsaw, this is normal.

Pair it with a glass of kompot (stewed fruit drink) or strong black coffee. Sit with the view of pastel facades and let the carbs settle. This is not a city that rushes mornings.

**Cost: $8-12**

### Afternoon

Walk south through the Old Town toward the Vistula riverbank, letting your breakfast digest. By 1:30pm, find a *bar mleczny* — a milk bar, those subsidized cafeterias that survived communism and now serve pensioners and budget travelers alike. The fluorescent lighting is harsh, the trays are plastic, and the *bigos* (hunter's stew) is the best in the city.

This fermented cabbage and meat stew has been simmering since before you woke up. It tastes like woodsmoke and patience. Get it with a side of buckwheat groats and rye bread. The whole meal costs less than a cappuccino in the Old Town.

After lunch, walk to Hala Mirowska, the covered market operating since the 1800s, tucked in a neighborhood most tourists miss. Here, vendors sell smoked oscypek cheese from the mountains, jars of pickled everything, and fresh bread still warm. Buy a wedge of aged sheep's cheese and eat it on a bench outside. This is your afternoon snack.

**Cost: $6-10 (lunch) + $4-6 (market snacking)**

### Evening

Dinner belongs in the Old Town proper, at a restaurant where white tablecloths meet old recipes. Order *żurek* — sour rye soup served in a bread bowl, with a bobbing egg and chunks of white sausage. Follow it with duck leg roasted with apples, or go full commitment with roast pork knuckle, the skin shattered and golden. The portions are obscene. Share if you have any sense.

Walk back along the Royal Route after, past the Presidential Palace, letting Warsaw's evening light do its work.

**Cost: $35-55**

### Day 1 Total
**Budget: ~$55 | Mid-range: ~$75 | Splurge: ~$110**

## Day 2: Praga District — The Gritty Side of the River

### Morning

Cross the Vistula to Praga, the working-class neighborhood that avoided wartime destruction and now draws artists, bartenders, and anyone tired of the reconstructed prettiness across the river. The buildings are crumbling in the right way. The food is honest.

Find a corner bakery — every block has one — and get *pączki*, Polish doughnuts filled with rose hip jam, dusted with powdered sugar. They're denser than American doughnuts, more like fried brioche, and they'll fuel a full morning of walking. Add a glass of kefir if you're feeling local.

**Cost: $4-7**

### Afternoon

Lunch in Praga means street food eaten standing up. Look for a window selling *zapiekanka* — half a baguette loaded with sautéed mushrooms, cheese, and whatever else the vendor adds, then torched under a broiler until bubbling. It's Polish fast food, invented in the communist era, and the versions in Praga are messy and magnificent.

After, explore Bazar Różyckiego, the hidden flea market that's been operating since 1901. The food stalls here sell smoked fish, pickled herring, and homemade *kiełbasa* that vendors slice to order. Buy a few pieces of smoked mackerel and some dark bread. Find a bench. This is lunch part two.

If you want to get your hands dirty, book a pierogi-making class at one of Praga's small cooking schools — they run most afternoons and cost around $40-50. You'll roll dough, crimp edges, and eat your mistakes.

**Cost: $10-15 (street food) + $40-50 (cooking class, optional)**

### Evening

Praga's restaurant scene has exploded. Tonight, find a spot in a converted factory or courtyard — the neighborhood specializes in industrial spaces turned into something warmer. Order *kotlet schabowy*, the breaded pork cutlet that's Poland's answer to schnitzel. It should arrive golden, nearly the size of your plate, with a mound of sauerkraut and potatoes alongside.

End with *szarlotka*, apple cake served warm with a scoop of vanilla ice cream. The apples should be soft, the crumb topping thick.

**Cost: $30-50**

### Day 2 Total
**Budget: ~$50 | Mid-range: ~$80 | Splurge: ~$130**

## Day 3: The Waterfront and a Farewell Feast

### Morning

Your last Warsaw morning deserves a proper Polish breakfast — the full spread. Head to a cafe in the Business District or along the Waterfront, where modern Warsaw meets the river. Order *śniadanie polskie*: a plate of cold cuts, soft-boiled eggs, pickles, fresh tomatoes, radishes, and thick slices of sourdough bread. Add a bowl of white borscht if you didn't get enough sour soup this trip. Coffee is mandatory.

This is a meal for sitting, reading, watching the Vistula catch the morning light.

**Cost: $12-18**

### Afternoon

Walk the Waterfront promenade, then cut inland to the Arts Quarter around Ujazdów Castle. Stop for lunch at a restaurant doing modern Polish cooking — updated classics, local ingredients, smaller portions than the Old Town tourist traps. Order *tatar wołowy* (beef tartare, Polish-style, with pickled cucumber and egg yolk) if you trust raw meat. Follow it with pike-perch from the Masurian lakes, simply prepared with seasonal vegetables.

Before dinner, stop at a hidden cafe for *sernik* — Polish cheesecake, denser than New York-style, made with farmer's cheese, often with a whisper of lemon and a layer of raisins. Get it with black coffee. Write down the address because you'll want to remember this.

**Cost: $25-35 (lunch) + $5-8 (coffee and cake)**

### Evening

End in the City Center, at a restaurant that represents the best of where Warsaw is going. This is your splurge meal — multiple courses, paired wines if you're so inclined, service that anticipates without hovering. Order the tasting menu if it's offered: expect fermented and pickled vegetables, game meats, freshwater fish, horseradish in places you don't expect, and desserts built on apples, plums, and poppy seeds.

The check will sting. It's worth it. Warsaw's fine dining scene is underrated, running 30-40% cheaper than equivalent restaurants in Paris or London, and the young chefs here cook with something to prove.

Walk back to your hotel through the evening streets, past the Palace of Culture glowing Soviet-ugly against the sky, and let three days of eating settle into memory.

**Cost: $60-100**

### Day 3 Total
**Budget: ~$100 | Mid-range: ~$130 | Splurge: ~$180**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Milk bars close early** — most shut down by 6pm. Plan your cheap, excellent lunch before mid-afternoon.
- **Learn the bread code**: Żytni is rye, razowy is whole grain, biały is white. Point and smile at bakeries; they'll help you.
- **Sour is the flavor** — żurek, bigos, pickles, fermented cabbage. If you don't like sour, Warsaw will challenge you. Lean in.
- **Vodka comes in rounds** — if Polish friends invite you out, pace yourself. Say *na zdrowie* (to health), make eye contact, and don't sip. It's a shot or it's nothing.
