# Istanbul Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Istanbul

Three days. One city split across two continents. An unreasonable number of meals. Istanbul doesn't reward the casual eater—it rewards the obsessive, the person willing to ferry across the Bosphorus for a better lahmacun, to wake early for the right simit cart, to end each night with one more tea because refusing would be rude. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I started.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Turks eat breakfast late and dinner later—don't expect restaurant kitchens to be fully awake before 9am or winding down before 11pm. Tipping is 10-15% at sit-down restaurants; round up at casual spots. Cash remains king at traditional lokantas and street vendors, though cards work at modern establishments. Accept every offered tea—it's hospitality, not a sales tactic, and refusing reads as cold. No reservations needed except for high-end Bosphorus-view restaurants on weekends.

## Day 1: Sultanahmet — The Historic Peninsula

**Morning: The Simit Start**

Skip the hotel breakfast. By 8am, the red simit carts are already stationed near the Blue Mosque, and this sesame-crusted bread ring—crisp outside, chewy within—is how Istanbul has started its day for centuries. Find a cart on the streets leading toward Hagia Sophia and get your simit fresh, still warm. Pair it with a small cube of white cheese from any nearby bakkal (corner shop) and a tulip glass of strong çay from a tea vendor.

This isn't a meal that requires sitting down. Eat while walking through the quiet morning streets of Sultanahmet, before the tour buses arrive and the kebab touts start their shifts. The whole thing costs almost nothing—₺30-40 ($1-1.50)—and sets the tone for how Istanbul actually eats: standing, walking, the city itself as your dining room.

**Cost: $2-4**

**Afternoon: The Grand Bazaar Circuit**

By noon, make your way into the Grand Bazaar—not for shopping, but for eating. The 4,000 shops need feeding, and hidden among the carpet dealers and lamp sellers are some of the city's most honest lunch spots.

Find Havuzlu Restaurant, tucked in the bazaar's interior near Zenneciler Sokak. It's been serving traders since 1958, and the lamb şiş kebab here—chunks of meat charred over hardwood coals—arrives with grilled tomatoes, a pile of rice, and bread still puffing from the oven. This is workday food, meant to sustain you, and it does. Expect to pay ₺250-350 ($8-12) for a full plate with ayran (the salty yogurt drink that cuts through lamb fat perfectly).

After lunch, walk the Spice Bazaar at Eminönü. Don't buy the Turkish delight from the tourist stalls near the entrance—go deeper, where the lokum is sold by weight to locals. Taste before buying. The rose and pistachio varieties should be soft enough to leave a fingerprint but firm enough to hold their shape. Budget ₺100-150 ($3-5) for a box worth bringing home.

**Cost: $15-25**

**Evening: Balık Ekmek at Golden Hour**

As the sun drops toward the Golden Horn, make your way to the Galata Bridge. The boats moored at Eminönü have been serving balık ekmek—grilled fish sandwiches—since before the bridge was rebuilt, and while some dismiss them as tourist fare, they're wrong. The fish is grilled over open flame, tucked into half a loaf with raw onion, lettuce, and a squeeze of lemon. Eat it on the bridge's lower level, watching ferries cross the water, seagulls circling overhead.

At ₺100-150 ($3-5), this is one of the best meals-per-lira in the city. But dinner isn't over. Walk up through Karaköy to Karaköy Güllüoğlu, the baklava institution that has ruined all other baklava for me permanently. The pistachio version here—layers of phyllo so thin they shatter, nuts ground fine, syrup absorbed completely—costs ₺80-120 ($3-4) for a portion. Eat it at the counter with black tea. The staff will not rush you.

**Cost: $10-20**

**Day 1 Total: Budget ~$27 | Mid-range ~$49 | Splurge ~$100** (add a rooftop dinner overlooking the Blue Mosque for the splurge tier, ₺1,500-2,500/$50-80)

## Day 2: Kadıköy — The Asian Side

**Morning: Ferry and Feast**

Take the 9am ferry from Eminönü to Kadıköy. The 20-minute crossing is itself a meal—grab a glass of tea from the onboard vendor, find a seat on the upper deck, and watch the city's skyline recede behind you. This ferry ride, at ₺17 ($0.50) with an Istanbulkart, might be the best cheap experience in Istanbul.

Kadıköy's market district wakes up early, and you're here for kahvaltı—the elaborate Turkish breakfast spread that turns morning eating into a two-hour event. Head to Çiya Sofrası on Güneşlibahçe Sokak. Chef Musa Dağdeviren has spent decades collecting recipes from across Anatolia, and his breakfast spread reflects this obsession: thirty-plus small plates of cheeses, olives, clotted cream with honey, eggs cooked in butter, tomato-pepper spreads, and bread still warm. You'll pay ₺400-600 ($13-20) per person and leave unable to imagine lunch.

**Cost: $15-25**

**Afternoon: The Market Trail**

Kadıköy's food market is the reason to cross the Bosphorus. Unlike the tourist-oriented Spice Bazaar, this is where Istanbul's home cooks shop—fishmongers shouting prices, produce stacked in pyramids, pickle vendors offering tastes from enormous barrels.

Graze rather than feast. Get a dozen midye dolma (stuffed mussels) from a street vendor for ₺60-80 ($2-3)—each mussel is pried open and filled with spiced rice, eaten in one bite with a squeeze of lemon. Find a lahmacun stand and watch the thin rounds of dough get topped with minced lamb, herbs, and pepper before sliding into a wood-fired oven. At ₺40-60 ($1.50-2) each, you can eat two rolled up with parsley and onion. Get a glass of şalgam (fermented turnip juice, aggressively purple, weirdly addictive) to wash it down.

Before leaving Kadıköy, stop at the fish market stalls and pick up a portion of çiğ köfte—raw bulgur kneaded with tomato paste and spices, served wrapped in lettuce. It's traditionally made with raw meat, but the street version is vegan, punchy with heat, and costs ₺50-70 ($2).

**Cost: $10-18**

**Evening: The Real Kebab Education**

Return to the European side for dinner, but avoid Sultanahmet's tourist-priced grills. Instead, head to Zübeyir Ocakbaşı near Taksim, where businessmen and families crowd around a long metal grill watching their kebabs cook. This is Adana kebab territory—hand-minced lamb mixed with tail fat and red pepper, pressed onto wide skewers and charred over charcoal. Order a portion of Adana, a portion of the less-spicy Urfa kebab, and the full accompaniment: warm bread, grilled peppers, onion salads, and the tomato-based ezme that ties everything together.

With a cold Efes or ayran, dinner runs ₺450-700 ($15-23). This is where Istanbul's kebab tradition meets its peak—not the döner spinning on Istiklal Avenue, but the slow-cooked, hand-made tradition that traces back to southeastern Turkey.

**Cost: $20-35**

**Day 2 Total: Budget ~$45 | Mid-range ~$78 | Splurge ~$150** (add craft beers at Kadıköy's bars for the splurge tier)

## Day 3: Balat to Beşiktaş — The Farewell Feast

**Morning: Balat's New Guard**

The old Jewish quarter of Balat has become Istanbul's most photogenic neighborhood—colorful houses stacked down steep hills, antique shops beside third-wave coffee roasters. But you're here to eat. Start at Forno Balat, a small bakery where the açma (soft, butter-brushed rolls) and poğaça (savory filled pastries) emerge fresh throughout the morning. Get one of each, plus a strong Turkish coffee, and eat at the tiny counter or perched on the steps outside. ₺80-120 ($3-4).

Then walk downhill toward the Golden Horn, detouring through the backstreets where elderly residents still shop at Ottoman-era bakeries. This neighborhood eating isn't about famous restaurants—it's about stumbling into a place with three tables and a grandmother running the kitchen.

**Cost: $5-10**

**Afternoon: Beşiktaş Fish Market**

Take the tram and then walk to Beşiktaş, a working neighborhood where students and families share the streets. The fish market here is smaller than Kadıköy's but more local—fewer tourists, more aggressive pricing negotiations in Turkish.

For a late lunch, pick a meyhane (traditional tavern) near the market and settle in for meze. This is how Istanbul does lunch when there's time to spare: a parade of small plates ordered one by one. Start cold—haydari (thick yogurt with garlic and dill), patlıcan salatası (smoky eggplant salad), deniz börülcesi (sea beans in olive oil). Move to warm dishes—crispy fried calamari, grilled octopus, maybe some midye tava (fried mussels). You're not ordering a main course. You're building a meal from a dozen small bites, each plate ₺80-150 ($3-5).

Add a glass of rakı if you're ready for the anise hit, or stick with beer. Two hours later, you'll have spent ₺500-800 ($16-26) and eaten better than any single-entrée restaurant could deliver.

**Cost: $20-35**

**Evening: The Bosphorus Send-Off**

Your last Istanbul dinner should involve water. Head to Ortaköy, where the waterfront restaurants look across to the Asian side and the floodlit Bosphorus Bridge soars overhead. This is splurge territory—a proper sit-down dinner with white tablecloths and menus that list fish by the kilogram.

Order the catch of the day, grilled simply with lemon and olive oil. Add a full meze spread. Drink rakı properly—one part spirit to two parts water, turning the clear liquid cloudy white. Watch the boats pass. Remember every meal that led to this one.

A full dinner here runs ₺1,200-2,500 ($40-80), but this is the meal you'll describe to people back home. Save room for künefe for dessert—shredded pastry around melted cheese, soaked in syrup, served burning hot. It shouldn't work. It absolutely does.

**Cost: $50-100**

**Day 3 Total: Budget ~$75 | Mid-range ~$145 | Splurge ~$250**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The tea is always free with meals**—it arrives unbidden after kebabs and will keep arriving until you leave. Accepting is expected; ordering coffee instead is fine but marks you as foreign.

- **Lunch is the value meal.** Many restaurants that charge ₺600 for dinner offer fixed lunch menus for ₺200-300. Do your heavy eating midday.

- **Learn "hesap lütfen"** (check, please) and use it confidently. Servers won't bring the bill until asked—lingering is culturally expected, rushing you out is rude.

- **The best simit cart is the one near where you're staying at 7am.** Freshness matters more than location. If the sesame falls off when you bite, it's been sitting too long.
