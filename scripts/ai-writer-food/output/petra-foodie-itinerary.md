Three days in Petra taught me that the ancient Nabataeans knew something about hospitality that still pulses through every kitchen in Wadi Musa. I arrived thinking I'd spend my time staring at rose-red tombs — I left having eaten my weight in mansaf, discovered that the best knafeh requires a 6 AM wake-up call, and learned that refusing a Bedouin's tea is basically a diplomatic incident. This is the eating itinerary I wish someone had handed me at the Petra gate.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Jordanians eat lunch as the main meal, typically between 1–3 PM, with dinner often starting around 8 PM or later. Cash in Jordanian Dinar is essential — many smaller spots don't take cards, and you'll want small bills for tipping (10% at restaurants is standard, round up at casual spots). Booking ahead matters only for Petra Kitchen's cooking class; everywhere else, walk in. When a Bedouin offers you tea inside the archaeological site, accept it — hospitality is sacred here, and that glass of sweet sage-infused tea is part of the Petra experience.

## Day 1: Wadi Musa — Gateway to Everything

### Morning

Start at 6 AM. Not because I'm cruel, but because the bakeries in central Wadi Musa are pulling fresh bread from ovens and the town has a quiet energy that disappears by mid-morning when tourist buses arrive. Find a spot serving *fatayer* — flatbreads stuffed with za'atar, cheese, or spiced meat — alongside thick Arabic coffee with cardamom. The small bakeries near the main roundabout sell these warm from the oven for barely 0.50 JOD each. Order three varieties, add a side of labneh (strained yogurt) with olive oil, and you're fortified for the Siq walk ahead.

**Estimated cost: $5–$8**

### Afternoon

You'll spend the morning inside Petra, which means lunch happens at one of the Bedouin-run cafes scattered along the main trail. The Basin Restaurant near the Monastery trailhead serves decent food at inflated prices, but I'd rather you find one of the smaller family-run tea stops along the way. Near the Royal Tombs, several Bedouin families run cave-side operations where you can get simple plates of hummus, falafel, and bread alongside that ever-present sage tea. The **falafel** here — crispy, herb-flecked, served in fresh pita with pickles and tahini — costs around 2–3 JOD and tastes better than it has any right to given the setting.

After you exit Petra in the late afternoon, walk the main street of Wadi Musa and stop at one of the shawarma stands. The vertical spits of spiced meat have been rotating since morning, and by 4 PM the outer layers are perfectly caramelized. A shawarma wrap with garlic sauce and pickled turnips runs about 1.50 JOD — street food perfection after 20,000 steps.

**Estimated cost: $10–$15**

### Evening

Tonight is about **mansaf**, the Jordanian national dish, and you need to do this properly. Head to one of the family-style restaurants in upper Wadi Musa where locals actually eat — look for places with Arabic-only signs and large groups of Jordanian families. Mansaf arrives as a ceremonial mountain: tender lamb cooked in *jameed* (fermented dried yogurt reconstituted into a tangy sauce), served over rice and thin bread, topped with toasted almonds and pine nuts. Traditionally, you eat this with your right hand, forming small balls of rice and meat. The lamb should fall apart at the suggestion of a fork. A full mansaf serving costs 8–15 JOD depending on the restaurant, and you won't need dessert — you won't have room.

**Estimated cost: $25–$40**

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~$40 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$100

## Day 2: Little Petra and Back Trail Discovery

### Morning

Drive 15 minutes north to **Little Petra (Siq al-Barid)** before the day heats up. This miniature Nabataean site has painted ceilings, carved dining rooms, and almost no crowds — but more importantly, the Bedouin families here have been serving visitors for generations. Find the tea stand run by local women near the site entrance. They'll offer you sweet black tea infused with wild sage gathered from the surrounding hills, plus simple flatbreads cooked on a *saj* (domed griddle). This isn't a restaurant experience; it's sitting on cushions in the shade while someone's grandmother tells you about her grandfather who lived in these caves. The tea is technically free (hospitality culture), but leave 3–5 JOD — these families depend on tourism.

**Estimated cost: $5–$8**

### Afternoon

Back in Petra for the afternoon, take the **Wadi Farasa trail** — the alternative route past the Soldier's Tomb and garden ruins that most tourists skip. Along this path, you'll find some of the quietest Bedouin cave cafes, tucked into rock formations with views across the valley. Stop at one for a late lunch of *musakhan* if they're serving it — chicken roasted with sumac and onions over taboon bread, soaked in olive oil. Not every spot has it, but when you find it, the tangy-sweet-savory combination is worth the hunt. Otherwise, fresh-squeezed pomegranate juice (2 JOD) and more falafel will keep you moving.

In the late afternoon, return to Wadi Musa and sign up for the **Petra Kitchen** cooking class, which starts around 6 PM. This isn't a demo — you'll actually cook, chopping vegetables for mezze, rolling grape leaves, and learning to balance the spice mix for *maqluba*.

**Estimated cost: $15–$25 (excluding cooking class)**

### Evening

The cooking class doubles as dinner. Over three hours, you'll prepare and then feast on your own creations: **maqluba** (the dramatic upside-down rice and vegetable dish that gets flipped onto a platter like a cake), multiple salads, stuffed vegetables, and dessert. The class costs around 35 JOD including everything you eat, plus you leave knowing how to recreate Jordanian home cooking. This is the best food education money can buy in Petra.

**Estimated cost: $50 (cooking class) or $25–$40 (restaurant alternative)**

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~$70 | Mid ~$90 | Splurge ~$130

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

### Morning

Your last morning demands **knafeh**, and the best version I found came from a small sweet shop in lower Wadi Musa that starts pulling fresh trays around 7 AM. Knafeh is deceptively simple — shredded phyllo dough, soft white cheese, rose-scented syrup — but the texture contrast between crispy top and molten cheese only works when it's fresh. By afternoon, it's been sitting too long. Get there early, order a portion (2–3 JOD), and eat it standing at the counter with a small cup of Arabic coffee to cut the sweetness. This is breakfast, and it's perfect.

Follow it with a wander through the small produce market near the center of town. Vendors sell local olives, za'atar blends, dried fruits, and the fat Medjool dates that grow in the Jordan Valley. Buy a small bag of dates and a za'atar mix to take home — they'll cost almost nothing and remind you of this trip for months.

**Estimated cost: $8–$12**

### Afternoon

Spend your final afternoon inside Petra, and let lunch happen organically at one of the higher-elevation tea stops. The cafe near the High Place of Sacrifice serves surprisingly good *ful medames* (stewed fava beans with olive oil, lemon, and cumin) alongside bread and tea. Eating this simple, ancient dish while looking out over the Nabataean ruins below feels appropriate — you're having more or less the same meal travelers here have eaten for centuries.

Alternatively, pack a small picnic from the Wadi Musa markets: fresh bread, local cheese, olives, tomatoes. Eat it on the steps of the Monastery after climbing those 800 steps. You'll have earned it.

**Estimated cost: $8–$15**

### Evening

Your final dinner should be **zarb**, and this requires planning. Zarb is the Bedouin underground barbecue — lamb, chicken, and vegetables slow-cooked in a sand pit for hours, emerging impossibly tender and smoky. Several camps near Wadi Musa offer zarb dinners, often combined with traditional music and storytelling. The experience runs 25–50 JOD depending on the operator, and it's worth the higher end for smaller groups with better-quality meat. The lamb, specifically, should shred at the touch of a fork, infused with smoke and the earthy sweetness of the vegetables it cooked alongside.

This meal takes time. Clear your evening. The stars over the desert, the smoke rising from the sand pit, the generosity of the portions — this is how you say goodbye to Petra.

**Estimated cost: $35–$70**

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~$50 | Mid ~$80 | Splurge ~$130

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Carry small bills**: Bedouin tea stops inside Petra don't have change for large notes. Break your money at restaurants in Wadi Musa before entering the site.

- **Eat lunch late**: Most tourists eat at noon and clog the few restaurants inside Petra. Wait until 2 PM and you'll have cave cafes to yourself.

- **The cooking class books out**: Petra Kitchen is popular with tour groups. Email a day ahead if you're visiting during peak season (March–April or October).

- **Ask about the lamb**: For mansaf and zarb, ask if the lamb is local Jordanian. The imported stuff is fine, but local lamb — raised on the scrubby herbs of the desert — has a distinctive sweetness you won't find elsewhere.
