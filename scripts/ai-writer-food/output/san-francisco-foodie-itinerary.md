Three days in San Francisco taught me that this city eats differently than anywhere else in America. The sourdough is tangier, the burritos are bigger, and the crab tastes like the Pacific smells. I came with a list and left with a notebook full of crossed-out plans — because the best meals here aren't always where you expect them.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

San Francisco tips 18–20% at sit-down restaurants — service workers depend on it. Most places take cards, but carry cash for market vendors and dim sum carts. Dinner reservations matter on weekends; book popular spots 3–5 days ahead. Meals run late here — brunch crowds peak at 11am, dinner at 8pm. Dress in layers even for dinner; the fog doesn't care about your plans.

## Day 1: The Mission — Burritos, Markets, and the Heart of SF Food

### Morning: Start with the bread that built this city

Begin in the Ferry Building Marketplace, where Acme Bread has been pulling sourdough from ovens since 1983. Get there by 9am before the Saturday crowds descend. Order a levain loaf and a coffee from Blue Bottle (they started here, after all), then sit at the long communal tables watching ferries cross the bay.

The bread costs $7; the coffee runs $5. But the real move is walking the market stalls — Cowgirl Creamery for cheese samples, Boccalone for paper-thin salumi, Humphry Slocombe for ice cream flavors that shouldn't work but do (secret breakfast = bourbon and cornflakes). Budget an extra $15–20 for grazing.

*Morning spend: $25–40*

### Afternoon: The Mission burrito, done right

Take BART to 16th and Mission. This neighborhood has the city's best concentration of taquerias within six blocks, and you're here for one thing: the Mission burrito. It's not just a burrito — it's a foil-wrapped architecture project, steamed tortilla holding rice, beans, meat, salsa, and whatever else you choose.

La Taqueria on Mission Street has won awards and earned the hype. Order the carnitas burrito without rice (their move, trust it) for around $13. The meat is slow-cooked, the salsa has actual heat, and one burrito is legitimately two meals. Get it with everything.

Walk it off down to Balmy Alley, a narrow street absolutely covered in murals — political art, memorial portraits, abstract color explosions. It's free, it's uncrowded, and it pairs well with a full stomach and slow walking.

*Afternoon spend: $13–18*

### Evening: Where the Mission gets serious

For dinner, stay in the Mission but move upscale to Californios on 18th Street. This is modern Mexican tasting menu territory — around $275 per person for 12+ courses that treat Mexican cuisine with the same respect French food gets at three-star restaurants. Hoja santa leaves, obscure moles, Baja seafood. Book two weeks ahead minimum.

Not ready for that commitment? Lazy Bear offers a communal dinner experience in a converted warehouse for around $195 — like eating at a friend's house, if your friend employed world-class chefs. Or stay casual at Trick Dog, one of the country's best cocktail bars, where drinks run $16–18 and the food menu (Korean fried chicken, fancy hot dogs) satisfies without formality.

*Evening spend: $50–275*

**Day 1 Total: Budget ~$90 · Mid ~$170 · Splurge ~$340**

## Day 2: Chinatown and North Beach — Two Countries in Twelve Blocks

### Morning: Dim sum in the oldest Chinatown in America

San Francisco's Chinatown predates every other Chinatown in the country, and the dim sum here hasn't softened for tourists. Get to Good Mong Kok Bakery on Stockton Street by 8:30am for baked char siu bao (barbecue pork buns) straight from the oven — $2.50 for three, and worth every minute of the line.

For a proper sit-down dim sum experience, walk to City View Restaurant on Commercial Street. Carts roll past with har gow (shrimp dumplings), siu mai, and rice noodle rolls. Point at what looks good; that's the entire ordering system. Two people eat until uncomfortable for $35–45. Arrive before 10am or wait 45 minutes.

*Morning spend: $20–50*

### Afternoon: Walk to Italy through a park

Chinatown and North Beach share a border at Columbus Avenue — cross the street and the language on the signs switches from Cantonese to Italian. This is San Francisco's Little Italy, where Beat poets drank espresso and argued about art in the 1950s.

Lunch at Golden Boy Pizza on Green Street: square Sicilian slices, thick and oily in the best way, $4–6 each. The clam and garlic slice is the order. Get two and eat standing at the counter like everyone else.

After, walk to Liguria Bakery (arrive before 2pm or they're sold out). They make exactly one product — focaccia — in flavors like tomato, garlic, and raisin. $6 gets you a massive slab in wax paper. This is your afternoon snack, your hotel breakfast tomorrow, your reason to return.

*Afternoon spend: $15–25*

### Evening: Seafood stew and North Beach wine bars

Cioppino was invented in San Francisco by Italian fishermen who pooled their catch into one communal pot. The dish has become tourist bait at Fisherman's Wharf, but the real versions still exist. Sotto Mare on Green Street serves cioppino in cast iron — Dungeness crab, clams, mussels, prawns in tomato broth — for around $45. Order crusty bread on the side (sourdough, obviously) and don't wear white.

For a lighter evening, Tosca Cafe reopened a few years ago and serves approachable Italian-American cooking with serious cocktails. The chicken for two is a project worth sharing ($65). End the night at Vesuvio Cafe next to City Lights bookstore — a bar that's barely changed since Kerouac drank here. Beers run $8, and the upstairs window seats look down on Columbus Avenue like a time machine.

*Evening spend: $40–120*

**Day 2 Total: Budget ~$75 · Mid ~$140 · Splurge ~$195**

## Day 3: From the Wharf to the Finale — Best of Everything

### Morning: Irish coffee at the source

The Buena Vista Cafe at Fisherman's Wharf invented Irish coffee in America in 1952, and they've made over 40 million since. Go at 9am when it's just locals and insomniacs. Order the Irish coffee ($14) — hot coffee, Irish whiskey, sugar, cream floated on top — and eggs with sourdough toast ($16). Sit at the bar and watch them make a dozen at once; it's a choreography.

Yes, it's Fisherman's Wharf. Yes, the surrounding blocks are tourist chaos. The Buena Vista earns its exception.

*Morning spend: $25–35*

### Afternoon: Dungeness crab and market crawling

Dungeness crab season runs November through June, and if you're here during those months, you're eating crab. Walk along Jefferson Street to the outdoor crab stands (look for steam rising) and order half a cracked crab — around $25–30 — with sourdough bread. Eat standing at counters overlooking the water, throwing shells into paper boats.

Then head back to the Ferry Building for a final market run. Stock up on Recchiuti chocolates for the plane ($15–40 depending on your self-control), Dandelion Chocolate bars ($10 each, single-origin and worth it), and one last oyster from Hog Island Oyster Co. ($3–4 each at the raw bar, or $24 for a half dozen).

*Afternoon spend: $50–85*

### Evening: The meal to end it

For your final dinner, two paths. If you want the San Francisco restaurant experience that put the city on the food map, book Zuni Cafe in Hayes Valley. The roast chicken for two ($72) takes an hour to cook and comes with bread salad underneath — arguably the most influential single dish in California cuisine. Reserve a week ahead.

For something more current, Mister Jiu's in Chinatown serves Chinese-American food with California ingredients and technique. The smoked quail, the hot and sour soup with Dungeness crab, the cheung fun noodles — this is what happens when a chef respects tradition and refuses to be bound by it. Tasting menu runs $150; à la carte lets you spend $60–100.

*Evening spend: $65–175*

**Day 3 Total: Budget ~$140 · Mid ~$200 · Splurge ~$295**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Reservation timing matters**: OpenTable releases tables 30 days out at midnight; book Californios, State Bird Provisions, and similar spots exactly then.
- **The fog affects your appetite**: Mornings start cold and gray; eat something warm before heading out. Afternoons warm up; plan your lighter meals accordingly.
- **Skip Pier 39, not Fisherman's Wharf entirely**: The tourist strip is avoidable, but the crab stands and Buena Vista earn their spots.
- **Bring your own bags to the Ferry Building**: Vendors charge for bags, and you'll accumulate more food than you planned.
