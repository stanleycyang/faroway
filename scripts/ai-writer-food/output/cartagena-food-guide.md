The steam rises from a freshly fried arepa de huevo at seven in the morning, the corn shell crackling as a woman in a bright yellow headwrap splits it open to reveal a perfectly runny egg inside. Behind her, a cart rattles past with coconut shavings piled high, and somewhere down the cobblestone street, a radio plays champeta while the smell of coffee and salt air mingles in the heat. This is breakfast in Cartagena, and you're already behind schedule.

## Why Cartagena Eats Different

Cartagena's food tells the story of a port city that never stopped receiving visitors—and never stopped feeding them. African, Indigenous, Spanish, and Caribbean influences collide in every bite, creating a cuisine that leans heavily on coconut, plantain, seafood, and corn. The African influence is particularly strong, brought by enslaved people who became the backbone of coastal Colombian cooking. You'll taste it in the rich coconut rice, the fried everything, the bold use of tropical fruit in savory dishes.

But what makes eating here genuinely different is the street culture. Unlike many Latin American cities where street food takes a backseat to restaurants, Cartagena's best meals often come from carts, baskets balanced on heads, and plastic chairs set up on sidewalks. The palenqueras—Afro-Colombian women in explosively colorful traditional dress—aren't just selling fruit and cocadas; they're carrying on a culinary tradition that predates the colonial walls surrounding them. You can spend $200 on a rooftop dinner, and you should, but you'll find yourself thinking about that $2 ceviche from a cart in Getsemaní three months later.

## The Essential Bites

**Arepas de Huevo** deserve your first morning in the city. These fried corn patties come stuffed with a whole egg, the exterior golden and crispy, the interior soft with that just-cooked yolk that runs when you bite in. Find them at breakfast carts throughout the Old Town, usually for around $1.50 each. The version with ground beef added costs a bit more but earns it. Eat them standing up, napkin in hand, before the midday heat makes fried food feel like punishment.

**Ceviche de Camarón** here is nothing like its Peruvian cousin. Cartagena's version adds coconut milk to the lime-cured shrimp, creating something creamier, sweeter, and deeply tropical. Street vendors throughout Getsemaní serve it in small plastic cups for $3-4, usually with saltine crackers on the side. The best carts have lines by noon.

**Patacones** are fried green plantain discs, smashed flat and re-fried until they're crispy outside and starchy within. They arrive topped with hogao—a tomato and onion sauce cooked down until jammy—and sometimes cheese, shredded chicken, or more ceviche. Think of them as Cartagena's answer to chips and dip, except more filling and served everywhere from beach shacks to white-tablecloth restaurants.

**Bandeja Paisa** technically comes from Medellín, but you'll find this gut-busting platter throughout Colombia, and it's worth ordering at least once. Rice, red beans, ground beef, chicharrón (fried pork belly), chorizo, fried egg, plantain, avocado, and arepa—all on one plate. It's a hangover cure, a celebration meal, and a challenge all at once. Budget restaurants in San Diego serve versions for under $8 that could feed two reasonable humans.

**Cocadas** are the coconut candies sold by palenqueras walking through every plaza and beach. They come in dozens of flavors—plain coconut, tamarind, passion fruit, arequipe (dulce de leche)—and cost almost nothing. Buy them directly from the women carrying bowls on their heads; it's part of the experience, and they'll usually let you sample first.

**Fresh Fruit** deserves its own category. Market stalls and cart vendors sell fruits you've likely never seen: lulo tastes like citrusy rhubarb, maracuyá (passion fruit) is aggressively tart and perfect, guanábana (soursop) is creamy and strange in the best way. Order a fresh juice at any cafe and point at the fruit you can't identify—it's never a bad decision.

## Street Food: Where the City Really Eats

The street food pulse beats strongest in two places: Getsemaní in the evenings and Mercado Bazurto any morning you're feeling brave. Getsemaní's Plaza de la Trinidad transforms after sunset into an open-air food court of sorts, with vendors selling ceviche, grilled meats, arepas, and cold beer. Plastic tables appear from nowhere. The vibe is local, the prices are honest, and the people-watching is excellent.

Mercado Bazurto is different. This is the real market, with no tourist polish whatsoever—chaotic aisles of fish, fruit, vegetables, and prepared food stalls serving the city's workers. It's loud, it's hot, it's overwhelming, and the sancocho (hearty soup) here costs a third of what you'd pay inside the walls. Go before 10 AM, don't bring anything you'd miss if someone grabbed it, and eat whatever the longest line is serving.

For breakfast street food, walk the Old Town between 7-9 AM when carts set up along the plazas. Look for buñuelos (fried cheese bread), empanadas, and that essential arepa de huevo.

## Where to Eat: Budget to Blowout

**Under $25:** The walled city and Getsemaní are full of cafeterias and local spots where almuerzo corriente—the set lunch of soup, main plate, drink, and sometimes dessert—runs $5-8. This is how Cartageneros actually eat during the day. La Cocina de Pepina in Getsemaní has been serving massive portions of traditional coastal food for decades. In San Diego, small restaurants along the quieter streets offer bandeja paisa and fried fish plates that deliver maximum food for minimum spend. For the best value, follow construction workers at lunchtime; they know where the portions are real.

**$38-$56:** This mid-range gets you into proper restaurants with air conditioning, tablecloths, and menus that balance traditional dishes with contemporary presentation. Look for restaurants around Plaza Santo Domingo in the Old Town that serve high-quality seafood—whole fried snapper, coconut shrimp, arroz con mariscos (seafood rice)—in settings that feel like a real dinner out. Getsemaní's growing restaurant scene offers this tier with more personality and fewer tourists; the grilled octopus and ceviche at the better spots here compete with anything in Centro.

**$70+:** Splurge money in Cartagena goes far. Rooftop restaurants overlooking the illuminated domes of the Old Town serve sophisticated takes on coastal cuisine—deconstructed ceviches, pork belly with tamarind glaze, coconut desserts that belong in magazines. You're paying for the setting as much as the food, and on a warm evening with the church bells ringing below, it's worth every peso. Save these for your last night when you've calibrated your palate with street food and want to see what chefs do with the same ingredients.

## The Vegetarian Reality

Cartagena is not a vegetarian city. Meat and seafood dominate, and "vegetarian" often means "fish is okay, right?" That said, you won't starve. Patacones are naturally vegetarian when topped with cheese and hogao instead of meat. Arepas de huevo are vegetarian-friendly. Rice and beans appear at most meals. Fresh fruit juices are everywhere, and fruit salads from market vendors make excellent snacks.

Specifically vegetarian restaurants exist but mostly cater to tourists and health-conscious locals—look for them in the walled city and Getsemaní. Ask for things "sin carne, sin pollo, sin pescado" (without meat, chicken, or fish) and be specific. Coconut rice, fried plantains, and ensalada can make a meal if you adjust expectations.

## Eating Your Way Through the Neighborhoods

**Old Town (Centro)** has the widest range—tourist restaurants around the major plazas, hidden local spots on quieter streets, upscale dining on rooftops. Eat breakfast here, splurge on dinner, but lunch prices can run high. The plaza cafes are better for watching than eating.

**Getsemaní** is where serious eating happens. Street food at night, small restaurants by day, and an increasingly vibrant cafe scene for afternoon coffee and pastries. The street art makes walking between meals its own entertainment. This is the neighborhood that rewards wandering.

**San Diego** is the quiet corner of the walled city where restaurants feel less performative. Working-class lunch spots serve massive plates to actual residents. Come here for midday meals away from the Centro crowd.

**Bocagrande** has ceviche carts along the beach and hotel restaurants that are fine but forgettable. The one reason to eat here: beachside fish with sand between your toes and cold Club Colombia beer.

## How to Eat Like You Belong

Greet every restaurant and shop with "buenas tardes" or "buenos días"—it matters more than you'd think. Lunch is the big meal, typically 12-2 PM, and many smaller restaurants close between lunch and dinner. Dinner starts late, rarely before 8 PM for locals.

Tipping 10 percent at sit-down restaurants is standard and appreciated but not always expected at casual spots. Service is often included as "propina" or "servicio" on the bill—check before doubling. At street carts, no tip is expected.

When palenqueras approach with fruit or cocadas, buying something is more respectful than photographing and walking away. Negotiate gently on prices, but remember—these women are maintaining a cultural tradition while making a living in a city that hasn't always treated them fairly. A few thousand extra pesos matters to them more than you.
