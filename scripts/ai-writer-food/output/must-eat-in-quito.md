The steam rises from the bowl at 6:47 AM, curling into the cold highland air of Quito's Mercado Central. I'm hunched over a worn wooden counter, spoon in hand, facing a bowl of *locro de papa* so thick the spoon stands upright. The first bite—creamy potato, sharp cheese, a whisper of annatto—warms me from throat to stomach in seconds. Around me, market vendors in wool sweaters are doing the same thing they've done for generations: starting their day with soup. This is breakfast in Quito. This is how you eat at 2,850 meters above sea level.

I spent three weeks eating my way through Ecuador's capital, from the colonial streets of the Historic Center to the residential kitchens of the north, from dawn market stalls to midnight food carts. What I found was a cuisine shaped by altitude, by Indigenous Andean roots, by Spanish collision, and by the practical genius of people who learned to cook with what the mountains provided. Here's what you need to eat.

---

#### Locro de Papa

This soup is Quito's soul in liquid form. A thick, golden-white porridge of potatoes—three or four varieties mashed together into silk—seasoned with cumin, garlic, and *achiote* (annatto seed that stains everything sunset orange). The surface shimmers with a slick of butter, and somewhere in there, cubes of fresh *queso fresco* are melting into soft submission. You eat it topped with half an avocado, the cool green fat cutting through all that starchy richness.

Locro de papa exists because potatoes are everything here. Ecuador grows over 400 varieties, and the Andes invented potato cultivation thousands of years ago. This dish is pure geography: high altitude, cold mornings, and root vegetables that store well.

Find it at any market food hall before 10 AM—the older the vendor, the better the recipe. *Mercado San Roque* does a particularly punishing version that will keep you full until dinner. Expect to pay $2.50–$3.50 for a bowl that could anchor a small boat.

---

#### Hornado

The pig arrives whole, or close to it. Skin lacquered and crackling, meat falling apart at the suggestion of a fork, fat rendered down to something approaching butter. *Hornado* is Quito's roast pork—slow-cooked for hours in wood-fired ovens until the flesh takes on a sweetness that borders on caramel. It's served with *mote* (giant hominy corn), *llapingachos* (potato patties), and a tangle of pickled onions sharp enough to cut through all that richness.

This is celebration food, originally reserved for festivals and family gatherings. Spanish colonizers brought the pigs; Indigenous cooks perfected what to do with them. The result is neither European nor pre-Columbian—it's purely Ecuadorian.

The Sunday market at *Sangolquí*, about 30 minutes outside the city center, is hornado paradise. Entire families gather at long tables, pulling pork with their hands. But you can find excellent versions in Quito's Old Town markets any day of the week. A heaping plate runs $4–$6.

---

#### Ceviche de Camarón

Wait—ceviche in the mountains? Quito sits in the Andes, hours from any coast, but Ecuadorians have never let geography stop them from eating shrimp. The city's ceviche is its own beast: pink-orange from tomato, swimming in a tangy broth spiked with lime and orange juice, loaded with fat Pacific shrimp that arrived by truck this morning.

Unlike Peruvian ceviche, the shrimp here are cooked before marinating—a concession to transport times, perhaps, but also a preference. The texture is tender, not raw-firm. You eat it with *canguil* (popped corn) scattered on top, *chifles* (fried plantain chips) on the side, and *ají* hot sauce bleeding into the broth. The combination of crunch, acid, sweetness, and slow-building heat is relentlessly satisfying.

Look for ceviches in dedicated *cevicherías*, usually open only for lunch (this is a daytime food, period). The neighborhood of *La Floresta* has several excellent spots. Budget $5–$8 for a portion sized for sharing, or not sharing.

---

#### Encebollado

If hornado is celebration, *encebollado* is resurrection. This fish soup is Quito's legendary hangover cure—a sour, briny, onion-loaded bowl of tuna poached in a tomato-citrus broth with yuca chunks softening at the bottom. The onions are quick-pickled, still crunchy, adding brightness to every spoonful. Squeeze lime over the top. Add *ají*. Dunk bread.

The dish originates from the coast but has been fully adopted by Quito's late-night crowds. There's science behind its reputation: the acidity, the protein, the carbs, the salt—it's a perfect storm of recovery.

Find it in 24-hour spots along *Avenida Amazonas* or in the food courts of *Mercado Santa Clara*. Some places specialize exclusively in encebollado and nothing else—those are the ones to trust. A bowl costs $3–$5 and might save your life at 3 AM.

---

#### Llapingachos

These potato patties are simple until they're not. Mashed potato, formed into thick cakes, stuffed with cheese, pan-fried in lard until the exterior crisps into a golden shell while the inside stays molten. They're served with *salsa de maní*—a peanut sauce thick as velvet, sweet and savory, that might be the most underrated condiment in South America.

Llapingachos are rarely eaten alone. They appear as sidekicks to hornado, to fried eggs, to *fritada* (fried pork). But their quiet excellence is undeniable. The Kichwa name translates roughly to "something squished," which undersells the craftsmanship involved.

Street vendors along *Calle García Moreno* make them fresh on *plancha* grills. You can watch the cheese string out as they flip. Two or three llapingachos with sauce run about $1.50–$2.

---

#### Fritada

If hornado is the slow seduction, fritada is the ambush. Chunks of pork—shoulder, belly, everything—braised in their own fat with water, cumin, and garlic until the liquid evaporates and the meat starts to fry in the rendered lard. The result is pork that's simultaneously tender inside and crackling outside, served in a heap with mote, llapingachos, and *tostado* (crunchy roasted corn).

Fritada represents the beautiful efficiency of Andean cooking: one cut, one pot, maximum extraction of flavor. No waste. No fuss. Pure satisfaction.

The town of *Machachi*, an hour south, claims fritada as its birthright, but Quito's *Mercado San Roque* gives fierce competition. Expect to pay $4–$6 for a plate that requires no other meals that day.

---

#### Empanadas de Viento

"Empanadas of the wind"—they're called that because they puff up light as air when fried, the dough ballooning into golden pillows with nearly hollow centers. Inside: just a whisper of fresh cheese that stretches when you pull the empanada apart. They're dusted with sugar immediately after frying, so you get that sweet-savory collision that haunts your cravings.

These are afternoon food, sold by street vendors outside schools and churches, eaten standing up, grease dripping down your wrist. They exist because Spanish colonial bakers met Ecuadorian cheese culture, and everyone agreed this was a good idea.

Find them anywhere you see a cart with a *paila* (shallow frying pan) and a line of teenagers. $0.50–$1 each. Buy three—you'll want three.

---

#### Canelazo

Night falls, the mountain air turns sharp, and Quito drinks *canelazo*. This hot cocktail is simple: *aguardiente* (sugarcane liquor), cinnamon, naranjilla fruit (a citrus-tomato hybrid native to Ecuador), and sugar, simmered together until fragrant. It arrives in a small glass, steaming, and it tastes like falling asleep next to a fire.

Canelazo is festival fuel—originally served during *fiestas* when nights were long and cold. It's also Plaza Foch on a Friday night, passed between friends on outdoor patios.

Any bar in *La Mariscal* serves it after sunset. A glass costs $2–$3. Two glasses, and you'll understand why Quiteños don't fear the cold.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Timing is everything. Breakfast soups are served before 10 AM—markets open at 6, and the serious eaters arrive by 7. Lunch is the main event, served between 1 and 3 PM; look for *almuerzos* (set menus) at family restaurants for $3–$4 that include soup, main course, juice, and sometimes dessert. Dinner is lighter, often just empanadas or sandwiches.

Don't be afraid of markets—*Mercado Central*, *San Roque*, *Santa Clara*—these are where Quiteños eat, not tourist traps. Point at what looks good if your Spanish falters. Share tables with strangers. Ask the vendor what's best today; they'll tell you.

And always, always say "gracias" with eye contact. Food here is personal. They cooked it this morning. They want to know you enjoyed it.
