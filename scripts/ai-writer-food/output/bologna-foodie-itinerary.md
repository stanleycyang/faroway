Three days in Bologna is barely enough time to scratch the surface of Italy's greatest food city, but it's enough to understand why Italians call it "La Grassa"—the fat one. This isn't a place for restraint. This is where tortellini was invented, where mortadella reaches its highest form, and where a proper ragù simmers for hours until it coats fresh tagliatelle like velvet. I spent 72 hours eating with single-minded purpose, and what follows is everything I wish someone had told me before I arrived.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Lunch runs 12:30–2:30pm; dinner rarely starts before 7:30pm. Many trattorias close between services, so don't show up at 4pm expecting food. Cash is still preferred at markets and older establishments, though cards work at most sit-down restaurants. Tipping isn't expected but rounding up is appreciated. Book dinner reservations at least a day ahead for popular spots—Bologna is a university city, and locals know where to eat. Dress comfortably but neatly; Bolognese take their tables seriously.

## Day 1: The Historic Center and Market Education

### Morning

Start in the City Center with coffee and pastry at a bar—not a café, a *bar*, which is what Italians call the places where locals stand at the counter and drink espresso in three sips. Order a caffè (espresso) and a brioche, or better yet, a *raviola*, a local pastry filled with mostarda or jam. The ritual matters: stand at the counter, don't sit (sitting doubles the price), and watch how the regulars do it. You'll spend maybe €3–4, and you'll be properly awake for what comes next.

**Cost: $4–$6**

### Afternoon

Walk to Mercato di Mezzo in the Old Town, tucked inside the ancient market streets near Piazza Maggiore. This isn't a tourist market—it's where Bologna feeds itself. The ground floor has vendors selling fresh pasta, wheels of Parmigiano-Reggiano aged 24, 36, even 48 months, and mortadella sliced so thin you can see light through it.

For lunch, grab a *tigelle* sandwich from one of the stalls—these are small, round flatbreads split and stuffed with mortadella, squacquerone cheese, and maybe some rocket. It's simple, perfect, and costs around €7–8. Eat it standing at the counter or perched on a stool overlooking the market chaos.

After, walk the porticoes to Via Pescherie Vecchie, the old fishmonger's street now lined with food shops. Stop at a *salumeria* and taste mortadella IGP—the real thing, made here, studded with pistachios and fat that melts on your tongue. Buy 100 grams (about €3) and eat it with bread while you walk.

**Cost: $15–$22**

### Evening

Dinner in the Arts Quarter calls for your first proper plate of tagliatelle al ragù. Not "bolognese"—that word doesn't exist here. Just *ragù*, a meat sauce slow-cooked with beef, pork, soffritto, tomato paste, wine, and milk until it becomes something almost spreadable.

A traditional trattoria will serve it on fresh egg tagliatelle, cut to the width of the Portico di San Luca's columns (seriously, there's a specification). Order an antipasto of mixed salumi first, then the tagliatelle, and finish with a glass of Pignoletto, the local sparkling white. The meal with wine runs €35–55, depending on the restaurant.

**Cost: $45–$70**

**Budget ~$65 | Mid-range ~$85 | Splurge ~$100**

## Day 2: The Old Town Deep Dive and Tortellini Obsession

### Morning

Day two begins with a proper Bolognese breakfast: more coffee, but this time paired with a *certosino*, a dense spiced fruit cake that's traditionally Christmas fare but available year-round in bakeries that know what they're doing. Find a *pasticceria* in the Old Town rather than a tourist bar—look for places where elderly Bolognese are reading newspapers. Espresso and pastry, €4–5.

**Cost: $5–$7**

### Afternoon

Today is about tortellini, and to understand them properly, you need to make them. Several cooking schools in the City Center offer half-day classes where you'll learn to make fresh pasta dough, roll it thin enough to read a newspaper through, and fold tortellini using the traditional technique—allegedly inspired by Venus's navel, though every nonna tells the story differently.

A hands-on class runs €60–90 and includes lunch: the tortellini you made, served *in brodo* (in capon broth), the way God intended. This isn't soup with pasta floating in it. This is broth as a delivery mechanism for little pockets of pork, prosciutto, mortadella, and Parmigiano, each one a single bite of concentrated Bologna.

If cooking classes aren't your thing, seek out a neighborhood trattoria away from Piazza Maggiore for tortellini in brodo. The further from the tourist center, the better the broth-to-pasta ratio tends to be. Expect €12–16 for a proper bowl.

**Cost: $65–$100** (with class) **or $18–$25** (lunch only)

### Evening

Tonight, venture to the Business District—less charming than the centro storico but where Bolognese professionals eat when they want quality without tourist markup. This is where to try *cotoletta alla bolognese*, a breaded veal cutlet topped with prosciutto and melted Parmigiano, sometimes finished with truffle shavings and broth.

It's indulgent, unapologetically rich, and absolutely correct. Pair it with a side of sautéed greens to maintain the illusion of balance, and drink Sangiovese from the Colli Bolognesi. Dinner here runs €40–60 with wine.

**Cost: $50–$75**

**Budget ~$75 | Mid-range ~$140 | Splurge ~$185**

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

### Morning

Your final morning deserves ritual. Return to the City Center and find a bar with outdoor seating under the porticoes—Bologna has 40 kilometers of covered walkways, and there's something about drinking coffee beneath medieval arches that makes everything taste better.

Order a *caffè macchiato* (espresso with a drop of milk) and a *torta di riso*, a traditional rice cake made with almonds and a hint of lemon that's been a Bolognese specialty for centuries. Sit this time—you've earned it. €5–6.

**Cost: $6–$8**

### Afternoon

Spend your last afternoon grazing through the Old Town's back streets. This is when to find that hidden café you'd have missed without wandering—a tiny place selling *crescentine*, fried bread dough served with prosciutto and soft cheese, or *gramigna con salsiccia*, short curly pasta with crumbled sausage and cream.

Stop at a *friggitoria* for street food: fried polenta, fried vegetables, fried custard cream if you're lucky. Nothing costs more than a few euros. Buy a small wheel of Parmigiano-Reggiano vacuum-sealed for the journey home (€15–25 for a kilo). Grab a final slice of mortadella for the road.

This afternoon is about accumulation—small bites from multiple sources, the kind of eating that only works when you've spent two days calibrating your stomach.

**Cost: $25–$40**

### Evening

End in the Old Town with the meal worth booking ahead for. This is the night for *lasagna verde alla bolognese*—green lasagna, layered with spinach pasta, ragù, béchamel, and Parmigiano, baked until the edges crisp and the center melts into itself.

Order it as a primo, then follow with *bollito misto*, a mixed boiled meat platter that sounds unglamorous until it arrives: tender beef, cotechino sausage, chicken, tongue, all served with salsa verde and mostarda. This is Emilian soul food, the kind of cooking that doesn't need to impress because it already knows it's perfect.

Finish with *zuppa inglese*, a trifle-like dessert of alchermes-soaked sponge cake and custard, and a glass of *Amaro del Capo* to settle everything. The bill will be €55–80 with wine, and you'll walk home under the porticoes knowing you did Bologna right.

**Cost: $70–$100**

**Budget ~$100 | Mid-range ~$130 | Splurge ~$150**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Order pasta as a primo, not a main.** Bolognese meals have structure: antipasto, primo (pasta), secondo (meat), contorno (vegetables), dolce. You don't have to order everything, but don't make pasta your entire meal—you'll miss the rhythm and fill up before the good stuff arrives.

- **Learn the phrase "vorrei assaggiare"** (I'd like to taste). Salumerie and cheese shops expect you to try before you buy, and asking makes you look like someone who knows what they're doing.

- **Avoid any restaurant with photos on the menu in Piazza Maggiore.** The square is magnificent; the restaurants facing it are not. Walk two blocks in any direction and prices drop while quality rises.

- **Mortadella sandwiches are better at 11am than at noon.** Arrive early at market stalls for fresher slices and shorter lines.
