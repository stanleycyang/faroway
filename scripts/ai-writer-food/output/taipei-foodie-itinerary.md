# Taipei Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Taipei

Three days in Taipei means roughly nine meals, plus the snacking that happens between them — and in a city where night markets run past midnight and breakfast shops open at dawn, "between meals" is where some of the best eating happens. This itinerary is built for serious hunger: geographically logical routes that minimize backtracking, specific dishes worth crossing town for, and the kind of practical details I wish someone had handed me before my first bowl of beef noodle soup went cold while I fumbled for cash.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Most restaurants are cash-only, especially in markets and older neighborhoods — keep NT$500–1,000 on you at all times. Tipping isn't expected anywhere. Lunch runs 11:30 AM–1:30 PM, dinner starts around 6 PM, and night markets hit their stride by 7 PM. Reservations aren't needed except for high-end spots. Get your EasyCard loaded immediately — it works for transit, convenience stores, and some market vendors. And queue politely; Taiwanese lines are sacred.

## Day 1: Da'an District — The Yongkang Street Classics

**Morning: The Breakfast That Launched a Thousand Queues**

Start in Da'an at one of the traditional breakfast shops clustered near Yongkang Street. You want *dan bing* — a thin egg crepe rolled with scallions, sometimes stuffed with cheese or ham — alongside fresh warm soy milk, either sweet or savory (the savory version comes with dried shrimp, pickled vegetables, and a splash of vinegar that curdles the milk into something closer to warm tofu). This is what Taipei office workers eat standing up before work, and it costs almost nothing.

Find a shop with a line of locals and limited seating — that's your quality indicator. The whole meal runs NT$80–120 (about $2.50–4), and you'll be fueled for hours of walking.

*Cost: $3–5*

**Afternoon: Soup Dumplings and the Art of the Slow Lunch**

By noon, you're ready for xiao long bao — those pleated soup dumplings with impossibly thin skins holding a mouthful of hot pork broth. The famous spot is Din Tai Fung (there's a flagship in the Da'an area near Dongmen MRT), but the lesser-known Hangzhou Xiaolong Tangbao on Hangzhou South Road serves versions just as delicate without the tourist crowd.

Order the classic pork xiao long bao, a plate of cucumber salad dressed in garlic and sesame, and *hong you chao shou* — wontons swimming in chili oil. Eat slowly. Bite a small hole in the dumpling, let steam escape, slurp the soup, then finish the rest. Rush this and you'll burn your mouth and miss the point.

After lunch, walk off the dumplings along Yongkang Street itself — duck into tea shops for samples, grab a *cong zhua bing* (scallion pancake) from a street vendor if you have any room left, and note the design boutiques for later non-food shopping.

*Lunch cost: $15–25*

**Evening: The Beef Noodle Soup That Defines the City**

Dinner is beef noodle soup — the dish Taipei claims as its own, with annual competitions to crown the city's best. Head to the original Lin Dong Fang near Zhongxiao Fuxing MRT station, where they've been serving the same deeply spiced braised beef broth since 1976. The meat falls apart, the hand-pulled noodles have proper chew, and the broth has layers — star anise, soy, something almost chocolatey from hours of reduction.

Order the half-tendon, half-meat version for textural variety. Add a side of pickled vegetables to cut the richness. This is a NT$200–280 bowl ($6–9), and it's worth every dollar.

Walk it off afterward with a slow stroll through the Da'an neighborhood, letting the evening cool and your stomach settle.

*Dinner cost: $8–15*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$30 | Mid ~$50 | Splurge ~$80

## Day 2: Dadaocheng and Zhongshan — History, Markets, and the Old Port

**Morning: Traditional Breakfast in a Trading Port**

Take the MRT to Dadaocheng, Taipei's oldest trading neighborhood, where merchants have sold dried goods, tea, and traditional medicines since the 1850s. Breakfast here feels different — slower, more deliberate. Find a traditional Taiwanese breakfast shop serving *fan tuan* (sticky rice rolls wrapped around fried dough, dried pork floss, and pickled vegetables) or *luo bo gao* (pan-fried radish cake with sweet soy paste).

Walk through Dihua Street afterward, even before the shops fully open. The dried goods stores — mushrooms, scallops, herbs — smell like history. This is where Taipei grandmothers still shop for wedding ingredients and Lunar New Year supplies.

*Cost: $3–5*

**Afternoon: Market Grazing and Gua Bao**

Lunch is intentionally loose today — this is your afternoon for market eating. Head to the Dadaocheng Wharf area first for a walk along the Tamsui River (locals cycle here at sunset; midday it's quiet and reflective). Then circle back through the neighborhood's scattered food vendors.

Your mission: find exceptional *gua bao*, the Taiwanese "hamburger" — a steamed bun cradling braised pork belly, pickled mustard greens, cilantro, and crushed peanuts. The best versions have belly that's been braised until it threatens to dissolve, with fat that melts into the soft bun. You'll find vendors in the market areas and side streets near Dihua Street.

Add to your grazing: *aiyu jelly* (a refreshing fig seed jelly with lime and honey), a cup of traditional *dong ding oolong* from one of the tea merchants who'll let you sample, and — if you're still hungry — a bowl of *ba wan* (gelatinous dumplings filled with pork and bamboo shoots in a sweet and savory sauce).

*Afternoon grazing cost: $12–20*

**Evening: Modern Taiwanese in Zhongshan**

Take the MRT down to Zhongshan for dinner. This neighborhood feels more contemporary — boutique hotels, underground book streets, cafes that take their coffee seriously. For dinner, look for restaurants doing modern interpretations of Taiwanese classics. You want a place that still serves fundamentally traditional flavors but with sharper technique.

Order: *three cup chicken* (san bei ji), braised in sesame oil, soy, and rice wine with Thai basil and a clay pot presentation; clams with rice wine and ginger; flash-fried water spinach with garlic. Share plates with whoever you're traveling with. End with *pineapple cake* from a bakery on your walk back — not as a souvenir, but as dessert, still slightly warm if you're lucky.

*Dinner cost: $25–40*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$70 | Splurge ~$120

## Day 3: Night Markets and Farewell Feasting

**Morning: The Rice Porridge Reset**

After two days of heavy eating, start gently with *xian zhou* — savory rice porridge served with an array of small dishes: braised tofu, pickled bamboo, salted fish, fermented bean curd, stir-fried greens. This is traditional Taiwanese breakfast comfort, and it's how locals eat when they want something restorative.

Look for a porridge shop near Zhongshan or head to Ximending if you want to end your trip in the pedestrian shopping district's morning calm (before the afternoon crowds arrive). The meal is cheap — NT$100–150 ($3–5) for porridge and several sides.

*Cost: $4–6*

**Afternoon: Oyster Omelettes and Night Market Prep**

Late lunch should position you near Raohe Street Night Market, which opens around 5 PM. Arrive early, around 4:30 PM, to eat the famous oyster omelette (*o ah jian*) from the stall that's been there for decades — you'll know it by the crowd forming before the market officially opens.

The dish is strange and perfect: small oysters, egg, and a chewy sweet potato starch batter, pan-fried and topped with a sweet-savory red sauce. The texture is gooey in ways that Western palates don't expect, but trust it. This is one of Taiwan's most iconic night market foods.

Use the remaining afternoon light to explore Raohe's full length. Mandatory stops: *hu jiao bing* (black pepper pork buns baked in a clay oven), *chou doufu* (stinky tofu — fermented, fried, and served with pickled cabbage — smell terrible, taste incredible), and fresh *bubble tea* from any vendor with a line.

*Afternoon cost: $10–15*

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

For your final dinner, go upscale — but upscale Taiwanese, not fusion or hotel dining. Seek out a restaurant serving refined versions of the classics you've been eating all trip. Mountain Yam has multiple locations and does excellent modern Taiwanese; Shin Yeh is a local institution with decades of history.

Order what you haven't yet tried: steamed fish with ginger and scallion, *lu rou fan* (braised pork rice) done with care, seasonal greens stir-fried simply with garlic, and — if they have it — *ma you ji* (sesame oil chicken soup, traditionally postpartum food but increasingly common in restaurants because it's deeply nourishing).

This is a splurge meal: expect NT$800–1,500 per person ($25–50), possibly more with drinks. But this is how Taipei celebrates, and you should too.

*Dinner cost: $30–60*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$50 | Mid ~$90 | Splurge ~$150

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Night markets peak between 7–9 PM.** Arrive at 5 PM for no lines and the freshest oil; arrive at 8 PM for full atmosphere but longer waits at popular stalls.
- **No eating or drinking on the MRT.** This is enforced with fines, and locals take it seriously. Finish your bubble tea before you go through the gates.
- **Point and gesture confidently.** English menus are rare outside tourist zones. Most stalls have pictures or display cases — point at what looks good, hold up fingers for quantity. This works everywhere.
- **Convenience stores are legitimate food sources.** 7-Eleven and FamilyMart sell surprisingly good *lu rou fan*, tea eggs braised in soy and tea, and hot *guan dong zhu* (oden). When your feet hurt and you need to refuel fast, there's no shame in a convenience store dinner.
