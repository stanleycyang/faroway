The first bite of *babi guling* catches you off guard. It's 9 AM in a concrete warung outside Ubud, ceiling fans churning the humid air, and you're eating roast pork for breakfast because that's when Balinese families eat it—and suddenly you understand why they wake up for this.

---

Bali's food tells a different story than its Instagram persona might suggest. Forget the smoothie bowls and avocado toast of Canggu's expatriate cafes. The real eating here happens in family warungs that have served the same recipes for three generations, at night markets where the smoke from a dozen grills creates its own weather system, at temple ceremonies where food is offering before it's sustenance. This is Hindu-Balinese cooking—bold with galangal and turmeric, sweet with palm sugar, rich with coconut, and completely unlike anything you'll find elsewhere in predominantly Muslim Indonesia.

Here's what to eat. Bring your appetite and a willingness to get your hands dirty.

---

#### Babi Guling — The Ceremonial Pig That Became Breakfast

This is it. The dish that defines Balinese cuisine. A whole suckling pig, rubbed inside and out with a paste of turmeric, coriander, lemongrass, galangal, shallots, garlic, and chilies, then spit-roasted over coconut husks for hours until the skin turns into something approaching stained glass—amber, crackling, shattering against your teeth.

The meat arrives in a riot of textures: slices of the crackling skin, rich shoulder meat, crispy bits of ear and tail for the adventurous, all served over rice with spoonfuls of *lawar* (we'll get there) and a fiery sambal. The fat has rendered into the meat during roasting, and each bite collapses into this unctuous, spice-perfumed richness that coats your mouth.

Traditionally, *babi guling* was reserved for Hindu ceremonies, weddings, and temple festivals—a celebratory food too labor-intensive for casual eating. Now warungs serve it daily, but the best spots still sell out by noon. In Ubud, follow the smoke and the crowds. Expect to pay around $3–5 for a plate that will leave you physically incapacitated with satisfaction.

#### Nasi Goreng — The Democratic Fried Rice

Every restaurant in Bali serves *nasi goreng*. Most are mediocre. But find the right one—a late-night cart with a blackened wok, an aunty who's been making the same version for forty years—and you'll taste why this is Indonesia's national dish.

The secret is *kecap manis*, a molasses-thick sweet soy sauce that caramelizes against the screaming-hot wok, coating each grain of day-old rice in smoky sweetness. A fried egg goes on top, the yolk still liquid, meant to be broken and stirred into the rice. Shrimp paste lurks in the background, funky and oceanic. Crispy fried shallots provide crunch. A wedge of lime waits on the side, its acid cutting through the richness.

This is 2 AM food. This is "I'm not sure what I want but I know I'm hungry" food. This is the dish that costs $1.50 at a street stall and $15 at a resort restaurant, and the cheap version is almost always better because the wok has been seasoned by decades of flames.

---

#### Lawar — The Sacred Salad

You won't find *lawar* at tourist restaurants, and that's a shame. This is ceremonial food, prepared by men for temple offerings and feasts, a minced meat salad that walks the line between raw and cooked, between earthly and divine.

Finely chopped pork or chicken (sometimes raw, sometimes blanched) mixes with shredded young coconut, green beans cut into confetti-thin rounds, and a base paste of chilies, shallots, and *base genep*—the Balinese spice paste built from turmeric, galangal, ginger, and shrimp paste. Fresh pig blood sometimes binds the mixture in more traditional versions, adding iron-rich depth and an almost purple tinge.

The texture is unexpected: slightly granular from the coconut, tender from the meat, with the occasional pop of crispy shallot. It tastes ancient—complex and herbal and nothing like anything you've eaten before. *Lawar* comes standard with *babi guling* plates, but you can also find it at markets during religious festivals. Around $2–3 as part of a mixed plate.

#### Sate Lilit — The Satay That Stays on the Stick

Most satay slides off the skewer. *Sate lilit* wraps around it, clinging to stalks of lemongrass or thick bamboo in ribbons of minced fish paste that char at the edges while staying silken inside.

The mixture starts with mackerel or tuna, pounded smooth and seasoned with coconut milk, lime leaves, palm sugar, and that ubiquitous *base genep* spice paste. The fish mixture gets pressed around the lemongrass in spirals, each one shaped by hand, then grilled over coconut husk coals until spotted with char.

The lemongrass perfumes each bite. The coconut keeps everything impossibly moist. A peanut sauce is optional—good *sate lilit* doesn't need it, the fish sweet enough on its own. Look for these at night markets and beachside grills, where vendors cook them to order. Around $1.50–2 for a generous portion.

This is coastal Bali's answer to inland pork dishes—a reminder that the island's Hinduism embraces seafood where its neighbor islands don't eat pork.

#### Nasi Campur — The Greatest Hits Plate

If you want to taste Bali in a single sitting, order *nasi campur*. The name translates to "mixed rice," which undersells the experience: a mound of white rice surrounded by whatever the cook does best that day.

At a good warung, this means four, five, six different preparations radiating outward. A slick of *sambal matah*—raw shallots and lemongrass in coconut oil. Shredded chicken stewed in turmeric broth. *Lawar*, of course. Fried tempeh, nutty and crisp. *Kare ayam*, yellow curry-braised chicken falling off the bone. Cucumber slices for cooling. A single *sate lilit*. Maybe some *urab*, vegetables dressed in spiced coconut.

Every warung's *nasi campur* is different. Some specialize in pork, some in vegetables for the Brahmin customers who eat no meat. The magic is in the mixing—a bite of rice with sambal, then rice with curry, then all of it together, the flavors building and contrasting across the plate. $2–4 at most local spots, and it's usually enough food for two cautious eaters or one very hungry one.

#### Bebek Betutu — The Duck of Patience

This is not fast food. *Bebek betutu* takes half a day to make, sometimes longer—a whole duck stuffed with *base genep*, wrapped in banana leaves, and slow-cooked over coals until the meat surrenders completely to the spices.

The result emerges tender enough to eat with a spoon, the flesh saturated with turmeric and ginger, the skin gone soft from steaming. It's rich and warming, the kind of dish you eat slowly in the shade of a rice paddy after a morning of temple visits. Traditional preparations bury the wrapped duck in rice-husk embers; modern restaurants use ovens but maintain the patience.

Find it in Ubud, where several family restaurants have made their reputations on this single dish. Expect to pay $8–15 for a portion, and call ahead—some places need a day's notice for the real thing.

---

#### Pisang Goreng — The Perfect Fried Banana

Sometimes the simplest food hits hardest. *Pisang goreng* is a fried banana, nothing more: ripe plantains dipped in rice-flour batter and dropped into hot oil until the exterior crisps into lace and the interior turns to warm custard.

The batter is barely sweetened—palm sugar, a whisper of salt—letting the banana's natural sweetness carry the dish. Some vendors drizzle condensed milk or chocolate sauce over the top. The purist version needs nothing but the contrast between shatter and melt, between tropical fruit and hot fat.

Street carts sell these for around $0.50 each, usually near markets and beaches in late afternoon. Eat them immediately, while steam still rises from the batter.

---

#### Es Campur — The Sweet, Cold Chaos

You'll need this after the *sambal matah* sets your mouth on fire. *Es campur* is Bali's approach to shaved ice: a tall glass of ice shards layered with grass jelly, coconut flesh, fermented cassava tape, sweetened red beans, jackfruit, and whatever else the vendor has on hand, all drowned in coconut milk or condensed milk.

It looks like a Jackson Pollock painting in dessert form. The textures are disorienting—chewy, crunchy, slippery, soft—but the cold sweetness cuts through Bali's humidity like a ceiling fan you can eat. Find it at markets and dessert stalls for $1–2, best consumed mid-afternoon when the heat peaks.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Balinese people eat early. Warungs open by 8 AM and the best dishes—*babi guling*, fresh *lawar*, the crispiest *sate lilit*—are gone by early afternoon. Plan your eating for morning and lunch; dinner in Bali is often lighter, leftover, or tourist-oriented.

Rice is the meal. Everything else is *lauk*, accompaniment. You'll be served more rice than you expect; this is intentional. The ratio matters.

Eat with your right hand when spoons and forks aren't offered. Tear off small portions of rice, press them lightly with your fingers to form a bite, scoop up some *lawar* or sambal, and eat. Your left hand stays on the table, unused.

Don't rush. Warungs expect you to sit. The water comes slowly. The bill comes when you ask. This is not a culture of efficiency; it's a culture of presence. Let the meal take its time.
