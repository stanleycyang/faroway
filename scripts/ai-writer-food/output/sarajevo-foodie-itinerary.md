# Sarajevo Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Sarajevo

Three days in Sarajevo, and I regret nothing except the pants I packed. This is a city that feeds you like you're family — massive portions, absurd prices, and flavors that sit somewhere between Istanbul and Vienna with a smoky Balkan edge. Here's the itinerary I wish I'd had before I started wandering into random ćevapi shops and accidentally ordering three breakfasts.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Sarajevo runs on cash — convertible marks (BAM), and many smaller spots don't take cards. Tipping is relaxed: round up or leave 10% at sit-down restaurants, nothing expected at bakeries or counters. Lunch is the big meal here, often stretching past 2pm. Dinner starts late, around 7pm or later. You won't need reservations except at a handful of upscale spots on weekends. And yes, you will be offered coffee constantly — saying no is borderline rude, so pace yourself.

## Day 1: The Baščaršija Baptism

**Theme: Ottoman Sarajevo — copper, smoke, and the dishes that define the city**

### Morning: Standing Burek at a Pekara

Start where Sarajevans start: standing at a bakery counter at 8am, eating burek with your hands while locals push past for their morning fix. Head to **Baščaršija**, the Ottoman bazaar quarter, and find a *pekara* (bakery) with a line — that's your quality control. Order *burek sa mesom* (meat) or *sirnica* (cheese), and a cup of plain yogurt to cut the richness. The pastry should shatter, the filling should be greasy in the best way, and the whole thing should cost you almost nothing.

**Where**: Any busy pekara in Baščaršija — Buregdžinica Bosna is reliable, but honestly, follow the crowd.
**Cost**: $2–4 for burek and yogurt.

### Afternoon: Ćevapi at the Source

This is the meal. Sarajevo's ćevapi — small grilled minced-meat sausages tucked into *somun* flatbread with raw onion and *kajmak* (a tangy cream cheese that's closer to clotted cream) — is arguably the best street food in the Balkans. The debate over which shop serves the best version has started actual arguments.

Walk the narrow lanes of Baščaršija until you hit **Ćevabdžinica Željo**, the most famous name in the game. Order a full portion (10 pieces) with kajmak and onion. Eat it immediately — ćevapi don't wait. The meat should be smoky, slightly charred, impossibly juicy. The kajmak should be room temperature and spreadable. The onion should make your eyes water.

After, wander **Kazandžiluk** (Coppersmith Street) and watch craftsmen hammer out *džezva* coffee pots by hand. You'll want one by the time you leave.

**Where**: Baščaršija, central old town.
**Cost**: $4–6 for a full portion with drink.

### Evening: Lamb and History at Halvat

For dinner, walk deeper into the bazaar to **Halvat**, hidden inside a 16th-century caravanserai. This is where merchants once stabled their horses; now it's where you eat the best lamb stew in Bosnia. Order *bamija* — okra and lamb slow-cooked until the meat falls apart — and *klepe*, Sarajevo's answer to dumplings, stuffed with spiced meat and drowned in garlicky yogurt and brown butter.

The room is dark, the service is unhurried, the bill is absurd (in a good way). This is a sit-and-stay kind of place.

**Where**: Halvat Restaurant, Baščaršija.
**Cost**: $12–20 for a full dinner with drinks.

**Day 1 Total**: Budget ~$20 | Mid ~$30 | Splurge ~$45

## Day 2: Austro-Hungarian Elegance and the Food Markets

**Theme: The other Sarajevo — cake shops, coffee rituals, and the city beyond the bazaar**

### Morning: Sarajevo Coffee Ceremony

Forget espresso. Bosnian coffee is its own ritual: ground fine, boiled in a *džezva*, poured into a tiny *fildžan* cup, and served with a sugar cube and a piece of *rahat lokum* (Turkish delight). You sip, you talk, you let the grounds settle. This is not grab-and-go.

Walk to **Ferhadija**, the pedestrian spine of the Austro-Hungarian quarter, and find a traditional café. **Kawa** or **Café Tito** both do the ceremony properly. Order a coffee, order a *tulumba* (fried dough soaked in syrup) or a slice of *baklava*, and sit for at least an hour. This is the pace.

**Where**: Ferhadija pedestrian zone.
**Cost**: $3–5 for coffee and pastry.

### Afternoon: Market Lunch and Sweet Detour

Head to **Markale**, the city's central market — rebuilt after the siege-era tragedy that made it infamous. Today it's full of cheese wheels, smoked meats, and produce from the surrounding valleys. Buy some fresh *kajmak*, a hunk of hard sheep's cheese, and whatever stone fruit is in season. This is your picnic.

For a sit-down option, find a *ćevabdžinica* in **Grbavica** — the neighborhood that bore the worst of the siege and is now quietly redeveloping. The ćevapi here are the same recipe, the vibe is just more local, less polished. You'll pay $4 for a full plate and eat among office workers on lunch break.

On your way back, stop at **Baklava Dućan** or any sweet shop with a display case full of diamond-shaped pastries glistening with honey. Bosnian baklava uses walnut and real honey — not the sugar syrup you get elsewhere — and it's denser, stickier, more satisfying. A piece and a glass of water is all you need.

**Where**: Markale Market, Grbavica neighborhood.
**Cost**: Market snacks $5–8; ćevapi lunch $4–6; baklava $2–3.

### Evening: The Spite House

Dinner tonight is at **Inat Kuća** — the "Spite House" — Sarajevo's most atmospheric restaurant. The story: when the Austro-Hungarians built the National Library across the river, one stubborn homeowner refused to sell. So they moved his entire house, stone by stone, to its current spot. Now it's a restaurant serving traditional Bosnian dishes in wood-paneled rooms overlooking the Miljacka.

Order the *sahan* (a slow-cooked meat and vegetable stew served in a clay pot), or the *dolma* (stuffed peppers and grape leaves). Finish with *tufahije* — poached apples stuffed with walnuts and drowned in syrup. It's aggressively sweet, and you'll love it.

**Where**: Inat Kuća, across from the Vijećnica library, river's edge.
**Cost**: $15–25 for a full dinner.

**Day 2 Total**: Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$40 | Splurge ~$55

## Day 3: Hillside Views and the Farewell Feast

**Theme: Climbing for your food — Ottoman neighborhoods and the best meal of the trip**

### Morning: Neighborhood Bakery in Bistrik

Skip the tourist center. Climb the steep cobblestone lanes of **Bistrik**, the hillside *mahala* (neighborhood) above the old town, where Ottoman-era wooden houses lean into the slope and minarets poke through the fog. Find a bakery — any bakery — and order *pita* (the Bosnian version: thinner, rolled, often filled with potato or pumpkin). Eat it on a bench with a view of the valley waking up below.

This is the Sarajevo that most visitors miss. The streets are quiet, the coffee is cheaper, and the light in the morning is unreal.

**Where**: Bistrik, hillside above Baščaršija.
**Cost**: $2–4.

### Afternoon: Yellow Fortress Snacking and Sunset Prep

Walk up to **Žuta Tabija** (Yellow Fortress) — the city's best viewpoint, free to enter, and the only place where you can see every minaret, every red roof, every scar from the siege in one panorama. Bring snacks from the market: bread, cheese, *sudžuk* (dried beef sausage), and something sweet.

On your way down, stop at a street vendor for *ćevapi* one more time. You'll crave it later, I promise.

**Where**: Yellow Fortress, above the old town.
**Cost**: Snacks $5–10.

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your final dinner should be at **Dveri**, a quiet, sophisticated spot in a restored Austro-Hungarian courtyard. This is new Bosnian cooking — the same ingredients, elevated presentation, wine list that actually knows what it's doing. Order the slow-roasted lamb, the seasonal vegetable dishes, and whatever the waiter recommends.

Or, if you want one more night in the old world, return to **Baščaršija** and eat at **Aščinica Asdž**, a traditional *aščinica* (workers' cafeteria) where you point at pots of stewed meat and vegetables behind the glass. It's $6 for a full meal, eaten at plastic tables, and it's the most honest food in the city.

Either way, end with coffee. Always end with coffee.

**Where**: Dveri (Ferhadija) or Aščinica Asdž (Baščaršija).
**Cost**: $20–40 at Dveri | $6–10 at Aščinica.

**Day 3 Total**: Budget ~$20 | Mid ~$35 | Splurge ~$60

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Ćevapi is a lunch food.** Most serious ćevabdžinicas close by early evening. Plan accordingly.
- **Coffee is a commitment.** When someone offers you coffee, they're offering you 45 minutes of their time. Don't gulp it.
- **Cash only at most traditional spots.** ATMs are everywhere, but keep marks in your pocket.
- **Don't ask strangers about the war unprompted.** If someone wants to share, they will. Let conversations unfold naturally, especially over food.
