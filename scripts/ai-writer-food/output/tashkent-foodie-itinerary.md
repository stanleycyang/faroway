Three days in Tashkent taught me that Uzbek hospitality expresses itself most clearly through food — in portions that could feed a small army, in the ritual of tea poured precisely three times before it's considered ready, in strangers at market stalls insisting you try before you buy. This isn't a city where you graze delicately. You arrive hungry, you stay hungry, and somehow you leave even hungrier.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Uzbeks eat late by Western standards — lunch rarely starts before 1 PM, dinner often at 8 PM or later. Cash remains king at traditional restaurants and markets; bring Uzbek som in smaller denominations. Tipping isn't expected at casual spots, but 10% is appreciated at upscale restaurants. No reservations needed except for high-end places on weekends. And please — never refuse tea. It's not actually a question.

## Day 1: The Old City Foundation

**Morning — Chorsu Bazaar, Old Town**

Start where Tashkent has started its mornings for centuries. The Chorsu Bazaar opens early, and by 8 AM the bread vendors are already pulling rounds of non (traditional flatbread) from tandoor ovens sunk into the ground. Find a chaikhana (teahouse) at the bazaar's edge and order kaymak — thick clotted cream spread across warm non, sometimes drizzled with honey. Pair it with a pot of green tea and watch the city wake up. The bread here costs almost nothing; a proper breakfast runs $3–5. Don't leave without buying a round of non stamped with elaborate patterns — it keeps for days and you'll want it later.

**Afternoon — Lunch in the Old Town, then Market Exploration**

Walk ten minutes deeper into the Old Town to find a proper plov restaurant. Plov — the rice dish that Uzbekistan has elevated to near-religious status — is traditionally a lunch affair, cooked in massive kazans (cauldrons) that can feed fifty people. The version here layers long-grain rice with carrots, chickpeas, and chunks of lamb that fall apart at the suggestion of a fork. Order the standard portion; it's enough. A plate runs $4–7, and watching the oshpaz (plov master) work the kazan is half the experience.

After lunch, return to Chorsu for the full tour. The domed central building holds spices, dried fruits, and nuts — buy cumin by the handful, taste the sulfur-yellow dried apricots, let vendors press samples of halvah into your palm. Budget another $5–10 for snacking and provisions.

**Evening — Dinner in the City Center**

Clean up and head to the City Center for something more refined. Tashkent's modern Uzbek restaurants reinterpret traditional dishes with better ingredients and actual presentation. Order shashlik (grilled meat skewers) — lamb is traditional, but the minced lamb lyulya kebab often has better flavor. Add a salad of fresh tomatoes and cucumbers dressed simply with oil, and more non for scooping. A proper dinner here runs $15–25 with drinks. End with a walk through Amir Timur Square, letting the meal settle under the illuminated monuments.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$45 | Splurge ~$80

## Day 2: Beyond the Classics

**Morning — Neighborhood Breakfast in the Arts Quarter**

Skip the hotel breakfast. The Arts Quarter has small cafes that serve what locals actually eat in the morning: somsa (meat-filled pastries) pulled from tandoor ovens, served with cups of black tea strong enough to restart your heart. The pastry should shatter when you bite it, releasing steam and lamb fat in equal measure. Two somsas and tea cost under $4. Eat standing at the counter like everyone else.

**Afternoon — A Different Kind of Lunch**

Tashkent has pockets of Korean influence dating back to Soviet-era deportations, creating a fusion cuisine found nowhere else. In a local neighborhood away from tourist routes, small Korean-Uzbek restaurants serve kuksi — cold noodles in tangy broth with vegetables and meat. It sounds strange until you're sweating through a Tashkent summer and the cold, sour, spicy soup becomes the only thing that makes sense. Follow it with manti, the oversized steamed dumplings filled with spiced lamb and onion, served with a dollop of sour cream. Lunch runs $8–12.

Consider booking an afternoon cooking class to learn plov technique — several home cooks in residential neighborhoods offer three-hour sessions where you shop for ingredients at local markets and cook over open flames. Classes run $40–60 and include the meal you prepare.

**Evening — Hidden Cafe Discovery**

For dinner, seek out a tucked-away cafe in the quieter parts of town — the kind of place with no English menu and ten tables maximum. This is where you find the regional variations: dimlama, a slow-cooked pot of meat and vegetables that's somewhere between stew and steam; or lagman, hand-pulled noodles in tomato-lamb broth that's distinctly Central Asian despite its Chinese origins. Order what the table next to you is eating. You'll spend $10–18 and eat better than most restaurant meals.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$30 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$140 (with cooking class)

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

**Morning — Classic Breakfast, Properly Done**

Return to what works. Find a chaikhana in the Business District that caters to locals heading to work — the food is fast, hot, and unpretentious. Order non with kaymak again, but add a bowl of shorpo, the clear lamb broth with potato and carrot that Uzbeks consider both breakfast and medicine. The combination of bread, cream, and restorative soup costs $5–8 and prepares you for a final day of serious eating.

**Afternoon — Market Grazing and Final Shopping**

Spend your last afternoon walking through a different market — smaller than Chorsu, less touristed, where vendors have time to explain what you're looking at. Buy dried fruits and nuts to take home (they pack and travel well). Snack on fresh samsa, cups of fresh-pressed pomegranate juice, and whatever samples vendors offer. Pick up suzma, the strained yogurt that Uzbeks eat with everything, and eat it right there with bread. This isn't lunch — it's a sustained graze. Budget $10–15 for eating your way through.

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your last dinner in Tashkent should be the meal you'll remember. Return to the City Center or venture to a rooftop restaurant as the sun sets. Order the full spread: a proper plov made to order (worth the wait), grilled lamb chops charred at the edges, fresh salads, and more bread than any table needs. Drink black tea or venture into vodka territory if the mood strikes. This is the meal where you order too much intentionally, where you let the waiter recommend, where you stay until they begin stacking chairs. Budget $25–40 for a memorable finale, or push to $60–80 if you're doing it right.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$110

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Plov is a lunch dish.** Restaurants make it in the morning; by evening, it's gone or sitting. Plan accordingly.
- **Bread is sacred.** Never place non face-down, never throw it away, never cut it with a knife — tear it by hand. Locals notice.
- **Tea ritual matters.** The first two pours go back into the pot; only the third pour is served. This isn't superstition — it mixes the leaves properly.
- **Learn to say "rahmat" (thank you).** It opens doors, earns second helpings, and occasionally gets you pulled behind the counter to see how things are made.
