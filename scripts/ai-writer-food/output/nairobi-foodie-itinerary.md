Three days in Nairobi taught me that this city eats with purpose. The smoke from nyama choma grills hangs in the evening air across every neighborhood, chai flows sweeter and stronger than anywhere else I've traveled, and the line between street food and fine dining blurs in the best possible way. This is the itinerary I built from my mistakes, my discoveries, and far too many plates of ugali.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Kenyans eat lunch between 1–2 PM and dinner rarely before 8 PM — adjust your timing or face empty restaurants. M-Pesa mobile money works everywhere, but carry some cash for market stalls and older establishments. Tipping 10% is standard at sit-down restaurants; round up for casual spots. Greet your server warmly before ordering — Kenyans value politeness, and a simple "Habari" goes far. Book ahead for upscale spots in Kilimani and Karen on weekends; everywhere else, walk in.

## Day 1: The CBD and Learning to Eat Like Nairobi Does

### Morning: Chai and Mandazi in the City's Heart

Start in the CBD, where Nairobi's working class fuels up before the day begins. Find a tea stall near the City Market and order Kenyan chai with mandazi — the triangular fried dough that lands somewhere between a doughnut and a beignet. The chai here isn't delicate: milk, tea leaves, cardamom, ginger, and enough sugar to make your teeth ache, all boiled together until it's thick and rust-colored. The mandazi should be warm, slightly crisp outside, pillowy within. Stand at the counter like everyone else does, dunk the mandazi into your chai, and watch the city wake up around you.

**Cost**: $2–4 for chai and 2–3 mandazi

### Afternoon: Lunch at City Market and Street Food Recon

Walk deeper into the CBD to City Market for lunch. Upstairs, past the tourist curios, you'll find the food stalls where office workers queue for plates of pilau — spiced rice studded with meat, the aromatics layered deep with cumin, cardamom, and cinnamon. Order pilau with a side of kachumbari (fresh tomato and onion salad) to cut through the richness. The rice should glisten with rendered fat and spice. Eat at the communal tables, elbow-to-elbow with strangers.

After lunch, explore the surrounding streets for snack reconnaissance. Look for roasted maize vendors, muhogo (cassava chips), and if you're lucky, a vendor selling smokie pasua — a sliced sausage stuffed into a roll with kachumbari and hot sauce. This is Nairobi's grab-and-go street food, and it costs almost nothing.

**Cost**: $6–10 for pilau lunch, $2–4 for street snacks

### Evening: Your First Nyama Choma Experience

For dinner, head to Westlands for your introduction to nyama choma — Kenya's national obsession with flame-grilled meat. This isn't rushed. You'll order your meat by weight (start with half a kilo of goat or beef), then wait 45 minutes to an hour while it cooks over hot coals. Use this time for Tusker beers and conversation. When the meat arrives, it comes with ugali, that dense maize porridge that serves as your edible utensil — tear off a piece, press it into a scoop, grab the meat. Add sukuma wiki (sautéed collard greens with tomatoes and onions) to round out the plate.

The meat should have char on the outside, stay pink within, and need nothing but salt. If the restaurant offers their house kachumbari or a tomato-chili sauce, take it. You'll be full in a way that lasts until morning.

**Cost**: $15–30 depending on meat quantity and drinks

**Day 1 Total**: Budget ~$25 | Mid-range ~$45 | Splurge ~$90

## Day 2: The Karen Slow Down

### Morning: Breakfast in the Leafy Suburbs

Take an Uber to Karen — Nairobi's green, unhurried suburb where jacaranda trees line the roads and the pace drops immediately. Have breakfast at one of Karen's café-restaurants, where you can get proper eggs, fresh juice, and excellent Kenyan coffee (yes, the country exports most of its best beans, but the specialty cafés keep some back). Order a full breakfast with beef sausages instead of pork — they're spiced differently here and worth trying. The coffee should be bright and fruity, nothing like what Kenya ships abroad.

This is your gentle start before a more intensive eating afternoon.

**Cost**: $10–18 for a full breakfast with coffee

### Afternoon: A Detour Through Kazuri and Karen's Hidden Lunch Spots

Before lunch, stop at Kazuri Beads — the women's cooperative making hand-painted ceramics — not for the food, but because you'll walk past their small café serving fresh-squeezed juices and simple snacks. It's a good mid-morning refresher.

For lunch, find one of Karen's garden restaurants serving Kenyan-fusion food — the kind of place with outdoor seating, bougainvillea climbing the walls, and a menu that respects local ingredients while taking liberties with presentation. Order mukimo (mashed potatoes with peas, corn, and greens) alongside grilled tilapia from Lake Victoria. This is home-style Kenyan food elevated slightly, served in a setting that explains why Karen residents rarely leave their neighborhood.

If you want the more traditional experience, ask your driver for the nearest local spot serving matoke (stewed plantains) with beans — it's harder to find in tourist areas but worth the hunt.

**Cost**: $15–25 for a proper lunch

### Evening: The Kilimani Splurge

Tonight, dress up slightly and head to Kilimani for dinner at one of Nairobi's upscale restaurants. This neighborhood has attracted the city's most ambitious chefs, and the best spots here reinterpret Kenyan and East African flavors with technical precision. Look for tasting menus featuring locally sourced ingredients: Lake Turkana fish, Kenyan lamb, indigenous vegetables you won't recognize. The cocktails often incorporate Kenyan honey, local botanicals, or African spirits.

This is where you spend money. A full dinner with drinks and wine will run high, but the experience justifies it — Nairobi's fine dining scene is genuinely exciting, not just expensive for the sake of it.

**Cost**: $50–120 depending on wine and courses

**Day 2 Total**: Budget ~$30 | Mid-range ~$60 | Splurge ~$165

## Day 3: Farewell Feast

### Morning: The Market Breakfast

Your last morning belongs to the markets. If it's a Maasai Market day (the rotating location changes — ask your hotel), go early and find the food vendors on the periphery. Otherwise, return to the CBD area and find a local café serving uji — a thin, warm millet or sorghum porridge that Kenyans drink for breakfast. It's lightly sweet, faintly sour, and more sustaining than it looks. Pair it with a chapati (the Kenyan version, flakier and richer than Indian chapati) or another round of mandazi.

This is the breakfast that millions of Kenyans eat before work. It costs almost nothing and tastes like the real city.

**Cost**: $3–6 for uji and accompaniments

### Afternoon: The Karura Forest Detour and Westlands Food Walk

Burn some calories with a short walk in Karura Forest — the urban forest with waterfalls hiding inside Nairobi — then emerge hungry into Westlands for an afternoon of grazing. This neighborhood has everything: Ethiopian injera spreads, Indian chaat stalls, Lebanese shawarma, and Kenyan staples side by side. Don't commit to one restaurant. Instead, eat your way through: a samosa here, some grilled mishkaki (meat skewers) there, a stop for mutura (Kenyan blood sausage, spiced and grilled) if you're feeling adventurous.

Mutura is Nairobi's most divisive snack — it looks intimidating and tastes deeply savory, served sliced with hot sauce and toothpicks. The vendors who grill it at roadside stands have often been doing so for decades. Try it once.

**Cost**: $10–20 grazing through multiple stops

### Evening: The Final Nyama Choma

End where Nairobi eats best: another round of nyama choma, this time at a dedicated nyama choma joint in Langata or a Westlands institution. Order differently than Day 1 — if you had goat, try beef ribs. If you had beef, order the mixed grill. Add chapati this time instead of ugali, and ask for their best hot sauce.

This is your farewell meal, so stretch it. Order a second round of sukuma wiki. Get another Tusker. Watch the smoke rise from the grills, listen to the conversations around you, and understand why Kenyans will argue for hours about whose nyama choma is best. They're all right, and they're all wrong, and you'll be thinking about this meal on your flight home.

**Cost**: $20–40 for a proper send-off

**Day 3 Total**: Budget ~$35 | Mid-range ~$65 | Splurge ~$130

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Nyama choma takes time** — always add an hour buffer to any dinner plan involving grilled meat. Order it, settle in, and stop checking your watch.
- **Nairobi traffic destroys schedules** — if you have a dinner reservation in Kilimani and you're in Karen at 6 PM, you're already late. Use Uber's time estimates, then add 30%.
- **The best chai comes from the humblest stalls** — avoid anywhere that serves chai from a machine. You want to see it boiled in a pot.
- **Ask locals where they eat nyama choma** — everyone has an opinion, and following those opinions led me to my best meals. Your Uber driver knows spots that aren't on Google Maps.
