The plastic bowl arrives at 7 AM, still steaming, and I realize I've been wrong about breakfast my entire life. A mountain of carbohydrates — rice, lentils, macaroni, chickpeas — crowned with mahogany-fried onions so crispy they shatter at the touch of my spoon. The vendor splashes tomato sauce and a garlic-vinegar kick across the top, and somewhere in the chaos of Downtown Cairo's morning rush, I take my first bite of koshari and understand why this city has been feeding civilizations for five thousand years.

Cairo doesn't have a food scene. Cairo *is* a food scene — one that operates on its own clock, in its own language, with its own stubborn insistence that the best meals cost less than a cup of coffee and come served on the street, in paper, standing up. After three weeks of eating through this city, my notebook is stained with tahini and my sense of culinary pretension thoroughly humbled.

#### Koshari

Start here. Everyone does. Koshari is Egypt's national dish, a carb-on-carb collision that sounds chaotic on paper and tastes like pure, working-class genius in practice. The base is a tumble of short-grain rice, brown lentils, elbow macaroni, and sometimes vermicelli, all cooked separately and layered together with the precision of a Pharaonic tomb builder. Over this goes a bright, slightly spiced tomato sauce (often made with cumin and a whisper of cinnamon) and a sharp garlic-vinegar condiment called dakka that cuts through the richness like a blade.

But the soul of koshari is the fried onions — thin half-moons cooked until they're almost burnt, impossibly crispy, piled high like edible confetti. You mix everything together yourself, creating a different ratio with every spoonful. It's filling without being heavy, humble without being boring, and at 15–25 EGP ($0.50–$0.80), it's the cheapest full meal in the city.

The dish emerged in the 19th century when Cairo's diverse population — Indian traders, Italian engineers, Egyptian farmers — began sharing kitchens. Koshari is what happened when nobody could agree on what to cook, so they cooked everything. Find it at any *koshary* shop (they specialize in nothing else), lined up behind glass warming stations. Abou Tarek in Downtown is famous, but honestly, the street corner cart near your hotel is probably just as good.

#### Ful Medames

If koshari is Cairo's lunch, ful is its morning heartbeat. Slow-cooked fava beans, stewed overnight in a narrow-necked copper pot called a *qidra* until they're creamy and soft, then mashed roughly and dressed with cumin, lemon juice, olive oil, and raw garlic. The texture falls somewhere between refried beans and hummus — earthy, dense, satisfying in a way that feels ancient.

Ful has been eaten in Egypt for over four thousand years, appearing in Pharaonic tombs alongside gold and jewels as provisions for the afterlife. Today it's the breakfast of taxi drivers, shop owners, students, and presidents alike. The democratizing power of ful is real: everyone eats it, everyone has an opinion on where to get the best version.

You'll find it at *fuul* carts starting around 5 AM, often served in a split pocket of *baladi* bread with a drizzle of tahini and a scatter of chopped tomatoes. Some places offer it with a fried egg nestled on top, the yolk running into the beans. Expect to pay 10–20 EGP ($0.30–$0.65). Order it at a plastic table in Old Cairo, or wrapped in paper for the walk to the Egyptian Museum.

#### Ta'meya

Forget everything you know about falafel. Egyptian ta'meya is a different creature entirely — made with dried fava beans instead of chickpeas, mixed with massive handfuls of fresh herbs (parsley, cilantro, dill, leeks) until the interior is brilliantly green. The patties are formed thinner and flatter than their Levantine cousins, coated in sesame seeds, and fried until the exterior is a shattering bronze shell.

The inside stays moist and vegetal, almost fluffy, with a flavor that's more herb garden than bean. When you eat ta'meya, you're eating something close to the original form of falafel — food scholars believe the dish originated in Egypt before spreading north through the Mediterranean.

Street vendors fry them to order in blackened vats of oil, handing you three or four in a paper wrap for 5–10 EGP ($0.15–$0.30). Stuff them into bread with pickled vegetables and tahini, or eat them straight, the sesame seeds popping between your teeth. Best consumed within two minutes of leaving the fryer; they don't travel well.

#### Molokhia

This is the dish that divides rooms. Molokhia is a soup — or is it a sauce? — made from jute leaves (yes, the same plant used to make burlap) chopped so finely they dissolve into a thick, glossy, almost mucilaginous green liquid. It's finished with a *taqleya*, a sizzling mixture of fried garlic and coriander that gets stirred in at the last moment, releasing an aroma that can clear a sinus and call a family to dinner simultaneously.

The texture troubles some people. It's silky but also slightly viscous, somewhere between okra and melted spinach. For Egyptians, though, it's comfort food of the highest order — poured over rice, served alongside roast chicken or rabbit, mopped up with bread until the bowl gleams. The dish dates back to medieval Cairo, when the Fatimid caliphs allegedly tried to ban it (unsuccessfully).

You'll find molokhia in home-style restaurants and family kitchens rather than street carts. Look for places advertising *akl baity* (home cooking) in neighborhoods like Sayeda Zeinab or Maadi. A plate with rice and meat runs 40–80 EGP ($1.30–$2.60). Eat it at lunch; it's too heavy for Cairo's late, slow evenings.

#### Shawarma

The rotating spits appear around dusk, massive cones of marinated meat — usually beef or chicken — turning slowly beside gas flames, their edges caramelizing into crispy, fatty shards. Cairo's shawarma has a particular style: the meat is shaved fine, piled into *baladi* bread or rolled in thin *sham* wraps, dressed with tahini, pickled vegetables, and sometimes a hit of hot sauce or garlic cream.

It's street food at its most theatrical. You choose your meat, watch it sliced onto a waiting bread, and accept your change before the wrap even finishes coming together. The best shawarma arrives dripping slightly, the bread soggy with meat juices where it meets the tahini, the pickled turnips adding acid and crunch.

Find it in Islamic Cairo near Khan el-Khalili after the bazaar starts winding down around 9 PM, or along 26th of July Street in Zamalek where late-night stalls cater to the gallery-and-lounge crowd. Expect 25–50 EGP ($0.80–$1.60) depending on size and location.

#### Hawawshi

Think of it as Egypt's answer to the empanada — spiced ground meat (usually beef) mixed with onions, peppers, and parsley, stuffed inside a round of *baladi* bread and baked in a wood-fired oven until the exterior is charred and crackly. The bread absorbs the meat juices during cooking, turning the inside layer slightly soggy while the outside stays crisp.

Each bite gives you crunch, then soft, then the mineral punch of beef and the sweetness of caramelized onion. It's deeply satisfying in a way that needs no sauce or accompaniment. Bakers sell them stacked in baskets, still warm, for 15–30 EGP ($0.50–$1). Perfect walking food.

#### Feteer Meshaltet

The Egyptian croissant, if croissants were the size of a pizza and involved a flour-dusted man slapping dough against a marble slab until it stretched gossamer-thin. Feteer is flaky, layered pastry — hundreds of sheets folded together, baked until golden, then served either sweet (with honey, sugar, cream) or savory (with cheese, minced meat, eggs).

Watching it made is half the experience. The dough gets pulled and spun like strudel until you can nearly see through it, then folded, slapped, folded again, a rhythm that's hypnotic at 2 AM in a Dokki bakery. The finished feteer tears apart in sheets, each layer thin as paper, shattering into buttery shards. Expect to pay 30–60 EGP ($1–$2) for a small round.

#### Om Ali

The dessert that ends every proper Egyptian meal — a warm bread pudding made from puff pastry or phyllo scraps, soaked in sweetened milk, and baked with raisins, coconut, pistachios, and pine nuts until the top forms a golden, bubbling crust. It arrives at the table still simmering, the kind of dessert you eat slowly, blowing on each spoonful, tasting the cream as it coats your tongue.

The name means "Ali's mother," and the legend involves a 13th-century queen celebrating her husband's assassination by ordering the royal cooks to create a dessert for the entire nation. Whether true or not, Om Ali does feel celebratory — rich, excessive, unabashedly sweet. Find it at traditional restaurants and hotel buffets for 25–50 EGP ($0.80–$1.60). Order one for the table. You will finish it.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Cairenes eat late. Lunch might not happen until 3 PM; dinner often starts at 10 PM or later. Street food, though, operates on its own schedule: ful carts open before dawn, koshari shops stay busy through the afternoon, shawarma spits don't start spinning until sunset.

Sharing is expected and refusing food is impolite. If someone at the next table offers you a taste, accept graciously — and offer something back. Order more bread than you think you need; it costs nearly nothing and serves as utensil, napkin, and palate cleanser.

Don't fear the street carts. The turnover is high, the food is fresh, and the vendors are feeding their own neighbors. The rougher the stall looks, often the better the cooking. Carry small bills — 10 and 20 EGP notes — because nobody has change. And follow the crowds: if twenty locals are lined up at a cart with no menu and no signage, get in line.
