Three days in Hội An with a single mission: eat everything worth eating, skip nothing important, and leave with the kind of food memories that ruin you for lesser versions forever. This ancient trading port has been perfecting its signature dishes for centuries — the water here literally makes the noodles taste different — and I spent 72 hours proving it.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Hội An eats early. Breakfast spots open by 6:30 AM and the best bánh mì sells out by 10 AM. Lunch peaks at 11:30 AM, dinner by 6 PM — eat on local time or miss the freshest food. Cash is king everywhere except upscale restaurants; ATMs are plentiful in the Ancient Town. Tipping isn't expected at street stalls or markets but 5-10% is appreciated at sit-down restaurants. Remove your shoes before entering family-run places with floor seating — watch what locals do. No reservations needed anywhere except cooking classes.

## Day 1: Ancient Town Essentials

**Morning: The Bánh Mì That Started Everything**

Start at 7 AM outside Bánh Mì Phương on Phan Châu Trinh street in the Ancient Town. Yes, the queue is real. Yes, it moves fast. Yes, it's worth it. This is the bánh mì that Anthony Bourdain called "a symphony in a sandwich," and eating it fresh — the bread still warm, the pâté silky, the pickled vegetables sharp and bright — you'll understand why people fly here for a $2 sandwich. Order the special (bánh mì đặc biệt) with everything: pork, pâté, ham, herbs, chili. Eat it standing on the street like everyone else. **Cost: 35,000₫ (~$1.50)**

Walk it off through the quiet morning streets before the tour groups arrive. The Japanese Covered Bridge sits in mist at this hour, nearly empty.

**Afternoon: The Holy Trinity of Hội An Noodles**

By 11 AM, make your way to Cam Pho, the residential neighborhood just north of the Ancient Town where locals actually eat. Here's your mission: Cao Lầu, the dish that exists nowhere else on earth.

The thick rice noodles are made with water from a specific well and ash from a specific island — this isn't marketing, it's chemistry. The texture is chewy, almost squeaky, topped with slices of barbecued pork, fresh herbs, and crispy rice crackers. Find a plastic stool at a family-run spot (look for the places with Vietnamese customers and no English menus) and order one bowl. Mix everything together. The combination of textures — soft noodle, crunchy cracker, tender pork — is what makes this dish legendary. **Cost: 40,000-60,000₫ (~$2.50)**

Spend the early afternoon wandering the Ancient Town with your ticket (120,000₫ covers five heritage sites). The Phước Kiến Assembly Hall and the old merchant houses are genuinely beautiful and you'll work up an appetite.

By 3 PM, hunt down White Rose Dumplings. These translucent rice paper parcels shaped like roses are filled with minced shrimp and topped with crispy shallots. Only one family in Hội An makes them, but several restaurants serve them. Find a spot along the Thu Bon River in the Ancient Town and order a plate with cold beer. **Cost: 50,000₫ (~$2)**

**Evening: Riverside Chicken Rice**

Dinner is Cơm Gà Hội An — Hội An chicken rice — and you want to eat it as the lanterns come on. Head to one of the riverside restaurants in the Ancient Town (Cơm Việt is solid) and order the full plate: turmeric-scented rice, poached chicken so tender it barely holds together, fresh herbs, and a bowl of chicken broth on the side. The rice is the star — golden from turmeric, fragrant from being cooked in chicken fat. Pair it with a fresh beer and watch the boats drift past with their paper lanterns. **Cost: 120,000-180,000₫ (~$7)**

After dinner, walk the lantern-lit streets. The night market along the river glows orange and red. Find a street vendor selling chè — Vietnamese sweet soup — and try the coconut milk version with beans and jellies. It's dessert and nightcap in one bowl. **Cost: 20,000₫ (~$1)**

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$15 | Mid ~$25 | Splurge ~$50

## Day 2: Villages and Markets

**Morning: Herb Farm Breakfast**

Rent a bicycle from your guesthouse (50,000₫ for the day) and ride to Tra Que Organic Village at 6:30 AM. The farmers are already watering their herbs in the cool morning light — rows of mint, basil, Vietnamese coriander, and vegetables stretching toward the river. This is where most Hội An restaurants source their greens, and walking through at dawn is free and beautiful.

Several farm-restaurants here offer cooking classes that start with breakfast. Book a morning session (around $25-35) that includes harvesting herbs, making your own bánh xèo (crispy rice crepes), and eating everything you cook. The crepes come out of the pan sizzling, stuffed with pork and shrimp, and you wrap them in rice paper with fresh herbs before dipping in nước chấm sauce. Making them yourself ruins restaurant bánh xèo forever. **Cost: 600,000-850,000₫ (~$25-35) for class including food**

If you skip the class, ride back to Cam Pho and find a bánh xèo street stall. The crepes should crackle when you bite through them. **Cost: 40,000₫ (~$1.75)**

**Afternoon: The Real Market**

Skip the tourist night market and head to Cồn Market in the early afternoon, when it's functioning as an actual market for locals. This covered market sprawls with vegetables, meat, fish, and — crucially — prepared food stalls in the back corners.

Find the bánh bèo ladies: they sell steamed rice cakes topped with dried shrimp and scallion oil, served in tiny ceramic dishes. Point at what you want, sit on a plastic stool, and eat six or eight of them. **Cost: 30,000₫ (~$1.25)**

Wander deeper and look for mì quảng stalls — the other great Hội An noodle dish, broader and flatter than cao lầu, served in a smaller amount of turmeric-rich broth with pork, shrimp, and peanuts. It's messier, more robust, and locals argue endlessly about whether it's better than cao lầu. (You should eat both and have your own opinion.) **Cost: 35,000₫ (~$1.50)**

Buy fruit for later: mangosteen if they're in season, dragon fruit, rambutan. **Cost: 40,000₫ (~$1.75)**

**Evening: Across the River**

Take a sampan (small wooden boat) across the Thu Bon River to Kim Bong Carpentry Village. The ride costs almost nothing and takes five minutes. The village is quiet, known for its woodworkers, but there are a few family-run restaurants serving home-style food that tourists rarely find.

Order whatever they're cooking that day — it might be cá kho tộ (caramelized fish in clay pot), bò lúc lắc (shaking beef), or grilled pork with rice. The vegetables will be from Tra Que, the herbs will be perfect, and you'll pay half what you would in the Ancient Town. Bring cash. **Cost: 150,000-250,000₫ (~$8-12)**

Take the sampan back as the lanterns reflect on the water.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$20 | Mid ~$45 | Splurge ~$80

## Day 3: Beach, Best Of, and Farewell

**Morning: Fishing Village and Beach Breakfast**

Wake early and bicycle to An Bang fishing village by 6 AM. The morning catch is coming in: fishermen hauling round basket boats onto the sand, women sorting fish into baskets, the whole chaotic commerce of a working beach. Watch for 20 minutes, then walk south to An Bang Beach proper.

Several beach cafes open early. Find one with plastic chairs in the sand and order a Vietnamese breakfast: phở gà (chicken pho) or bánh cuốn (steamed rice rolls with pork). Eat with your feet in the sand, watch the waves, drink strong Vietnamese coffee. **Cost: 80,000-120,000₫ (~$4-5)**

Swim if you want. You've earned it.

**Afternoon: Final Market Run and Best Bánh Mì Comparison**

Ride back to town by noon. Your mission: settle the bánh mì debate. You've had Bánh Mì Phương. Now try Bánh Mì Madam Khánh on Trần Cao Vân street, the other legendary sandwich shop. The bread is different — slightly chewier — and the filling ratio is its own philosophy. Get the same special order as Day 1 and eat them back-to-back in your memory. Have an opinion. Argue about it later. **Cost: 35,000₫ (~$1.50)**

Walk through the Ancient Town one more time in the afternoon light. Stop for cà phê sữa đá (iced milk coffee) at a street stall and watch the light change on the yellow buildings. **Cost: 25,000₫ (~$1)**

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your last dinner should be the best version of what Hội An does. Go back to the Ancient Town as the lanterns light, find a riverside table at a restaurant with tablecloths (this is splurge territory), and order everything you loved most: cao lầu, white rose dumplings, bánh xèo, grilled pork skewers, morning glory with garlic.

Order too much. Share it with whoever you're traveling with. Drink local beer or Vietnamese wine if you're brave. Watch the river. Let the meal go long.

This is the dinner you'll remember in five years when someone mentions Vietnam, when the smell of lemongrass hits you in some other city, when you try to explain why Hội An isn't just a pretty town with lanterns but a place that takes food seriously enough to perfect the same dishes for four hundred years.

**Cost: 400,000-800,000₫ (~$18-35)**

End with chè from a street vendor on the walk home. **Cost: 20,000₫ (~$1)**

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$40 | Splurge ~$75

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Eat on local time or miss out.** The best bánh mì sells out by 10 AM. The freshest pho is gone by 9 AM. Dinner crowds thin after 7:30 PM. Adjust your clock.
- **Walk five minutes north of the Ancient Town.** Cam Pho neighborhood serves the same food for half the price without the English menus and tourist markup.
- **Order cao lầu with extra crackers.** The fried rice paper is the textural magic — ask for more (thêm bánh đa).
- **Learn to say "không đường" (no sugar).** Vietnamese coffee and many drinks come extremely sweet by default. This phrase saves you.
