Mexico City doesn't ask if you're hungry. It assumes you are, always, and it's right. Over three days, I ate my way through markets where grandmothers have been making the same tacos for forty years, through fine dining rooms pushing Mexican cuisine into new territory, and through street corners where the best meal of my life cost less than a subway ride back home. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Mexicans eat late — lunch runs 2–4 PM, dinner starts around 8 or 9 PM. Tip 10–15% at sit-down restaurants; street food requires no tip but rounding up is appreciated. Cash dominates at markets and taquerias, though nicer restaurants take cards. The altitude (2,240 meters) will hit you harder than you expect — drink water between meals, not just micheladas. Book any restaurant over $50/person at least a week ahead.

## Day 1: Centro Histórico — The Ancient Heart

### Morning

Start where the city started: the Zócalo and the streets radiating from it. By 8 AM, the city is already eating. Find a corner café near the cathedral and order **chilaquiles** — yesterday's tortillas reborn in salsa verde or roja, topped with crema, queso fresco, and a fried egg if you're smart. The chips should be softened but not soggy, still holding their shape under the weight of everything good.

Walk it off through the colonial streets toward Templo Mayor, watching the city wake up around you. The light at this hour is forgiving, the crowds haven't arrived, and you'll have earned your next meal.

*Estimated cost: $8–$15*

### Afternoon

Lunch happens at Mercado de San Juan, and it's not optional. This isn't a tourist market dressed up for Instagram — it's where chefs come to source, where you can find everything from imported cheeses to grasshoppers. Walk the aisles first. Get your bearings. Then sit at one of the market stalls and order whatever looks freshest — ceviches, grilled seafood, or if you're feeling bold, try the escamoles (ant larvae) that taste like buttery corn.

After, walk north through Centro toward the Palacio de Bellas Artes. Stop for an **elote** from a street cart — grilled corn slathered in mayo, rolled in cotija cheese, dusted with chili, and squeezed with lime. Eat it standing up, making a mess, like everyone else.

*Estimated cost: $18–$35*

### Evening

For dinner, stay in Centro Histórico and eat like this is the only night you have. Order **mole** — the real thing, the dish that takes three days to make properly. The sauce should be complex enough that you taste something new with every bite: dried chiles, chocolate, spices you can't name, depth you didn't know food could have. Get it over chicken or turkey at a traditional restaurant near the Zócalo. Pair it with a mezcal — smoky, earthy, from Oaxaca if they have it.

Walk back through the lit-up Centro streets after. The Zócalo at night, with the cathedral glowing and the Palacio Nacional illuminated, will remind you that you're eating in one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the Americas.

*Estimated cost: $35–$85*

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~$60 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$250

## Day 2: Roma Norte — The New Guard

### Morning

Roma Norte wakes up slower than Centro — tree-lined streets, Art Deco buildings, the smell of good coffee drifting from corner cafés. Find a spot along Avenida Álvaro Obregón and order breakfast the way Romans (the Mexico City kind) do: fresh fruit, pan dulce from a bakery, strong coffee.

Or do what I did: hunt down **tamales** from a morning vendor. The ones wrapped in banana leaf (tamales oaxaqueños) are different from the corn husk version — softer, more aromatic, often filled with mole negro or chicken in green sauce. Buy two. You'll want a backup.

*Estimated cost: $6–$12*

### Afternoon

This is your day to learn, not just eat. Book a morning or early afternoon cooking class in Roma — several excellent ones operate out of beautiful old homes, teaching you to make salsas, tortillas from scratch, and dishes you'll actually recreate at home. You'll eat what you make, so consider this lunch.

If cooking isn't your thing, walk to Mercado Medellín instead. It's the market Roma locals actually use — less polished than San Juan, more alive. Look for the Yucatecan food stalls serving cochinita pibil, or the juice stands blending whatever's in season. Eat **tlacoyos** here: thick, blue corn masa pockets stuffed with beans, topped with nopales (cactus), salsa, and crumbled cheese. They're the kind of food that makes you understand why corn is sacred.

*Estimated cost: $25–$45 (cooking class) or $12–$20 (market lunch)*

### Evening

Roma Norte transforms at night. The mezcalerias open their doors, candles get lit, and the neighborhood becomes the kind of place where dinner stretches into something longer. Choose a restaurant pushing Mexican ingredients in new directions — places here are doing things with huitlacoche (corn fungus), chapulines (grasshoppers), and local produce that would cost twice as much in New York or London.

End the night at a mezcal bar. Order a flight, learn the difference between espadín and tobalá, and let the bartender guide you. Mezcal isn't tequila's smokier cousin — it's an entirely different philosophy of drinking.

*Estimated cost: $45–$120*

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~$75 | Mid ~$110 | Splurge ~$250

## Day 3: The Grand Tour — Coyoacán to Condesa

### Morning

Take an Uber south to Coyoacán, the old village that the city swallowed but never really tamed. The plazas here feel like small-town Mexico — slower, leafier, full of families and couples on benches. At the Mercado de Coyoacán, find the tostada stands and order one piled with tuna (the fresh kind, bright red) or ceviche. Follow it with a cup of fresh-cut fruit with lime and chili.

If you have time (and booked weeks in advance), visit the Frida Kahlo Museum nearby. You'll walk past her kitchen, painted the famous blue, and understand why food and art were never separate things in this house.

*Estimated cost: $10–$18*

### Afternoon

Head to Condesa for a walking lunch — this is grazing time. Amsterdam avenue curves through the neighborhood in an oval, perfect for an afternoon of eating in motion. Stop for a **taco al pastor** — the spit-roasted pork, shaved thin, topped with a slice of pineapple, served on a corn tortilla barely bigger than your palm. Find a stand with a line and high turnover. The meat should be crispy at the edges, sweet from the pineapple, and gone in three bites.

Keep walking. Try a gordita. Get a paleta (Mexican popsicle) from a cart. Sit in Parque México and watch the dogs being walked while you digest.

*Estimated cost: $12–$25*

### Evening

Your last dinner should be the one worth remembering. Go to Polanco — the upscale neighborhood where Mexico's best restaurants cluster. This is where you splurge, where you order the tasting menu and let the kitchen show you what's possible. The best places here are cooking food that's unmistakably Mexican but unlike anything you've had before — indigenous ingredients treated with technical precision, courses that tell stories, meals that take three hours and deserve every minute.

Make a reservation. Dress up a little. Drink the wine pairing. This is your farewell to a city that fed you better than you knew you needed.

*Estimated cost: $75–$250*

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~$75 | Mid ~$105 | Splurge ~$300

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Follow the smoke and the crowd.** The best street tacos come from stalls with visible flames and a line of locals. High turnover means fresh meat and safe eating.

- **Eat lunch like a Mexican.** The biggest, best-value meal of the day is *comida corrida* — a set lunch menu offered at countless restaurants between 1–4 PM. Three courses for under $8 is standard.

- **Mezcal before, not during.** Drink mezcal as an aperitif or nightcap, not paired with heavy food. It overwhelms; give it space.

- **Learn "sin cebolla, por favor"** (no onion) and "con todo" (with everything). Street vendors respect specificity — tell them exactly what you want.
