Three days in Siem Reap taught me that the temples aren't the only ancient art form here — Khmer cuisine has been perfecting itself for centuries, and the best versions hide in plain sight if you know where to look. I ate seventeen meals, wandered through four markets, and burned through three notebooks tracking down what actually matters. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me on arrival.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cambodians eat early — breakfast stalls peak between 6:30 and 8 AM, and many close by mid-morning. Both US dollars and Cambodian riel work everywhere, but carry small bills; breaking a $50 is a headache. Tipping isn't expected but appreciated — round up or leave $1–2 at sit-down restaurants. Cards work at upscale spots; everywhere else is cash only. Dress respectfully if you're eating near pagodas — covered shoulders and knees. And drink bottled water exclusively; skip the ice at small street stalls.

## Day 1: The Old Market Circuit

### Morning: The Pork and Rice That Starts Everything

Be at Old Market area by 6:45 AM, when the breakfast vendors are in full swing and the heat hasn't set in. You're hunting for **Bai Sach Chrouk** — grilled pork over broken rice — and you'll find it at several stalls along the market's outer edges. The pork should be slightly charred, marinated in coconut milk and garlic, served over rice with a bowl of clear broth and pickled vegetables on the side. The best versions have pork that's been grilling since 4 AM. Pull up a plastic stool, point at what the locals are eating, and settle in. You'll spend $2–3 and leave wondering why this isn't famous worldwide.

**Cost: $2–4**

### Afternoon: Market Grazing and a Proper Lok Lak

Spend the late morning wandering Psar Chas (Old Market) itself — not for souvenirs yet, but for orientation. Notice the dried fish section, the palm sugar vendors, the women selling herbs you won't recognize. This is your classroom for the next three days.

For lunch, stay in the Old Market area and find a local shophouse restaurant — the ones with laminated menus and ceiling fans, not the ones with hostesses outside. Order **Lok Lak**: stir-fried beef cubes with a pepper-lime dipping sauce (tuk meric), served over rice with a fried egg on top. The beef should be wok-charred and tender, the sauce sharp and addictive. A cold Angkor beer alongside is non-negotiable.

After lunch, walk through the fresh produce section of the market. Buy a bag of fresh longan or rambutan ($1) and eat it slowly while watching the afternoon commerce. This is when you start to understand how the city feeds itself.

**Cost: $6–10 (lunch with beer and fruit)**

### Evening: Your First Amok, Done Right

For dinner, walk to the quieter end of the Old Market area, away from Pub Street's noise. You need **Fish Amok** — the dish that defines Khmer cuisine — and you want it somewhere that steams it in banana leaves rather than baking it into a rubbery coconut custard. The texture should be silky, almost mousse-like, with lemongrass, galangal, and slok ngor (a slightly bitter local leaf) balancing the coconut richness.

At a mid-range restaurant with air conditioning and proper plating, expect to pay $8–12 for amok, plus $3–5 for a green papaya salad and $2–3 for rice. A bottle of wine runs $15–20 if you're inclined.

**Cost: $15–25 (mid-range dinner)**

**Day 1 Total**: Budget ~$23 | Mid ~$39 | Splurge ~$65

## Day 2: Kandal Village and the Quiet Side

### Morning: Noodles Before the Day Heats Up

Head to **Kandal Village**, the emerging arts and cafe district, around 7 AM. The neighborhood wakes up slower than Old Market — more Cambodian creatives than temple tourists — but the food comes early.

You're looking for **Nom Banh Chok**: thin rice noodles topped with a green fish curry made from pounded lemongrass, turmeric, and galangal, finished with fresh bean sprouts, banana blossom, cucumber, and herbs. This is a breakfast dish that looks delicate but fills you completely. The curry should taste bright and herbaceous, not heavy.

You'll find vendors along the quieter streets around the village — look for the women with massive aluminum pots and stacks of banana leaves. Point, sit, eat. $1.50–2.

Follow it with coffee at one of Kandal's small cafes — Cambodian iced coffee is sweet and strong, and you'll need it.

**Cost: $3–5 (noodles and coffee)**

### Afternoon: A Cooking Class That Actually Teaches

This is your day to cook. Book a half-day class (typically $25–35, running 9 AM to 1 PM or 2 PM to 6 PM) that starts with a market tour. The good ones take you through the wet market sections tourists avoid, explaining every ingredient before you cook.

You'll likely make amok, spring rolls, and a stir-fry — hands-on, with enough technique to replicate at home. The meal you cook becomes lunch, eaten with your classmates while the instructor explains what separates good Khmer food from tourist Khmer food. This is worth every dollar.

**Cost: $25–35 (class includes market tour and lunch)**

### Evening: Dinner in the Residential Calm

After the cooking class, rest through the hottest hours. By evening, take a tuk-tuk to **Wat Bo**, the quieter residential area where boutique guesthouses line tree-shaded streets. The restaurants here cater to long-stay visitors and in-the-know travelers — less theater, more substance.

Order **fresh spring rolls** — rice paper wrapped around shrimp, pork, herbs, and vermicelli, served with peanut sauce — as a starter. For mains, try a Khmer beef stew (samlar machu) or grilled river fish with green mango salad. The pace is slower here, the service warmer, the food more confident.

A proper dinner with drinks runs $18–30 at mid-range spots; $45+ if you're at one of the restored colonial villas that dot the area.

**Cost: $20–35 (relaxed dinner with drinks)**

**Day 2 Total**: Budget ~$48 | Mid ~$73 | Splurge ~$115

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

### Morning: One More Bai Sach Chrouk

Return to where you started. By now you've identified your favorite breakfast stall — maybe it's the one near Old Market's east entrance, or the cart by the river that the tuk-tuk drivers favor. Get your pork and rice one more time. Eat slowly. This is how Siem Reap has started its days for generations.

**Cost: $2–4**

### Afternoon: The Market You Almost Missed

Take a tuk-tuk to **Kompong Khleang** — not for the full village tour, but to see how the fishing communities on Tonle Sap actually eat. This is a less-visited stilted fishing village, about an hour from town, where the fish market operates on a different rhythm than the tourist-facing ones in town.

If you've arranged a local guide, they can help you find the small restaurants that serve whatever came off the boats that morning — grilled fish with dipping sauces, sour fish soup, fresh river prawns if you're lucky. This isn't polished, and that's the point.

Back in town by late afternoon, wander the **Made in Cambodia Market** (evenings only, near Old Market area) — not for food exactly, but for the palm sugar, Kampot pepper, and dried spices you'll want to bring home. Buy the pepper. Trust me.

**Cost: $15–25 (transport, lunch, spices)**

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your final dinner should be the best version of everything you've learned to love. Return to Old Market area or Charles de Gaulle Boulevard — where the upscale hotels and spas line up — and choose the restaurant that matches your budget and ambition.

Order across the menu: one more amok (maybe the prahok version, fermented fish, if you're feeling brave), lok lak cooked over charcoal, morning glory stir-fried with garlic, and sticky rice with palm sugar for dessert. Drink Angkor beer or local palm wine if they have it. Take your time.

At a splurge-level restaurant — white tablecloths, French-trained chefs interpreting Khmer classics — you'll spend $50–80 for the full experience with wine. Mid-range runs $25–35. Budget-wise, you can eat like a king for $12–15 at a local favorite.

**Cost: $15–80 (depending on ambition)**

**Day 3 Total**: Budget ~$32 | Mid ~$64 | Splurge ~$110

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Breakfast ends early**: The best Bai Sach Chrouk vendors sell out by 8:30 AM. Set an alarm or accept mediocre pork.

- **Watch the locals' hands**: At street stalls, if everyone is adding a specific condiment — chili paste, pickled garlic, that mysterious brown sauce — follow their lead.

- **Markets have rhythms**: Fresh produce peaks at 7 AM; cooked food stalls fire up around 10 AM and again at 4 PM. Time your visits accordingly.

- **Amok is not a casserole**: If your fish amok arrives looking like a baked quiche, you're at the wrong restaurant. Proper amok is steamed, silky, and slightly loose.
