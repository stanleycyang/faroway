The pizza arrives at your table still blistering, the crust charred in leopard spots, and you fold it in half the way the old man at the next table does—watching you from the corner of his eye to make sure you get it right. The first bite is hot enough to burn, but you don't care. This is Naples at 1 PM on a Tuesday, and nothing else in the world matters.

I spent three weeks eating through this city, and I left five pounds heavier with zero regrets. Naples doesn't cook to impress you. It cooks because this is what its grandmothers did, and their grandmothers before them, and to change a single ingredient would be a kind of betrayal. The food here is aggressive in its simplicity—tomatoes that taste like tomatoes, mozzarella pulled that morning, dough that's been fermenting longer than your flight. Here's what you need to eat before you leave.

#### Pizza Margherita

Let's start with the obvious, because in Naples, pizza isn't a cliché—it's a religion. The Margherita exists because of a queen. In 1889, pizzaiolo Raffaele Esposito made three pizzas for Queen Margherita of Savoy, and she favored the one with tomato, mozzarella, and basil—the colors of the Italian flag. Whether the story is entirely true doesn't matter. What matters is what lands in front of you: a disc of dough with a rim so soft it collapses when you lift it, pooled with San Marzano tomato sauce that tastes almost sweet, scattered with torn mozzarella di bufala still weeping its milky liquid. The basil wilts into the heat. You eat it with your hands, folded like a wallet, leaning forward so the oil drips onto the table and not your shirt.

Find it at any pizzeria with a wood-fired oven and a line out the door—the historic center is thick with them. Go at lunch, when the dough is freshest and the crowds slightly thinner. Expect to pay €5–€8, which will feel like theft for something this transcendent.

#### Cuoppo Fritto

Walk the narrow streets of the Spanish Quarter around 6 PM, when the fryers are hot and the evening passeggiata is just beginning, and you'll smell it before you see it: a paper cone stuffed with whatever came out of the sea that morning, battered and fried until golden. This is *cuoppo*, Naples' answer to fast food. Inside that cone you might find baby squid, tiny whole fish with crunchy bones, zucchini flowers, potato croquettes, or rice balls called *arancini*. The batter is light, almost lacy, and shatters when you bite through. Underneath, the seafood is impossibly tender.

This is walking food, eaten standing up while locals navigate around you on Vespas. Street vendors and friggitorie (fry shops) throughout the city sell cuoppo for €4–€7, depending on size and what's inside. The best ones have a line of Neapolitans waiting—never trust an empty friggitoria.

#### Sfogliatella

The first time you bite into a sfogliatella, you'll understand why Neapolitans start their day this way. The shell is architectural—hundreds of tissue-thin layers of dough, baked until they shatter like glass. Inside, a filling of sweetened ricotta studded with candied citrus and a whisper of cinnamon. The texture is the thing: crispy, then creamy, then that little resistance of fruit. It should be warm. It should be eaten at a marble counter with an espresso so short and strong it barely fills a thimble.

This pastry was invented in the 17th century by nuns at a monastery on the Amalfi Coast, which explains why eating one feels vaguely spiritual. Head to any historic pasticceria in the city center—the ones with wood-paneled cases and old men in white jackets behind the counter. A sfogliatella costs €2–€3 and will recalibrate your entire understanding of breakfast pastry.

#### Ragù Napoletano

Forget what you know about meat sauce. Neapolitan ragù is not a tomato sauce with meat in it—it's meat that has been braising so long it's become sauce. We're talking six, eight, sometimes twelve hours of gentle simmering: pork ribs, beef rolls stuffed with garlic and parsley (*braciole*), sometimes sausage, all swimming in tomatoes until the meat falls apart at the suggestion of a fork. The result is a sauce that coats pasta (usually *ziti* or *paccheri*) in a clinging, rust-colored gravy so rich it's almost savory-sweet.

Sunday is ragù day. Families gather, pots bubble on stoves across the city, and the smell drifts through open windows into alleyways. You'll find it at old-school trattorias in neighborhoods like Sanità or Quartieri Spagnoli, where the menu is whatever nonna made that morning. A plate of pasta with ragù runs €8–€12, and the meat is served as a second course—you pay for both, and you should eat both.

#### Mozzarella di Bufala

There is mozzarella, and then there is mozzarella di bufala eaten within hours of being pulled. The difference is violence. True buffalo mozzarella, made from the milk of water buffaloes raised in the nearby Campania plains, is soft and yielding, with a porcelain-white skin that tears to reveal a creamy, almost liquid interior. It tastes clean, milky, with a subtle tang that hits the back of your throat. Eaten with nothing but a pinch of salt and maybe a drizzle of olive oil, it's one of the purest pleasures in Italian food.

Cheese shops (*caseifici*) across Naples sell it still swimming in its own whey, and the best versions come from producers in Caserta or Battipaglia. Buy a ball for €4–€6 and eat it immediately—within 24 hours, it starts to lose that ethereal freshness. This is not cheese you save for later.

#### Pasta e Patate

This is poverty food elevated to art. Pasta and potatoes, cooked together in the same pot until the starch from both creates a texture that's neither soup nor solid—what Neapolitans call *azzeccata*, meaning "stuck together." It's humble ingredients doing extraordinary things: short pasta, cubed potatoes, onion, a pork rind for richness, sometimes a little provola cheese melted in at the end. The result is creamy without cream, comforting in a way that's almost aggressive.

This dish doesn't appear on tourist menus. You find it in the working-class trattorias of the Sanità neighborhood, or in family-run places where the handwritten menu changes daily. Go for lunch. Expect to pay €6–€9 for a bowl, and expect to need a nap afterward.

#### Espresso

Coffee in Naples is its own language. You order at the register, take your receipt to the bar, and stand—never sit—while the barista pulls a shot so concentrated it coats the cup like motor oil. Neapolitan espresso is shorter, stronger, and sweeter than Roman or Milanese versions; sugar is assumed unless you specify otherwise. The crema on top is thick and hazelnut-brown. You drink it in two sips, set down the cup, and walk out. The whole ritual takes ninety seconds.

For under €1, this is the cheapest pleasure in Italy. Any bar will do, but the historic cafés along Via Toledo have been perfecting this art for over a century.

#### Babà

If sfogliatella is breakfast, babà is evening. This dome-shaped cake, soaked in rum syrup until it's practically drunk, is soft and spongy and dripping. You pick it up and it compresses slightly, heavy with liquor, then springs back. The flavor is pure: yeast, butter, and a boozy sweetness that warms your chest. Some places add whipped cream or fruit, but purists take it plain, letting the rum do its work.

Babà came to Naples from Poland via France, but the Neapolitans made it their own—bigger, wetter, more extravagant. Find it at pasticcerias for €2–€4, or stuffed into jars of syrup at specialty shops if you want to bring the memory home.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Neapolitans eat late—lunch rarely before 1:30 PM, dinner often after 9 PM. Breakfast is a pastry standing at a bar, never sitting, and absolutely never anything savory. Meals are sacred and slow; if you try to rush, the waiter will look at you with pity. Sharing is uncommon—everyone orders their own pizza, their own pasta. Don't ask for substitutions. Don't ask for parmesan on seafood. Don't photograph every course; eat it while it's hot.

When you order espresso, say *caffè*, not *espresso*—espresso is the default, and asking for it by name marks you as a tourist. Always pay at the register first in a traditional bar. And if someone offers you a taste of something from their plate, you're being honored. Accept.
