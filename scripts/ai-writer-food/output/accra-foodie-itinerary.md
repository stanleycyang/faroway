Three days in Accra taught me that Ghanaians don't just eat—they commune. Every bowl of soup comes with conversation, every grilled fish with a story about where it was caught that morning. I came with a notebook and an empty stomach, and I left with pages of scribbled addresses and the kind of satisfaction that only comes from eating a city properly.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Accra eats late by Western standards—lunch rarely happens before 1pm, and dinner crowds don't peak until 9pm. Cash remains king at market stalls and local spots (Ghanaian cedi only), though upscale restaurants accept cards. Tipping 10% is appreciated but not mandatory at sit-down places. For popular dinner spots, especially on weekends, call ahead or arrive early. And learn to greet respectfully—a simple "good afternoon" before ordering changes everything about your service.

## Day 1: City Center & The Foundations

Your first day is about calibration—learning the baseline flavors that define Accra cooking before you start chasing variations.

### Morning

Start in the City Center with *hausa koko*, the spiced millet porridge that Accra wakes up to. Street vendors begin ladling it around 6am, and by 8am the best pots are scraped clean. The porridge arrives pale pink from the millet, fragrant with ginger and cloves, served with *koose*—black-eyed pea fritters that shatter when you bite through to their soft, peppery centers. Find a vendor near the main market with a line of office workers in pressed shirts; they know who makes it right. The whole breakfast costs around $2–3, and you'll stand while you eat, watching the city accelerate into motion.

### Afternoon

By early afternoon, make your way toward the Central Market for lunch and the kind of sensory immersion that shopping malls have tried to kill worldwide. The market sprawls across blocks, but you're looking for the cooked food section where women run operations that put restaurant kitchens to shame. Order *fufu* with palm nut soup—the pounded cassava and plantain arrives as a smooth, stretchy mound, the soup a rich orange slick with goat meat falling off the bone. You don't chew fufu; you pinch, dip, and swallow. Watching you struggle might earn you an audience, but Ghanaians teach through gentle laughter, not judgment. Budget $5–8 for a portion that renders dinner optional.

Spend the next hour walking the market's edges, sampling as you go: grilled plantains with groundnuts ($1), *kelewele*—spiced fried plantain chunks that hit sweet, salty, and fiery all at once ($1–2). The hidden stalls tucked behind the main thoroughfares often have the most interesting finds, including smoked fish in varieties you won't see in restaurants.

### Evening

Dinner belongs to *jollof rice*, and yes, you will have opinions about the Nigeria-versus-Ghana debate before you leave this city. Head to the Old Town for a proper sit-down plate. The best versions achieve a smoky char on the bottom layer—called "party jollof" because it comes from cooking for crowds over open flames. The rice should stain your fingers orange, the tomato base concentrated to something approaching sweetness. Order it with grilled chicken or fried fish, a side of shito (the black pepper sauce that improves everything), and a cold Club beer. A full dinner runs $15–25 depending on the venue, and you'll leave understanding why this dish starts arguments across West Africa.

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$40 | Splurge ~$75

## Day 2: Waterfront & Smoke

Today you follow the fish—from morning catch to afternoon grill to an evening spent understanding why coastal Ghanaian cooking deserves its own chapter in any serious food history.

### Morning

The Waterfront wakes to fishermen hauling in nets, and the women who buy their catch start cooking almost immediately. Track down *koko* with fresh fish—a simpler, less spiced porridge than the hausa version, served with grilled small fish and a pepper sauce that clears sinuses. This is fisher-family breakfast food, and eating it here, with boats still coming in, connects you to a routine that predates the city itself. Expect to pay $3–5, eaten on a bench if you're lucky, standing if you're not.

### Afternoon

For lunch, push deeper along the coast to where the smoke rises. Grilled tilapia, cooked whole over charcoal until the skin crackles and the flesh stays impossibly moist, represents Accra seafood at its most elemental. The fish comes with *banku*—fermented corn and cassava dough with a sour tang that cuts through the richness—and more of that pepper sauce. A full fish with sides runs $8–12, and you'll eat with your hands, pulling meat from bones, dipping everything in shito.

The afternoon opens up for a cooking class if you want to understand what you've been eating. Several home cooks in residential neighborhoods offer informal sessions where you'll pound fufu, grind peppers, and learn the patience required for proper palm nut soup. Expect to pay $30–50 for a 3-hour session that ends with eating what you've made. Worth every cedi.

### Evening

Dinner moves upscale but stays coastal. The Business District houses restaurants where the seafood comes in clay pots and the presentation matters, but the flavors remain rooted in tradition. Order the grilled lobster or crab in pepper sauce—messy, expensive ($40–60), and absolutely worth it. The Arts Quarter offers slightly more casual alternatives with the same quality seafood, better music, and prices that run $25–35 for a memorable meal. Either way, you're ending the day understanding that Accra's relationship with the ocean goes beyond geography into something closer to identity.

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$70 | Splurge ~$130

## Day 3: The Best of Everything

Your final day is about favorites—returning to techniques you now understand, seeking the best versions, and eating goodbye.

### Morning

Begin in the Old Town with *waakye*, the rice and beans dish that Accra considers proper breakfast fuel. The rice turns reddish-brown from cooking with millet leaves, and the toppings make it a meal: boiled egg, fried plantain, *gari* (cassava granules), spaghetti (trust me), and stewed meat or fish, all drizzled with oil and pepper. The legendary waakye vendors start selling at dawn and finish when the pot empties, usually by mid-morning. Join the queue, accept whatever extras the server suggests, and pay your $3–5 for a breakfast that holds you until late afternoon.

### Afternoon

Spend your final afternoon grazing through a different market—the one locals mention when you say you've "done" the Central Market. The off-the-beaten-path markets in residential neighborhoods sell fewer tourist crafts and more serious provisions, which means better food stalls. This is snacking territory: *bofrot* (sweet puff-puff doughnuts), *chinchinga* (spiced beef skewers), more kelewele, roasted corn with coconut, and whatever else catches your eye. Budget $10–15 for an afternoon of constant eating, and bring your own small bags—vendors sometimes run out.

### Evening

Your final dinner should be the meal you'll describe to friends back home. Return to whichever neighborhood served you best—the Waterfront for seafood, the Arts Quarter for atmosphere, City Center for tradition—and order deliberately. If you haven't yet tried *light soup* with goat, this is the moment: a thin, pepper-forward broth that tastes like pure heat distilled into something drinkable, served with fufu or rice. If you've fallen for jollof, find the place that does it over open flame and order enough for two. This meal runs $20–40 at a proper restaurant, potentially $60+ if you're marking the occasion with drinks and dessert.

End with *sobolo*, the hibiscus drink that Ghanaians reach for when they need to cool down. Sweet, tart, deep red—it's the taste of Accra in a glass.

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~$40 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$120

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Follow the queue**: Ghanaians wait for quality. A line at a market stall almost always means you've found the right vendor.
- **Eat fufu early in your trip**: Your technique improves with practice, and by Day 3 you'll handle it like you belong.
- **Carry small bills**: Exact change smooths every transaction at markets and street stalls.
- **Learn "medaase" (thank you)**: Basic phrases in Twi transform interactions from transactions into connections.
