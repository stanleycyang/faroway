The bandeja paisa arrives at 11:30 in the morning, and it's already almost too much to look at. A ceramic platter the size of a small shield, steam rising from every corner, colors piled on colors — the deep burgundy of beans bleeding into white rice, a fried egg with its yolk still trembling, a strip of chicharrón catching the light like amber glass. This is Medellín on a plate, and you haven't even picked up your fork yet.

#### Bandeja Paisa

Let's start with the meal that defines Antioquia, because everything else in Medellín food culture is, in some ways, a conversation with this dish. The bandeja paisa is not elegant. It was never meant to be. It was built for arrieros — the muleteers who crossed the Andes with coffee and cargo, burning through thousands of calories on mountain trails. They needed fuel that would stick, and stick it does.

The components read like a dare: red beans stewed until creamy, white rice, ground beef cooked with cumin and garlic, a fried pork belly (chicharrón) shattered crisp, a thin fried steak (carne en polvo), morcilla (blood sausage) with rice spilling from its casing, a slice of ripe plantain gone nearly black with caramelization, half an avocado, a fried egg, and an arepa on the side. That's twelve components, minimum. Some places add a wedge of lime. Some add hogao. Nobody adds restraint.

The magic is in eating it together — a bite of beans with a shard of chicharrón, the fat and the starch melding, then the cool green slice of avocado to cut through. The sweet plantain against the iron-rich morcilla. You build your own ratios. You find your rhythm. By the end, you'll be uncomfortably full, and you'll understand why paisas eat this at lunch and call it a day.

Find the best versions in Sabaneta at old-school spots where three generations are eating together. Around $4–7, and worth every centavo.

#### Arepa con Hogao

If bandeja paisa is the symphony, the arepa con hogao is the chord that everything else builds from. Arepas are everywhere in Colombia, but in Medellín, the white corn version — arepa paisa — is thinner, crispier at the edges, and eaten morning, noon, and night.

But the hogao is where it gets interesting. This tomato and scallion sofrito, cooked slow until the onions melt into the tomatoes and the whole thing turns into something jammy and just slightly sweet, transforms a simple corn cake into something deeply satisfying. The arepa itself is neutral, a little earthy, with a faint sweetness from the corn. The hogao brings acid and depth. Add a slice of quesito — fresh, salty white cheese — and you've got breakfast that costs less than a dollar and tastes like someone's grandmother made it.

You'll find these at arepa carts throughout El Centro and Laureles in the mornings, usually around 2,000–4,000 pesos ($0.50–$1). The woman running the cart has probably been making them since before you were born.

#### Sancocho

There is a soup in Medellín that people talk about the way other cities talk about love affairs. Sancocho is a weekend ritual, a hangover cure, a celebration dish, and a comfort food all in one bubbling pot. It's what grandmother makes when the whole family comes over on Sunday. It's what you eat the morning after a night of aguardiente.

The broth is everything. Chicken (sometimes beef, sometimes both) simmers for hours with yuca, potato, corn on the cob, plantain, and whatever else the cook believes in, until the liquid turns golden and slightly viscous from all the starch. The flavor is clean but impossibly deep — you can taste each vegetable's ghost even after they've surrendered themselves to the pot. The chicken falls apart when you look at it.

It's served with white rice on the side (which you're meant to spoon into the soup), a wedge of avocado, and ají — the house hot sauce that every Colombian restaurant makes differently. Some are fruity. Some are scorching. All of them are non-negotiable.

La Paloma in the city center has been serving sancocho since 1952. The line starts early on Sundays. Join it. $5–8 for a bowl that could feed two if you weren't being greedy.

#### Empanadas

Colombian empanadas are different from what you might know. Smaller. Crunchier. Built for eating while walking, while waiting for the bus, while watching a fútbol game on a screen bolted to the wall of a tienda. The shell is made from ground corn, tinged yellow with achiote, fried until it shatters on first bite. Inside: soft potato mashed with seasoned ground beef, or just potato with ají.

The best ones are the ones you don't think about. The cart on the corner of your street. The guy with the glass case near the metro station. The spot in Envigado where they come out of the oil glistening and puffed with steam. You eat them standing up, dipping each bite into ají or lime juice, feeling the oil run down your wrist.

Around 1,000–2,000 pesos each — under a dollar. Buy three. You'll wish you'd bought five.

#### Mondongo

This one separates tourists from travelers. Mondongo is tripe soup — cow's stomach cooked until tender in a thick, rust-colored broth with potatoes, carrots, and peas. It's the opposite of subtle. It's funky and rich and deeply, undeniably offal. But if you let it in, if you eat it at a dim corrientazo restaurant surrounded by construction workers at noon, it becomes something else. Honest food. Working-class food. Food that doesn't apologize.

Order it with a cold Pilsen. The contrast is part of the point. $3–5, served with rice and patacones (fried green plantain).

#### Chicharrón

Separate from the bandeja paisa, chicharrón deserves its own moment. In Medellín, it's pork belly fried twice — first simmered in its own fat, then crisped until the skin turns into something that crackles like fire when you bite through. The meat beneath stays tender, almost sweet. The fat layer between renders out just enough to feel luxurious rather than heavy.

You'll find it piled onto platters at fondas (traditional countryside restaurants) in Sabaneta and throughout the metro area. It's often served with arepa, lime, and hogao. It's not health food. It's joy.

#### Mazamorra con Bocadillo

This is the taste of Antioquian childhood. Mazamorra is whole corn kernels cooked until soft in water that turns milky-white, served cold with a splash of milk and a slab of bocadillo — guava paste, dense and sticky-sweet. You drink the corn kernels. You bite the bocadillo between sips. Sweet and starchy and cold and somehow deeply nostalgic even if you grew up a thousand miles away.

Find it at traditional dessert shops or street vendors in the late afternoon. Around $1–2. Drink it slow.

#### Aguardiente

It translates to "firewater," and for once, the name doesn't lie. Aguardiente is Colombia's national spirit — anise-flavored, sharp, and almost medicinal on the first sip. By the third, you'll understand. By the fifth, you'll be toasting strangers like long-lost family.

In Medellín, Aguardiente Antioqueño is the only acceptable brand. It's served in small shots, often with lime and salt, always in multiples. It's what lubricates every late-night conversation at Parque El Periodista in Laureles, where the bars spill onto the street and nobody leaves before 2 AM.

A bottle runs $10–15. Order it by the shot for $1–2 each. Say "¡Salud!" like you mean it.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is sacred. This is when paisas eat big — the almuerzo corriente (set lunch) is how most people fuel their day, and it's where you'll find the best value: soup, main plate, drink, and sometimes dessert for $3–5. Look for places packed with office workers between noon and 2 PM. If there's a handwritten menu on a chalkboard, even better.

Dinner is lighter, often just arepas or empanadas. Don't fight this. Save your heavy eating for midday. Breakfast is coffee and an arepa, maybe eggs — simple. And at any meal, ask for ají. Every spot makes their own. Tasting each version is half the fun.
