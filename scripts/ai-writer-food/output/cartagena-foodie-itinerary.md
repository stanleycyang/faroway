Three days in Cartagena taught me that the best food here doesn't announce itself — it's ladled from a pot behind a market stall, handed through a window by a grandmother, or tucked inside a fried corn pocket that costs less than a dollar. This is an itinerary built from sweaty walks, wrong turns that ended right, and the kind of eating that makes you skip dinner because lunch broke you in the best way.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cartagena eats late. Lunch stretches from 12:30 to 3pm, dinner rarely starts before 8pm. Carry small bills — street vendors and market stalls won't break a 50,000 peso note. Most sit-down restaurants accept cards, but cash is king everywhere else. Tip 10% at restaurants where you're served; street food requires no tip, just appreciation. Greet everyone with *Buenos días* or *Buenas tardes* — it opens doors and sometimes gets you the better portion.

## Day 1: The Walled City — Colonial Streets, Morning Markets, and Your First Arepa de Huevo

### Morning

Start in **Old Town (Centro)** before the heat arrives and the tour groups flood the plazas. Find a corner stand selling **arepas de huevo** — the fried corn patties stuffed with a whole egg, the yolk still soft if you're lucky. The technique is a two-stage fry: the arepa goes in, comes out, gets filled with raw egg, then goes back in until it puffs and seals. Eat it immediately, standing at the counter, hot oil still glistening on the surface. A squeeze of lime, a shake of ají, and you'll understand why this is the city's essential breakfast. Pair it with a fresh juice — lulo if they have it, its tart citrus unlike anything back home.

**Cost**: $3–5 for arepa + juice

### Afternoon

Walk toward **Mercado Bazurto** for lunch. This isn't the polished tourist market — it's the real thing, sprawling and chaotic, where Cartageneros actually shop. The seafood section will stop you: whole fish on ice, shrimp in mountains, vendors shouting prices. Find one of the comedores (simple lunch counters) in the back and order **cazuela de mariscos** — a coconut milk seafood stew rich with prawns, fish, and crab, served with rice and patacones. The fried green plantain discs arrive crisp and salted, perfect for scooping. The whole meal costs what an appetizer would in the walled city.

This market has no tourist polish. Watch your belongings, follow the locals, and eat what they're eating. The women selling lunch here have been perfecting these recipes for decades.

**Cost**: $8–12 for full lunch with drink

### Evening

Return to **Old Town** cleaned up for dinner. Head to one of the rooftop restaurants overlooking the illuminated church domes — the views of **San Pedro Claver Church** at night are worth the markup. Order ceviche to start: the **ceviche de camarón** here gets made with coconut milk alongside the lime, giving it a creamy sweetness that reads Caribbean rather than Peruvian. For your main, try grilled Caribbean lobster if the budget allows, or a whole fried pargo rojo (red snapper) if you want to eat like a local with money. The fish comes with coconut rice, more patacones, and a tomato-onion hogao sauce.

**Cost**: Budget $25 / Mid $45 / Splurge $90+

**Budget**: ~$40 | **Mid-range**: ~$65 | **Splurge**: ~$110

## Day 2: Getsemaní — Street Art, Street Food, and the City's Creative Heart

### Morning

Cross into **Getsemaní**, the bohemian quarter where murals cover entire blocks and the energy shifts younger, grittier, more awake. Breakfast here happens at small cafés tucked between hostels and art galleries. Order **calentado** — yesterday's rice and beans refried with eggs and topped with hogao, the quintessential Colombian breakfast that fuels a morning of walking. Wash it down with Colombian coffee, which somehow tastes different here than every export you've ever had.

After eating, wander the street art. The murals aren't decoration — they're social commentary, political statements, tributes to Afro-Colombian culture. You'll work up an appetite again.

**Cost**: $6–10 for breakfast

### Afternoon

Lunch in Getsemaní means finding the women selling from windows and carts. Look for **bandeja paisa** — the famous Colombian platter that arrives loaded: red beans, white rice, chicharrón (fried pork belly), chorizo, fried egg, sweet plantain, avocado, and a small arepa for scooping. It's excessive and perfect, a meal that announces Colombia's appetite. Most sit-down spots in Getsemaní serve solid versions, but the best come from family-run places with plastic tables and one daily menu.

Mid-afternoon, find a **palenquera** — one of the Afro-Colombian women in bright dresses carrying fruit bowls on their heads. Buy **cocadas**, the coconut candies she'll have in several flavors: plain, with anise, with fruit. They're sweet, chewy, and iconic to Cartagena. While you're at it, try whatever fruit she's selling that you don't recognize — **maracuyá** (passion fruit), **guanábana** (soursop), or **zapote** if the season's right.

**Cost**: $10–15 for bandeja paisa + snacks

### Evening

Tonight, cook your dinner — or rather, learn to. Several spots in Getsemaní offer cooking classes focused on Caribbean Colombian cuisine. You'll hit a market to shop for ingredients, then return to make ceviche, coconut rice, and often a main like fish in coconut sauce. The class doubles as dinner, and you leave with recipes that actually work back home. Book a day ahead.

If cooking class isn't your speed, find one of Getsemaní's courtyard restaurants for a slower dinner. The neighborhood's best kitchens are doing modern takes on traditional dishes — think ceviche flights, deconstructed arepas, and cocktails with tropical fruit you can't name. After dinner, Plaza de la Trinidad fills with street performers and locals drinking beer on the church steps.

**Cost**: Cooking class $40–60 / Restaurant dinner: Budget $20 / Mid $40 / Splurge $85

**Budget**: ~$45 | **Mid-range**: ~$75 | **Splurge**: ~$160

## Day 3: The Farewell — San Diego's Quiet Corners and the Meal Worth Remembering

### Morning

Finish your Cartagena eating in **San Diego**, the quiet residential corner of the walled city where tourists thin out and the pace slows. Breakfast here is unhurried. Find a café near one of the smaller plazas and order **huevos pericos** — scrambled eggs with tomatoes, onions, and scallions, served with fresh arepa and hogao. It's simpler than yesterday's calentado, gentler on a body that's been eating hard for two days.

Take your coffee slowly. Watch the neighborhood wake up. This is the Cartagena that residents know.

**Cost**: $7–12 for breakfast

### Afternoon

Your final afternoon belongs to snacking and wandering. Return to wherever you ate best over the past two days — maybe that market stall at Bazurto, maybe the palenquera near Plaza de la Trinidad. Buy another arepa de huevo. Get a second helping of cocadas. Find a cart selling **raspao** — shaved ice with fruit syrup and condensed milk — because you'll miss it the moment you leave.

For a proper lunch, seek out a local spot serving **sancocho**, the Colombian soup that varies by region. Here it's often **sancocho de pescado**: a whole fish simmered with yuca, plantain, corn, and potatoes in a broth that tastes like comfort has a flavor. It's a meal that makes you sweat in the Caribbean heat and want more anyway.

**Cost**: $12–18 for lunch + snacks

### Evening

Your last dinner should be your best. Return to **Old Town** for the meal that makes you understand why people fall in love with Cartagena. The high-end restaurants here — the ones in restored colonial mansions with candlelit courtyards — are doing something special with local ingredients. Start with ceviche, move to a perfectly grilled fish or slow-cooked pork, finish with a coconut dessert that nods to the palenqueras without copying them.

This is splurge territory if you're willing, but even mid-range restaurants in the walled city deliver. Order a bottle of something Colombian, eat slowly, and watch the city glow through the bougainvillea.

**Cost**: Budget $30 / Mid $55 / Splurge $120+

### Day 3 Total Food Spend
**Budget**: ~$55 | **Mid-range**: ~$90 | **Splurge**: ~$155

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Eat breakfast early, lunch late**: The best street food happens before 9am when vendors are fresh. Lunch crowds hit around 1pm — arrive at 12:30 for the best selection at market stalls.

- **Follow the construction workers**: At Mercado Bazurto and neighborhood comedores, watch where working Cartageneros eat. They know who serves the biggest portions and the freshest fish.

- **Negotiate fruit, not food**: You can haggle with fruit cart vendors, but at cooked food stalls, the price is the price. Trying to bargain down someone's cazuela is poor form.

- **Carry antacids and hand sanitizer**: Not because the food is unsafe — I never got sick — but because you'll eat more fried food and touch more surfaces than your body expects. Preparation beats regret.
