The ropa vieja arrives still bubbling, and for a moment I just watch the steam curl off the cast iron skillet. It's two in the afternoon at a paladar in Centro Habana, the kind of place where the air conditioning wheezes but the cooking is serious, and this tangle of beef and peppers and slow-cooked onions is about to ruin me for any version I'll ever eat back home. I tear off a piece of bread, drag it through the sauce, and understand immediately why Cubans call this dish the soul of their kitchen.

#### Ropa Vieja

The name translates to "old clothes" — a reference to how the beef shreds into long, ragged strips that supposedly resemble torn fabric. But there's nothing ragged about the flavor. This is beef that's been braised for hours until it surrenders completely, then simmered in a sofrito of tomatoes, cumin, oregano, and enough garlic to wake the dead. The peppers — red and green, sliced into ribbons — add a sweetness that plays against the meat's deep, almost smoky richness. Every forkful carries the weight of a dish that's been perfected across generations.

The origin story traces back to the Canary Islands, brought to Cuba by Spanish settlers, then transformed by the island's ingredients and African cooking influences. It's the national dish for a reason: humble cuts of flank steak made extraordinary through patience and technique. What strikes me most is the sauce — not quite a stew, not quite a braise, but something in between that clings to the beef and begs for rice.

You'll find ropa vieja at virtually every paladar in Havana, but the versions in Centro Habana and Habana Vieja tend to be most consistent. Lunch is prime time, when the pots have been simmering since morning. Expect to pay $8–$12 at a good private restaurant. The state-run places charge less but deliver far less soul.

#### Moros y Cristianos

No plate in Havana is complete without this: black beans and white rice, cooked together so the rice absorbs the inky, earthy liquid from the beans. The name — "Moors and Christians" — references the medieval Spanish conflict, the dark beans representing the Moors, the white rice the Christians. It's a dish born from history and survival, two cheap staples combined into something greater.

What surprises first-time visitors is how flavorful it is. This isn't plain rice and beans. The rice takes on a purple-gray hue and a richness that comes from pork fat, cumin, bay leaf, and oregano simmered into the cooking liquid. The beans are creamy but not mushy, holding their shape against the tender grains. Good moros has a subtle smokiness, a depth that makes it dangerous — you'll eat half the plate before touching your main course.

This is the foundation of Cuban eating, served alongside virtually everything savory. It appears at every meal, every paladar, every home kitchen. $2–$4 as a side. Free with most entrées. Essential always.

#### Lechón Asado

I first encountered proper lechón asado at a family celebration in Regla, across the harbor from Old Havana. The pig had been roasting since before dawn, turning slowly over wood coals until the skin crackled like parchment and the meat inside went soft as butter. Someone handed me a plate piled with meat pulled straight from the shoulder, and the first bite was all I needed to understand why this is the centerpiece of Cuban celebrations.

The magic is in the mojo — a marinade of sour orange juice, garlic (so much garlic), cumin, and oregano that gets rubbed into every crevice of the pig the night before. As the meat roasts, the mojo caramelizes into a sticky, tangy crust while perfuming the flesh beneath. The fat renders slowly, basting everything in its path. What you get is pork that's simultaneously crispy and succulent, tangy and rich, simple and completely addictive.

Whole lechón is traditionally reserved for Christmas, New Year's, and family gatherings. But you can find lechón asado year-round at paladares and the occasional street vendor, served sliced or shredded with rice and beans. Look for places where you can see the meat — ideally still on the bone. Vedado and Miramar have several restaurants known for their roast pork. Prices range from $10–$18 for a full plate.

#### Cuban Sandwich

The first thing you need to know: the Cuban sandwich as perfected in Miami is different from what you'll eat in Havana. Here, it's simpler. Pressed bread — crisp and yielding at once — holding roast pork, ham, Swiss cheese, yellow mustard, and pickles. No salami (that's a Tampa addition). No fancy bread. Just layers of meat and cheese pressed until everything melds together, the fat from the pork mixing with the mustard, the pickles cutting through with their bright acidity.

The texture is what gets me. The outside shatters slightly under your teeth, giving way to that warm, gooey interior where cheese has melted into pork and ham. The pickles stay cool somehow, a shock against the warm meat. It's handheld perfection, best eaten standing at a counter in Old Havana while watching the world shuffle past.

The sandwich emigrated with Cuban exiles in the early twentieth century and evolved in Florida, but its bones remain Havana. Find the best versions at small cafeterias and bakeries rather than tourist-facing restaurants. Calle Obispo has several reliable options. $3–$6 depending on size and location.

#### Tostones

Twice-fried green plantains. That's it. That's the dish. And it's one of the most satisfying things you'll eat in Cuba.

The plantains are sliced thick, fried once until soft, smashed flat with the bottom of a glass, then fried again until golden and crisp. The exterior crackles, the interior stays dense and starchy, and the whole thing gets dunked in mojo — that garlicky, citrusy sauce that appears on seemingly every Cuban table. The combination is elemental: fat, starch, acid, garlic.

Tostones show up everywhere as a side dish, but they're also street food, served in paper cones with extra mojo for dipping. The best ones are fried to order, still glistening with oil, hot enough to burn your fingers. African in origin, brought to Cuba through the slave trade, they've become as Cuban as the palm trees.

$1–$3 anywhere. Essential with lechón. Perfect with beer. Addictive beyond reason.

#### Mojito

You can't write about eating in Havana without writing about drinking in Havana, and the mojito is where the line blurs. This isn't some cloying vacation cocktail — made properly, it's bright, herbaceous, and dangerously easy to drink in the afternoon heat.

White rum, fresh lime juice, sugar, mint leaves muddled just enough to release their oils, topped with soda water and served over ice. The balance should tilt tart, the mint should smell like a garden, and the rum should whisper rather than shout. Hemingway drank his at La Bodeguita del Medio, and the tourists still line up there, but you'll find better versions at smaller bars where the bartender takes pride in the muddling.

The mojito was born in Havana — some say as a medicinal drink in the 1500s, others credit it to the African slaves who worked the sugar cane fields. Whatever the truth, it tastes like Cuba: sweet and sharp and complicated. $3–$5 everywhere. $6–$8 at tourist landmarks. Worth every peso.

#### Tamales Cubanos

Cuban tamales differ from their Mexican cousins — softer, wrapped in corn husks, the masa mixed directly with the filling rather than stuffed. The result is a uniform, porky, slightly sweet bundle that falls apart in your mouth. Cooked in the husk, steamed until tender, they carry the flavor of corn and pork fat in every bite.

Find them at markets in Centro Habana or from vendors who set up outside major plazas in the morning. $1–$2 each. Eat them for breakfast. Eat them as a snack. Eat them because they're there and they're warm and they cost almost nothing.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main meal in Havana — expect restaurants to be busiest between noon and 2pm, with the best dishes often running out by late afternoon. Dinner happens late, rarely before 8pm. Skip the state-run restaurants entirely; paladares are privately owned and operate on a different level of care and flavor.

Sharing is common. Order rice and beans for the table, let everyone pull from the same platters. Ask your server what's freshest — menus don't always reflect what's actually in the kitchen that day due to supply challenges. And always, always drink water between mojitos. The humidity and the rum conspire against tourists who forget.
