# Edinburgh Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Edinburgh

Three days in Edinburgh, one mission: eat everything worth eating. I walked until my legs ached, climbed volcanic hills on a full stomach, and tracked down haggis in centuries-old pubs and Michelin-starred dining rooms alike. Edinburgh doesn't shout about its food the way other cities do, but it rewards the hungry and the curious. Here's the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Scottish restaurants typically serve lunch from noon to 2:30pm and dinner from 6pm onward — don't show up at 4pm expecting a full menu. Tipping 10% is standard at sit-down restaurants; leave it in cash when you can. Cards work everywhere, but some traditional bakeries and market stalls prefer cash. Weekend brunch spots and fine dining require bookings, especially on Fridays and Saturdays. Don't call it "British food" — this is Scotland, and they're proud of it.

## Day 1: Old Town — The Classics Done Right

**Theme:** Royal Mile and Grassmarket — medieval Edinburgh, traditional dishes, the foundation of Scottish eating.

### Morning: Full Scottish Breakfast in Old Town

Start where the city starts: the Royal Mile, that medieval spine running from Edinburgh Castle down to Holyrood Palace. Before you do anything else, you need a Full Scottish Breakfast — and not a sad hotel buffet version. Find a proper café tucked into one of the Old Town's narrow closes (alleyways), where they'll bring you a plate heavy with eggs, back bacon, haggis, black pudding, tattie scone, baked beans, grilled tomato, and toast. The haggis and black pudding are what separate this from any English breakfast — earthy, peppery, satisfying. Take your time. You'll need the fuel.

**What to order:** Full Scottish Breakfast with extra tattie scone
**Neighborhood:** Old Town, near the Royal Mile
**Cost:** £12–18 ($15–22)

### Afternoon: Scotch Pie Hunt and Grassmarket Wander

Walk downhill from the Royal Mile toward Grassmarket, the lively pub square that sits dramatically beneath the castle walls. On the way, stop at a traditional bakery for your first Scotch Pie — a double-crusted meat pie, compact and portable, with mutton or beef filling in a hot-water crust. These aren't pretty, but they're perfect: the kind of hand-held lunch that's powered Scottish workers for centuries. Eat it standing up, like a local.

Spend the afternoon exploring Grassmarket's pubs and shops. For a late lunch or substantial snack, duck into a pub and order Cullen Skink — a creamy smoked haddock and potato soup that's become synonymous with Scottish comfort food. Good Cullen Skink should taste like the sea and the hearth at once, smoky and rich without being heavy.

**What to order:** Scotch Pie from a bakery (£2–4), Cullen Skink at a pub (£8–12)
**Neighborhood:** Grassmarket
**Cost:** £10–16 ($12–20)

### Evening: Haggis Elevated

For dinner, return to the Old Town for haggis done properly — served with neeps (turnips) and tatties (potatoes), doused in whisky sauce, at a restaurant that treats the dish with respect rather than as a tourist curiosity. Book ahead at a cellar restaurant in one of the historic buildings along the Royal Mile, where stone walls and candlelight set the mood. Order haggis as a starter, then Scottish salmon or venison as your main. This is where you begin to understand that Scottish cuisine isn't just hearty — it can be refined.

**What to order:** Haggis with neeps and tatties (starter), pan-seared Scottish salmon (main)
**Neighborhood:** Old Town
**Cost:** £45–70 ($55–85) with drinks

## Day 2: Stockbridge and New Town — Village Vibes and Georgian Elegance

**Theme:** Sunday market grazing, neighborhood cafés, and New Town polish.

### Morning: Stockbridge Farmers Market

If Day 2 falls on a Sunday, start in Stockbridge — a village-like neighborhood just north of the city center that hosts Edinburgh's best farmers market. Wander the stalls collecting breakfast: fresh-baked pastries, local cheeses, Scottish tablet (a crumbly fudge that's dangerously addictive), and hot food from vendors. The market runs from 10am to 5pm, and the morning energy is best. Even if it's not Sunday, Stockbridge's independent bakeries and cafés offer a gentler, more local start to the day than the tourist-packed Royal Mile.

**What to order:** Market grazing — pastries, cheese, Scottish tablet, hot food
**Neighborhood:** Stockbridge
**Cost:** £15–25 ($18–30)

### Afternoon: Dean Village and Smoked Salmon Lunch

From Stockbridge, walk to Dean Village — a hidden waterside hamlet just minutes from Princes Street that most tourists never find. The Water of Leith flows through this impossibly picturesque collection of old mill buildings, and it's the perfect digestive walk after a market morning. 

For lunch, head into New Town — the Georgian grid of elegant streets north of Princes Street Gardens. Find a smart café or bistro and order Scottish salmon, either smoked or pan-seared. Scottish salmon isn't like the stuff you get elsewhere; it's richer, more delicate, worth paying attention to. Pair it with a glass of something crisp and watch the well-dressed locals power through their Saturdays.

**What to order:** Smoked or pan-seared Scottish salmon with seasonal sides
**Neighborhood:** New Town
**Cost:** £18–28 ($22–34)

### Evening: Cooking Class or Fine Dining

Day 2 evening offers a choice. If you want to take Scottish cooking home with you, book an evening cooking class focused on traditional dishes — learning to make haggis or perfecting your Cullen Skink technique. Several schools operate in the city center and include the meal you've made plus wine.

Alternatively, this is your night to splurge on New Town fine dining. George Street and its side lanes hide some of Edinburgh's best restaurants, where Scottish ingredients meet modern technique. Order the tasting menu and let the kitchen show you what they can do with Highland venison, North Sea langoustines, and seasonal Scottish produce.

**What to order:** Tasting menu with wine pairing, or cooking class
**Neighborhood:** New Town
**Cost:** Cooking class £85–120 ($105–150); Fine dining £90–160 ($110–195)

## Day 3: Leith — The Waterfront Farewell

**Theme:** Edinburgh's port neighborhood, seafood excellence, and the meal that makes you miss the city before you've left.

### Morning: Waterfront Breakfast in Leith

Take the bus or walk north to Leith, Edinburgh's historic port district that's transformed into the city's most interesting food neighborhood. Craft breweries, wine bars, Michelin-starred restaurants, and no-nonsense cafés share these waterfront streets. Start with breakfast at a Leith café — simpler than the full Scottish you had on Day 1, but no less satisfying. Think excellent coffee, fresh pastries, maybe a bacon roll or eggs done well.

**What to order:** Coffee, pastries, bacon roll or eggs
**Neighborhood:** Leith
**Cost:** £10–15 ($12–18)

### Afternoon: Seafood and Brewery Hopping

Leith is where Edinburgh meets the sea, and lunch should honor that. Find a waterfront restaurant — several line the Shore, Leith's main dining strip — and order whatever's freshest: mussels, oysters, smoked haddock, or a proper fish supper if you're feeling traditional. The quality here rivals anywhere in the UK.

After lunch, explore Leith's craft brewery scene. Several excellent breweries offer taprooms where you can sample Scottish beer and chat with the brewers. This is Edinburgh's most unpretentious drinking, far from the tartan-draped tourist pubs of the Royal Mile.

**What to order:** Seafood lunch (mussels, oysters, or fish), brewery tasting flight
**Neighborhood:** Leith
**Cost:** £30–45 ($37–55)

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your final Edinburgh dinner should be in Leith, where several restaurants compete for the title of the city's best. Book well in advance — weeks ahead if you're visiting during festival season in August. This is where Scottish produce reaches its peak expression: North Atlantic seafood, Highland game, seasonal vegetables treated with intelligence and restraint. Order Cranachan for dessert — layers of whipped cream, toasted oats, whisky, and Scottish raspberries. It's simple, beautiful, and the right way to end.

**What to order:** Tasting menu or à la carte showcasing Scottish ingredients, Cranachan dessert
**Neighborhood:** Leith
**Cost:** £80–140 ($100–170)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Book Leith dinners early.** The best restaurants fill up weeks in advance, especially Thursday through Saturday. Don't assume you'll walk in.

- **Stockbridge Market is Sunday only.** If your trip doesn't include a Sunday, shift Day 2's itinerary and explore the market stalls inside the city center instead.

- **Ask for Scottish whisky recommendations.** Every decent restaurant and pub has staff who know their single malts. Tell them what flavors you like — smoky, sweet, sherried — and let them guide you. Expect to pay £6–15 per dram for something special.

- **Don't skip the bakeries.** Scotland's baking tradition runs deep, and the unassuming shops turning out Scotch pies, bridies, and empire biscuits are as essential to the food scene as any restaurant. Carry cash for these.
