The arepa de huevo arrives at 7:43 in the morning, still glistening from the fryer, wrapped in a square of brown paper that's already going translucent with grease. I'm standing on a corner in Getsemaní, the sun not yet brutal, and when I tear into that golden shell, the egg inside is somehow still soft, the yolk running lazy fingers down my wrist. This is breakfast in Cartagena — urgent, messy, and so good it makes you close your eyes on a public street.

I spent three weeks eating through this city, sweating through cotton shirts, chasing the palenqueras with their fruit bowls balanced like crowns, and learning that the best meals here often cost less than a decent coffee back home. Cartagena feeds you like it wants you to stay.

---

#### Arepas de Huevo

The genius is in the engineering. A corn masa patty gets fried once, then sliced open with surgical precision so a raw egg can slip inside, then sealed and fried again until the whole thing puffs into a crispy, egg-stuffed miracle. The exterior shatters. The interior is soft corn studded with a perfectly cooked egg — sometimes they add ground beef, sometimes cheese, but the classic is just corn and egg, an exercise in restraint that makes you understand why this dish has survived centuries.

This is coastal Colombian soul food, born from African and Indigenous traditions mixing along the Caribbean. The areperas — women who've been frying these since before dawn — set up their stations in the early morning hours, and by 9 AM the good ones are sold out.

Find them in Getsemaní near Plaza de la Trinidad, or hunt down the carts near Mercado Bazurto if you want the rawest, most authentic experience. Expect to pay around $1–$2, or 5,000–8,000 COP. Eat it standing. Let the yolk drip. Order a second one.

#### Ceviche de Camarón

The carts appear around midday, usually pushed by men in baseball caps who know exactly how long their shrimp will stay perfect in this heat. The ceviche here isn't like the Peruvian version — it swims in a creamy sauce made from leche de coco and hit with enough lime to make your lips pucker. The shrimp are small, pink, sweet, and impossibly tender, sitting in their coconut bath with a few rings of red onion and maybe some diced tomato.

You eat it from a plastic cup with a plastic fork, standing in the shade of a colonial archway while the Caribbean humidity wraps around your shoulders. This is the coast talking — the confluence of African, Indigenous, and Caribbean influences that defines Cartagena's food identity. The coconut milk is the African contribution, a flavor profile brought by enslaved peoples who made this coast their home.

Carts cluster near the Torre del Reloj and along the walls in the Old Town. Around $3–$5 (12,000–20,000 COP) gets you a generous cup. Some vendors will let you add hot sauce — take them up on it.

#### Bandeja Paisa

This is not a Cartagena dish. Let's be clear: bandeja paisa comes from the Antioquia region, from Medellín and the coffee country. But you'll find it everywhere in Colombia, including here, and it's worth understanding because it represents the country's relationship with abundance.

The platter arrives and you wonder if they meant to feed three people. A mound of red beans stewed until creamy. White rice, always white rice. A fried egg with crispy lace edges. A sweet-savory slice of plantain, caramelized and sticky. Avocado, impossibly green. Chicharrón — pork belly fried until it crackles like static. Chorizo, greasy and paprika-red. Ground beef cooked with onions. An arepa on the side.

It's a harvest celebration on a plate, created by farmers and muleteers who needed enough calories to work the steep mountain terrain. That it migrated to the coast says everything about Colombia's internal migrations and the way food follows people.

Find it at local restaurants outside the touristy walled city — Bocagrande has solid options, as does the neighborhood around Mercado Bazurto. Budget around $8–$15 (35,000–60,000 COP). Come hungry or bring a friend who isn't afraid of commitment.

#### Patacones con Hogao

Green plantains, sliced thick, fried once until softened, smashed flat with the bottom of a glass, then fried again until golden and crisp around the edges. That second fry is crucial — it creates a surface that's crunchy enough to handle the weight of hogao without going soggy.

Hogao is the Colombian sofrito: tomatoes and onions cooked slow with garlic and cumin until they collapse into a sauce that's sweet, savory, and just slightly acidic. It spoons over the patacones in a generous puddle, soaking into the cracks but never quite defeating that crunch.

This is peasant food elevated, the kind of dish that costs almost nothing but delivers everything. The plantain is the base, the hogao is the soul. Some places add cheese, shredded beef, or even ceviche on top, but start with the classic.

Street vendors in Getsemaní sell them from carts for around $1–$2 (4,000–8,000 COP). Restaurant versions run $3–$6. Eat them as a snack, a side, or — let's be honest — a full meal if you order three.

#### Cocadas

The palenqueras are impossible to miss: Afro-Colombian women in explosively colorful dresses, carrying metal bowls on their heads or balanced on one hip. Inside those bowls: cocadas, the coconut candies that taste like the Caribbean distilled into a single bite.

The texture varies — some are chewy, almost like taffy; others are firm and crumbly. The base is fresh coconut cooked with sugar until it transforms into something sweet enough to make your teeth tingle but balanced with that natural coconut fat. They come in colors: the natural white, pale pink from grenadine, or deep brown from panela (unrefined cane sugar) or chocolate.

These candies carry history. The palenqueras are descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped and formed free settlements called palenques — San Basilio de Palenque, an hour from the city, was the first free African town in the Americas. Buying a cocada is buying from a tradition of resistance and resilience.

You'll find palenqueras throughout the walled city and Getsemaní. Prices range from $0.50–$2 per candy depending on size and quantity. Buy several flavors. Tip generously.

#### Fresh Tropical Fruit

The fruit carts stop you in your tracks. Stacked plastic cups of mango drizzled with lime and salt. Chunks of papaya so orange they look digitally enhanced. And then the fruits you've maybe never seen: lulo, with its tangy citrus flavor perfect for juice. Maracuyá (passion fruit), tart and fragrant. Guanábana (soursop), creamy and tropical with a flavor somewhere between pineapple and strawberry.

The vendors in Mercado Bazurto will cut fruit to order, handing you a bag of something so fresh it's practically still warm from the sun. Outside the market, in the tourist zones, carts offer pre-cut cups with lime and sometimes chili salt on the side. Both experiences are worth having.

This is Colombia's geography at work — the country spans coastline, jungle, and high mountain, producing an absurd variety of fruit that most of the world never sees. A morning spent at Bazurto tasting your way through the fruit stalls will cost you maybe $5–$10 (20,000–40,000 COP) and will recalibrate your understanding of what fruit can be.

#### Cazuela de Mariscos

When the sun drops and the walled city starts to glow, the seafood comes out. Cazuela de mariscos is the showpiece: a clay pot filled with shrimp, fish, octopus, clams, and whatever else the coast delivered that morning, all swimming in a creamy coconut broth tinted orange from achiote.

The broth is the thing. Slightly sweet from the coconut, rich from the seafood giving up its essence, with a whisper of heat from ají peppers. You sop it up with bread or rice, you lean over the pot because you don't want to miss a drop, and you remember that this city was a major colonial port where African, Spanish, and Indigenous cuisines crashed into each other and produced this.

Find it at restaurants throughout the Old Town and Getsemaní — ask for places that locals frequent, not just the ones with English menus. Budget $12–$25 (50,000–100,000 COP) for a proper cazuela.

---

#### Limonada de Coco

Not a food, but essential. Fresh lime juice, coconut milk, sugar, blended with ice until frothy and served in a glass so cold it sweats immediately in the humidity. It's sweet, it's tart, it's rich, and it's the only thing that will save you from the 2 PM heat when you've been walking the walls for hours.

Every restaurant and many street vendors serve it. Around $2–$4 (8,000–15,000 COP). Order one, then order another.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main event. Colombians eat their biggest meal between noon and 2 PM — restaurants offer "menú del día" or "corrientazo" set lunches that include soup, a main plate, drink, and sometimes dessert for remarkably little (often under $5). Dinner is lighter and later, around 8 PM.

Don't be shy at market stalls or street carts. Point, smile, and say "uno, por favor." Most vendors are proud of their work and will serve you generously. Sharing is natural — platters like bandeja paisa are meant to be passed around. And always greet with "buenos días" or "buenas tardes" before ordering; skipping the greeting feels abrupt here.

Finally, carry small bills. Vendors often can't break a 50,000-peso note, and you don't want the best arepa of your life slipping away because of math.
