# Fiji Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The smoke hits you first—sweet, earthy, and unmistakably primal. Somewhere beneath the soil, wrapped in banana leaves and buried over hot volcanic stones, tonight's dinner has been cooking since dawn. You're standing barefoot on a village lawn in the Coral Coast, watching a lovo pit being unearthed, and the smell of slow-roasted pork mingling with coconut-steamed taro leaves is making your stomach audibly growl. This is Fijian food at its most honest: no plating, no pretense, just fire, earth, and 333 islands' worth of Pacific abundance.

## The Soul of Fijian Food

Fiji's cuisine tells the story of two cultures colliding in the most delicious way possible. Indigenous Fijian cooking—rooted in earth ovens, coconut milk, and ocean harvests—meets the spice-laden legacy of Indo-Fijian immigrants who arrived as indentured laborers in the late 1800s. The result is a table where kokoda (Fijian ceviche) sits comfortably next to a fiery lamb curry with torn pieces of fresh roti.

The Pacific provides the protein: mahi-mahi, walu (Spanish mackerel), mud crabs, and the prized lobster that fishermen pull from reef edges at dawn. The volcanic soil delivers the starch: cassava, taro, breadfruit, and sweet potato in varieties you didn't know existed. And the coconut palm—revered here as the tree of life—contributes everything from the cream that enriches every traditional dish to the water that hydrates you between meals.

Eating in Fiji is communal, generous, and unhurried. Meals stretch for hours. Portions assume you've been swimming all day (you probably have). And "Fiji time" means your food arrives when it arrives—which gives you ample opportunity to work through a few shells of kava with the locals.

## Must-Try Dishes

**Kokoda** is the dish you'll remember long after your tan fades. Raw walu or mahi-mahi, diced and marinated in fresh lime juice until the acid "cooks" the flesh opaque, then folded into rich coconut cream with onion, tomato, and a whisper of chili. The texture is silky, the flavor bright and fatty at once—served chilled in a coconut shell, it's the Pacific's answer to ceviche, and arguably superior. Find it at resort restaurants across Denarau Island, but the best versions come from family-run spots in Nadi where the fish was swimming that morning.

**Lovo** isn't a dish; it's an event. Meats, whole fish, and root vegetables are wrapped in banana leaves and buried in an earth oven lined with white-hot stones. Hours later, the pit is excavated to reveal pork that falls apart at a glance, chicken infused with smoky sweetness, and palusami—parcels of taro leaves drenched in coconut cream. The Coral Coast resorts stage lovo nights for tourists, but the real magic happens at village feasts, where the unveiling becomes ceremony.

**Rourou** appears at nearly every traditional meal: young taro leaves simmered in coconut milk until they collapse into something between creamed spinach and the most comforting vegetable dish you've ever eaten. The leaves retain a slight mineral edge that plays against the fat, and you'll find yourself spooning it over everything.

**Fiji-Indian Curry** deserves equal billing. The Indo-Fijian population brought their spice boxes and perfected them over generations. Goat curry, lamb curry, fish curry—served with roti so flaky it shatters, or on mountains of basmati rice. The heat builds slowly, fragrant with cumin, coriander, and fenugreek. Nadi is ground zero for this tradition; the curry houses near the market will ruin you for takeaway back home.

**Cassava Cake** closes every proper Fijian meal. Grated tapioca bound with coconut milk and sugar, baked until the edges caramelize and the center stays dense and chewy—think somewhere between mochi and bread pudding. Sweet without being cloying, satisfying without being heavy. You'll find it everywhere, but the homemade versions at village visits taste like someone's grandmother loves you.

## Street Food Scene

Fiji's street food heartbeat pulses strongest in Nadi, particularly around the main market and bus station. This isn't Instagram-ready night market territory—it's workers grabbing lunch, taxi drivers fueling up between fares, and you, the curious traveler, pointing at whatever smells best.

Look for the curry stalls serving thick dhal and vegetable curries ladled over rice for under FJ$10. The Indian snack vendors slinging samosas, puri (fried bread), and bhajia (vegetable fritters) that arrive blistering hot and disappear in three bites. Chinese-Fijian takeaway counters offer chow mein and sweet-and-sour pork that locals have loved for decades.

Timing matters: street food peaks at lunch, roughly 11am to 2pm. Early morning brings the freshest produce and baked goods—look for coconut buns and roti parcels. By late afternoon, the best stuff is gone. Come hungry, carry small bills (vendors rarely have change for large notes), and don't hesitate to ask "what's good today?" in a country where everyone speaks English and genuinely wants to feed you well.

## Restaurants by Budget

**Budget (Under FJ$30/meal)**

In Nadi, your money stretches furthest at the Indo-Fijian curry houses and Chinese-Fijian canteens clustered near the market. FJ$15 gets you a thali-style plate with curry, rice, roti, and pickles that could fuel a full day of island hopping. The ambiance is fluorescent-lit and no-frills, the portion sizes aggressive, and the flavors uncompromising. Try Mama's Pizza for surprisingly excellent Indo-Fijian pizza hybrids, or any of the nameless curry spots where the lunch rush tells you everything about quality.

**Mid-Range (FJ$60–$120/meal)**

The Coral Coast resorts offer buffets and à la carte menus that balance local ingredients with international technique. Expect fresh kokoda alongside grilled reef fish, lovo-style meats, and solid Indo-Fijian options. Port Denarau's marina restaurants serve respectable seafood in pleasant settings—you'll pay more for the waterfront view, but the tuna steaks and garlic prawns deliver. Budget around FJ$80–100 for a full meal with a drink or two.

**Splurge (FJ$150+/meal)**

The private island resorts—Laucala, Likuliku, Tokoriki—serve food that rivals anywhere in the Pacific. We're talking seven-course tasting menus with indigenous ingredients reimagined through fine dining technique. Kokoda deconstructed into an amuse-bouche. Lovo-smoked meats with contemporary plating. Wagyu beef flown in weekly. These meals run FJ$300–500 easily, but when you're watching the sun set over the Mamanucas from a candlelit overwater deck, the credit card damage feels almost reasonable.

## Vegetarian & Vegan Options

Fiji is surprisingly accommodating for plant-based eaters, thanks largely to the Indo-Fijian population's Hindu heritage. Vegetarian curries—dhal, chana masala, palak paneer, vegetable korma—appear on nearly every menu. Rourou (those coconut-braised taro leaves) is naturally vegan and ubiquitous. Cassava, breadfruit, and taro provide starchy substance at every lovo feast.

Vegans will need to specify "no cream, no ghee" at curry houses, as coconut cream and clarified butter appear liberally. Resort restaurants increasingly offer dedicated vegan menus—request them in advance for the best options. The tropical fruit alone could sustain you: papaya so ripe it's almost savory, pineapple that tastes like sunshine crystallized, coconuts cracked fresh from the tree. For strict vegans, Nadi offers the most variety; outer islands default to whatever's available, which usually includes fish.

## Food Markets & Neighborhoods

**Nadi** is the culinary gateway, and the Nadi Municipal Market is its beating heart. Open daily but liveliest on Saturday mornings, the market sprawls with produce stalls, spice vendors, and prepared food counters. Buy kava to bring home, pick up a bag of cassava chips, and watch locals haggle over the morning's catch. The streets surrounding the market hold the best budget eats—curry houses, Chinese takeaways, and bakeries selling coconut buns warm from the oven.

**Denarau Island** is resort territory, which means polished but sanitized. The marina complex at Port Denarau offers waterfront dining with more variety than quality—fine for a casual lunch, but not where you'll find the soul of Fijian cooking.

**The Coral Coast** stretches along Viti Levu's southern shore, resort after resort punctuated by villages. This is lovo country—the cultural village visits here include feasts that contextualize the food within Fijian tradition. Stop at roadside fruit stalls for the ripest mango you'll ever eat.

**The Yasawa Islands** are remote, which means your resort handles all meals. The upside: fish caught that morning, produce from village gardens, and nowhere to be except the dining table. The downside: no spontaneous food adventures—what they serve is what you eat.

## Dining Etiquette

When invited to a village meal—and you should seek this experience—certain courtesies apply. Remove your hat and sunglasses before entering; both are considered disrespectful to your hosts. Dress modestly, covering shoulders and keeping your voice measured. Sit cross-legged on woven mats; pointing your feet at the food or the chief is poor form.

Kava ceremony participation is non-negotiable if offered. Accept the coconut shell with both hands, drink the earthy, mildly numbing liquid in one gulp, clap three times, and say "bula." The taste is muddy and vaguely peppery—you won't love it, but you'll respect the ritual.

At restaurants, tipping isn't expected but is appreciated for good service—10% is generous by local standards. Meals move at Fiji time, which is to say slowly. Order everything at once, settle into your seat, and embrace the pace. The food arrives when the kitchen is ready, and rushing signals that you've missed the entire point of island life.
