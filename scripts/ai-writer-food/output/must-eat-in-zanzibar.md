The smoke finds you before the food does. It's barely 6 PM in Forodhani Gardens, and the charcoal grills are already throwing clouds of spiced heat into the salt air. A vendor slides a Zanzibar pizza off his griddle, folds it once, twice, and hands it over wrapped in newspaper. The first bite is chaos — runny egg, minced beef, crispy dough, a slick of chili sauce — and suddenly you understand why this waterfront transforms into the most intoxicating night market in East Africa every single evening.

Zanzibar sits at a crossroads of trade routes that brought Arab merchants, Indian spice traders, Persian sailors, and Portuguese colonizers to these shores. Each one left something in the pot. The result is a cuisine that tastes like nowhere else: coconut and cardamom, tamarind and turmeric, fire and sweetness colliding on every plate.

I spent three weeks eating through this archipelago — from the labyrinthine alleys of Stone Town to quiet fishing villages where octopus dries on racks in the afternoon sun. Here's what you need to eat.

#### Zanzibar Pizza

Let's be clear: this is not pizza. It's a thin, stretchy dough pulled across a hot griddle, filled with whatever you point at — minced meat, mayonnaise, cheese, onions, peppers, a whole raw egg cracked directly onto the surface — then folded into a crispy, greasy, completely addictive square. The texture is somewhere between a stuffed paratha and a French crêpe that spent a semester abroad and came back different.

The Zanzibar pizza was invented right here in Forodhani Gardens, born from vendors improvising for tourists who wanted something familiar but got something better. Now it's claimed as local pride, served from dozens of nearly identical carts along the waterfront.

Go at sunset. The vendors are still setting up, the crowds haven't peaked, and you can watch yours being made — the dough spinning, the fillings piled high, the whole thing hissing against the griddle. Order the "mix" for the full experience. Expect to pay around $3–4, which feels like theft for this much flavor.

#### Pilau

If Zanzibar had a national dish, pilau would win by a landslide. This isn't plain rice with spices thrown on top — it's rice that has absorbed an entire spice cabinet from the inside out. Cloves, cumin, cardamom, black pepper, cinnamon: each grain is stained golden from turmeric and slicked with ghee, studded with tender chunks of beef or goat that have been braising for hours.

Pilau came to East Africa through Arab and Indian Ocean trade networks, but Zanzibar made it its own. The island grows the spices that define this dish — you can visit the farms where cloves still hang heavy on the branch — so pilau here tastes richer, more aromatic, more *itself* than anywhere else on the Swahili coast.

Find it at any local restaurant, but the best versions come from places with no menu, just a woman stirring a massive pot in the back. Lunch is prime pilau time, served with a simple tomato-onion salad called kachumbari and a squeeze of lime. Around $2–3 for a heaping plate that will fuel you through the afternoon heat.

#### Octopus Curry

The octopus here doesn't come frozen from some industrial port. It comes from the women you see at low tide in Jambiani and Paje, walking the exposed reef flats with buckets and spears, pulling octopus from rocky crevices the way their grandmothers did. By evening, that same octopus is simmering in a clay pot somewhere, transformed.

The curry is rich with coconut milk, golden from turmeric, warmed by ginger and garlic. But the octopus is the revelation — tender, almost buttery, with that faint brininess of the Indian Ocean still present. It's the kind of dish that makes you go quiet for a moment.

Look for it at beach restaurants in the fishing villages on the east coast, where the catch-to-kitchen timeline is measured in hours. Jambiani and Paje have excellent versions. Pair it with coconut rice and cold Kilimanjaro beer. Budget $8–12 at a casual beach spot, more at resort restaurants that add the ambiance tax.

#### Urojo (Zanzibar Mix)

This is the dish that confuses everyone on first encounter. Urojo is a soup, except it's also a salad, except it's also a snack, except it's served in a cup with a toothpick. Stone Town locals slurp it down at odd hours — mid-morning, late afternoon — from vendors who ladle it out of massive pots with the efficiency of a well-rehearsed ballet.

The base is a tangy, yellow-green broth made from gram flour and spiced with cumin and chili. Into this goes a riot of textures: crispy bhajia (fritters), boiled potato, shredded mango, crunchy cassava chips, a drizzle of coconut chutney. Every spoonful is different from the last — sour, crunchy, creamy, hot.

Urojo traces back to Zanzibar's Indian community, but it's been remixed into something entirely local. Find it at Darajani Market in the mornings, or from street vendors near the Old Fort in Stone Town. It costs almost nothing — around $0.50–1 — and delivers more complexity than dishes ten times its price.

#### Mandazi

Every morning in Zanzibar starts the same way: with mandazi and chai. These triangular fried doughs look like doughnuts that decided to embrace a more angular lifestyle, golden brown and slightly puffed, with a hint of coconut and cardamom in the crumb. They're not overly sweet — just enough to make them dangerous alongside a cup of spiced tea.

Mandazi arrived via the Indian Ocean trade routes, cousins to the Indian maida-based snacks that traveled with merchants centuries ago. But here they've become breakfast, snack, and comfort food all at once, sold from bakeries and street corners by 6 AM.

The best mandazi are still warm when you buy them, the outside slightly crisp, the inside soft and yielding. Find them at any tea house or street stall in the morning hours. Around $0.20–0.30 each, which means you'll inevitably buy too many.

#### Spice Tea (Chai ya Tangawizi)

The chai here does not come from a bag. It comes from a pot that's been simmering for hours, dark as mahogany, thick with milk and heavy with spices that grow within miles of where you're sitting. Cloves, cinnamon, cardamom, ginger — the same aromatics that made Zanzibar wealthy enough to build Stone Town in the first place.

Drinking spice tea in Zanzibar feels like tasting the island's history. For centuries, these spices were currency, drawing traders from Arabia, India, and Europe. Now they're in every kitchen, every tea house, every morning ritual.

Order it at any tea stall — there's usually a man with a kettle and a few plastic chairs — and watch him pour from high above the cup, aerating the tea into a frothy cascade. Sweeten to taste with sugar. Around $0.30–0.50. Drink slowly.

#### Mishkaki

Think of mishkaki as Zanzibar's answer to the kebab, except better. Cubes of marinated beef or goat threaded onto wooden skewers, grilled over charcoal until the edges char and the fat renders into smoky rivulets. The marinade is usually simple — garlic, cumin, lime — but the smoke does most of the talking.

You'll find mishkaki wherever there's a grill and a crowd. Forodhani Gardens at night is the obvious choice, but don't overlook the smaller street vendors in Stone Town's back alleys, where the smoke is thicker and the skewers cost even less. Around $1–2 per skewer. Order several.

#### Coconut Bean Soup (Supu ya Maharage)

This is poor-people food, comfort food, the kind of dish nobody puts on a tourism poster but everyone eats when they need something warm and filling. Red kidney beans simmered until they start to fall apart, enriched with coconut milk, brightened with tomatoes and onions, served with a hunk of bread or over rice.

It's the taste of Zanzibar's agricultural heartland, where coconut palms and bean fields stretch inland from the coast. You won't find it in fancy restaurants. You'll find it in local eateries where the menu is whatever's in the pot today. Around $1–2 for a bowl that sticks to your ribs.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Zanzibar runs on a different clock. Breakfast happens early — by 7 AM, the mandazi and chai are flowing. Lunch is the main event, heavy and spiced, best followed by a rest during the hottest hours. Dinner arrives late, often after 8 PM, especially in Stone Town.

Forodhani Gardens operates on its own schedule: vendors start setting up around 5:30 PM, but the real action begins at sunset and runs until the crowds thin around 10 PM. Walk the full row before committing — prices are similar everywhere, but quality varies. Point at what looks good; language barriers dissolve when everyone's hungry.

Eat with your right hand when utensils aren't offered — tear bread, scoop rice, gesture for more. Share plates when invited. And always, always accept the offer of chai. It's hospitality in liquid form.
