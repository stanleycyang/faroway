The first bite of cuy arrives at your table still crackling, its skin so crispy it shatters against your teeth like caramelized glass. It's 8 PM in a stone-walled restaurant off Plaza de Armas, and the altitude has sharpened your hunger into something primal. You didn't expect to feel reverent eating guinea pig, but here you are — tearing into meat that tastes like the smokiest, most intensely flavored dark chicken you've ever encountered, while the Cusqueña beer in your hand sweats in the cool Andean air.

Cusco doesn't just feed you. It initiates you.

---

#### Cuy — The Dish That Tests Your Bravery and Rewards Your Trust

Let's address the obvious: yes, it's guinea pig. And yes, it arrives whole — splayed flat, limbs extended, looking undeniably like what it is. But cuy is not a tourist gimmick. Andean families have raised these animals for over 5,000 years, and in Cusco, serving cuy is an act of celebration, reserved for weddings, festivals, and honoring guests.

The preparation is simple: rubbed with cumin, garlic, and local herbs, then roasted in a clay oven or spit-fired until the skin turns mahogany and impossibly crisp. The meat beneath is dense and succulent, carrying an earthy richness that falls somewhere between rabbit and free-range chicken thigh. You'll find yourself picking the bones clean, chasing every morsel.

The best cuy comes from **chicherías** and traditional restaurants in the **San Blas neighborhood** or the outskirts of the city, where families have perfected the technique over generations. Expect to pay **$15–$25** for a whole cuy, often served with potatoes cooked in the drippings. Order it at least an hour in advance — this isn't fast food.

#### Lomo Saltado — When China Met the Andes

If Cusco has a comfort food that everyone agrees on, it's lomo saltado. The dish is a love letter to immigration — specifically the wave of Chinese workers who arrived in Peru in the 19th century and brought wok technique to Andean ingredients. The result is pure alchemy.

Strips of beef sirloin hit a screaming hot pan with red onions and tomatoes, get kissed with soy sauce and a splash of vinegar, then land on a plate still sizzling. But here's the Peruvian twist: those golden, slightly soft french fries get tossed right into the stir-fry, absorbing all those caramelized juices until they're half-crispy, half-saturated with umami. A mound of white rice sits alongside, ready to soak up everything else.

The beef should be slightly pink inside. The onions should still have bite. The whole thing should arrive making noise.

You'll find lomo saltado everywhere — from **San Pedro Market** food stalls to upscale restaurants — but some of the most satisfying plates come from no-frills **menú del día** spots frequented by taxi drivers and construction workers. Look for the lunch rush. **Around $4–$8** gets you a portion generous enough to fuel an afternoon of exploring Sacsayhuamán.

#### Alpaca Steak — Lean, Tender, and Surprisingly Refined

Alpaca tastes nothing like you'd expect from a fluffy camelid that looks like it wandered out of a children's book. The meat is remarkably lean — lower in fat than beef or chicken — yet somehow stays tender and silky when cooked properly. The flavor sits in delicate territory: mildly gamey, slightly sweet, with an iron-rich depth that recalls venison.

Traditional preparations often involve slow-braising alpaca in stews with ají panca peppers, creating mahogany-colored braises that warm you from the inside out on cold Cusco nights. But modern Cusqueño restaurants have embraced the grilled alpaca steak, seared medium-rare and served with Andean potato varieties you won't find anywhere else on earth.

Head to the restaurants around **Plaza de Armas** or the growing contemporary dining scene for beautifully plated alpaca. Budget **$12–$20** for a solid steak. The meat pairs beautifully with a glass of Argentine Malbec, which most restaurants carry.

#### Choclo con Queso — Giant Corn, Fresh Cheese, Pure Simplicity

Some dishes achieve perfection through complexity. Choclo con queso achieves it through restraint.

Andean corn kernels are enormous — easily three times the size of what you'd find in a North American supermarket — with a starchy, almost nutty sweetness that standard corn can only dream of. The kernels are boiled until just tender, still slightly toothy, then served warm alongside a thick slab of queso fresco: fresh, mild, slightly squeaky cheese that melts just a bit from the corn's heat.

You eat them together. A kernel of corn, a bite of cheese. The simplicity is meditative.

Women sell choclo con queso from baskets in **San Pedro Market** starting around mid-morning. You'll see businessmen in suits standing next to backpackers, everyone eating the same thing with their hands. **$1–$2** buys you an ear that will spoil you for any other corn forever.

#### Sopa de Quinoa — The Soup That Restores You

Altitude will humble you. At 3,400 meters, even walking uphill becomes an event. Enter sopa de quinoa: the restorative, warming, almost medicinal soup that Cusqueños turn to when their bodies need rebuilding.

The base is often a light chicken or vegetable broth, golden and fragrant. Quinoa — still chewy, not mushy — floats alongside chunks of potato, carrot, and whatever greens the cook found that morning. Some versions include a whisper of ají amarillo for gentle heat. Others drop in a handful of fresh herbs at the last moment, releasing a grassy brightness into the steam.

The soup arrives in wide bowls at **market comedores** (food courts) and family-run restaurants, always with a spoon and a sense of purpose. You'll find yourself craving it after long hikes, after sleepless adjusting-to-altitude nights, after any moment when your body asks for something gentle and nourishing. **$2–$4** for a bowl that will genuinely make you feel better.

#### Chicha Morada — The Purple Corn Drink That Becomes an Obsession

It's not juice. It's not soda. It's something harder to categorize and easier to become addicted to.

Chicha morada starts with purple corn — deep violet ears that turn their cooking water into something resembling liquid amethyst. The corn simmers with cinnamon sticks, cloves, pineapple rind, and sometimes a drift of apple peel. Once strained and sweetened, the drink is served cold, and the first sip delivers: fruit-forward sweetness balanced by warm spice, with a subtle earthiness that reminds you this came from the ground.

You'll find chicha morada at virtually every restaurant, market stall, and family table in Cusco. The plastic pitcher on the table at lunch. The glass bottle in the corner store. The ladle-served portion at the market. Drink it constantly — it's refreshing, hydrating, and genuinely delicious. **$0.50–$2** depending on setting.

#### Rocoto Relleno — Stuffed Peppers with Fire and Sweetness

The rocoto looks innocent enough — round and red, resembling a small bell pepper. Then you bite into it and discover that this Andean pepper carries a heat that rivals habaneros. But Cusqueños don't fear the rocoto; they stuff it.

The classic preparation involves hollowing out the pepper, parboiling it to tame (slightly) its fire, then filling it with a picadillo of ground beef, raisins, olives, and hard-boiled egg. Topped with melted cheese and baked until bubbling, the finished dish delivers a symphony of flavors: spice, sweetness, meatiness, and the unexpected pop of olive brine.

Find rocoto relleno at traditional **picanterías** and in the back-corner restaurants of San Pedro Market. **$5–$10** for a plate that usually includes papas doradas.

---

#### Anticuchos — Skewered Hearts at Midnight

As night falls in Cusco, the anticucho carts emerge. These beef heart skewers are the ultimate Peruvian street food: cubed, marinated in vinegar and ají panca, grilled over charcoal until charred outside and impossibly tender within.

Beef heart sounds aggressive, but the texture is pure silk — dense yet yielding, with a clean, iron-rich flavor enhanced by the smoky marinade. Served with boiled potato and a drizzle of ají sauce, anticuchos are the meal you'll find yourself craving at 11 PM after a few pisco sours.

Look for the carts and small stalls that set up around **Calle Plateros** and near the markets after dark. **$2–$4** for several skewers.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main event. Cusqueños eat their largest meal between noon and 2 PM, which is when you'll find the best prices and freshest preparations at market comedores and **menú del día** restaurants. For around $3–$5, you'll get soup, a main course, a drink, and sometimes dessert. Dinner is lighter.

At markets like San Pedro, don't hesitate to sit at the counter — point at what looks good, trust the señora running the stall, and eat what appears. Sharing is common in traditional settings; dishes often arrive meant for the table. And always finish with a mate de coca — the coca leaf tea that settles the stomach and eases the altitude.
