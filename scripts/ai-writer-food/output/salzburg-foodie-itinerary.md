Three days in Salzburg taught me that this city takes its food as seriously as its Mozart. Behind the baroque facades and tourist-packed Getreidegasse lies a food culture built on Alpine dairy, river fish, and an almost obsessive dedication to pastry. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I arrived hungry.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Austrians eat dinner early — kitchens often stop taking orders by 9pm, even on weekends. Most restaurants accept cards, but smaller market stalls and traditional beer halls prefer cash (euros). Tipping is simple: round up or add 5-10% for good service. For weekend dinners at popular spots in the Old Town, book ahead. And learn one phrase: *Mahlzeit* — it's what locals say before eating, roughly meaning "enjoy your meal."

## Day 1: Old Town and the Classics

**Morning — Café Culture Done Right**

Start where Salzburg starts: with coffee and cake before 10am. Head to the Old Town and find a traditional Kaffeehaus with marble tabletops and waiters who've been perfecting their dignified indifference for decades. Order a *Melange* (Austria's answer to the cappuccino) and a slice of *Sachertorte* — that dense chocolate cake with apricot jam hiding beneath the glaze. Yes, it's dessert for breakfast. Yes, this is correct.

The key is not rushing. Salzburg's café culture rewards lingering; the waiter won't bring your check until you ask. Budget €8-12 for coffee and cake.

**Afternoon — Market Grazing and a Proper Lunch**

Walk to the Grünmarkt at Universitätsplatz, where Salzburg shops for food that isn't packaged in plastic. This open-air market runs Monday through Saturday, and by noon it's humming with locals buying Alpine cheese, cured meats, and vegetables that still have dirt on them. Grab a paper cone of roasted chestnuts if it's cool, or a fresh pretzel (*Brezel*) twisted that morning. Snacking budget: €5-8.

For lunch, stay in the Old Town and find a spot serving *Wiener Schnitzel* — not the tourist-trap version drowning in sauce, but the real thing: pork or veal pounded thin, breaded with fine crumbs, and fried until the coating puffs away from the meat in golden waves. It should arrive bigger than your plate, with a wedge of lemon and potato salad dressed in broth, not mayo. This is the benchmark. €15-22 for schnitzel with sides and a drink.

**Evening — First Night Feast**

Dinner belongs to the City Center's restaurant row, where you can watch the fortress light up on the hill while you eat. This is the night for *Tafelspitz* — boiled beef that sounds boring until you taste what happens when good meat meets good broth for hours. It arrives with apple-horseradish sauce, chive sauce, and roasted potatoes. Order it at a white-tablecloth spot where the portions are generous and the service knows not to rush you.

Pair it with a glass of Austrian Grüner Veltliner — crisp, peppery, and made for cutting through rich meat. Budget €35-55 for a full dinner with wine.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€55 | Mid-range ~€75 | Splurge ~€120

## Day 2: Across the River and Into the Neighborhoods

**Morning — Bread and Cheese, No Frills**

Cross the Salzach to the right bank and find breakfast in the Arts Quarter, where the crowds thin and the prices drop. Skip the hotel breakfast buffet and hunt down a bakery (*Bäckerei*) instead. Order a *Semmel* — Austria's crusty breakfast roll — split and topped with butter and local Alpine cheese. Add a soft-boiled egg and strong coffee. This is how Salzburg actually eats in the morning: simple, fast, and carb-forward. €6-10.

**Afternoon — Sausage Pilgrimage and Hidden Courtyards**

Lunch means sausage. Not the tourist stands charging €7 for a mediocre Bratwurst, but the places where locals queue. Look for a *Würstelstand* near the market areas, where the sausages have been simmering since dawn. Order a *Käsekrainer* — a pork sausage stuffed with cheese that oozes when you bite in — served with a crusty roll, mustard so sharp it clears your sinuses, and a cold *Stiegl* beer from the local brewery. Stand and eat like everyone else. €8-12.

After, walk off the pork through the hidden courtyards of the older neighborhoods. Duck through unmarked archways and you'll find quiet squares with café tables, a world away from the Getreidegasse crush. This is where a mid-afternoon slice of *Apfelstrudel* makes sense — tissue-thin pastry around cinnamon-spiced apples, served warm with vanilla sauce. Find a tucked-away café where the strudel is made in-house. €5-7.

**Evening — Going Local**

Tonight, seek out a *Gasthaus* — a traditional Austrian inn — away from the tourist center. The Business District and residential neighborhoods hide places where the menu is short, handwritten, and hasn't changed since the owner's grandmother ran the kitchen.

Order *Käsespätzle* — soft egg noodles baked with mountain cheese and topped with crispy fried onions. It's Austrian comfort food at its most unapologetic: carbs on carbs, fat on fat, and devastatingly good when done right. Follow it with a seasonal dish if the waiter suggests one. These places cook what they find, not what the guidebooks expect.

Budget €25-40 for dinner with local beer.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€55 | Mid-range ~€75 | Splurge ~€120

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

**Morning — One Last Proper Breakfast**

Return to the Old Town for a final morning, but make it count. Find a traditional spot serving a full Austrian breakfast plate: cold cuts, cheese, a soft-boiled egg in a silver cup, fresh rolls, butter, and jam. Add coffee. Sit by the window. Watch Salzburg wake up.

This meal exists outside of efficiency. It's designed for reading a newspaper or talking across the table without checking your phone. Accept the pace. €12-18.

**Afternoon — The Waterfront and Final Snacks**

Spend your last afternoon along the riverfront areas, where the walking is easy and the food opportunities are scattered like breadcrumbs. This is grazing time: a slice of *Leberkäse* (the baked meatloaf served warm in a roll) from a takeaway window (€4-5), a *Bosna* from a street vendor — Salzburg's local spin on the hot dog, stuffed into white bread with curry powder and onions (€4-5), and finally, a stop at a pastry shop for *Mozartkugel*, the pistachio-marzipan-nougat chocolate balls named for you-know-who.

Skip the mass-produced ones in the tourist shops. Find a confectioner making them by hand — the real ones are wrapped in silver and blue foil and taste nothing like the imposters. Buy a few for the train. €3-6.

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your last dinner should be your best. Return to the Old Town and book a table at a restaurant serious about modern Austrian cooking — places that take the same ingredients (fish from the lakes, game from the mountains, dairy from the valleys) and treat them with technique and intention.

Start with *Frittatensuppe* — clear beef broth with ribbons of sliced pancake — if they offer it. It sounds simple. It isn't.

For the main, look for *Forelle* (trout) if it's on the menu, pulled from Alpine waters and served simply: pan-fried with brown butter, almonds, and boiled potatoes. Or go for roasted *Schweinsbraten* (pork roast) with crackling skin and bread dumplings soaking in the pan juices.

End with *Kaiserschmarrn* — the torn, caramelized pancake served in the skillet with stewed plums. It's a dessert that requires two people and zero shame. Order it.

Budget €50-85 for a memorable final dinner with wine.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€55 | Mid-range ~€80 | Splurge ~€130

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The lunch menus are a secret weapon.** Many restaurants offer *Mittagsmenü* — a set lunch special between 11:30am and 2pm that's significantly cheaper than dinner. Same kitchen, same quality, half the price.
- **Don't skip the beer.** Stiegl is Salzburg's local brewery, and drinking it here tastes different than anywhere else. Order it fresh from the tap at any Gasthaus.
- **Restaurant kitchens close early.** Arrive by 7:30pm for dinner to avoid that panic when you realize half the city has stopped cooking.
- **The markets close early on Saturdays and don't open Sundays.** Plan your Grünmarkt visit accordingly, or spend Sunday wondering where all the fresh food went.
