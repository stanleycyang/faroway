The afternoon sun slants through the stone archways of Diocletian's Palace, and I'm standing at a marble counter worn smooth by centuries of elbows, watching an old woman fold paper-thin dough around wild greens she picked that morning from the hills above the city. The soparnik arrives minutes later, its edges crisp and blackened in spots, the chard inside still bright green and faintly bitter. I eat it with my hands, olive oil running down my wrist, and I understand immediately why people have been eating this exact thing, in this exact way, for a thousand years.

Split doesn't try to seduce you with its food. It doesn't need to. This is a city where fishermen still sell their morning catch on the same waterfront where Roman emperors once walked, where grandmothers guard recipes like state secrets, and where the Adriatic and the Dalmatian mountains conspire to create ingredients that need little more than salt, olive oil, and fire. After three weeks of eating my way through every konoba, market stall, and seaside restaurant I could find, I've assembled the dishes that define this ancient city's table.

#### Pašticada

This is the dish that Split cooks for weddings, for Christmas, for the moments that matter. Beef braised for hours — sometimes days — in a sauce of prunes, dried figs, red wine, and a secret blend of spices that every family swears they invented. The meat arrives so tender it falls apart under the weight of a fork, swimming in a sauce the color of mahogany, sweet and savory in a way that feels almost medieval. And it is medieval, brought here by Venetian traders who left behind their love of sweet-sour combinations.

The first bite is always a revelation. The beef has absorbed so much wine and fruit that it tastes almost candied, but then the rosemary and bay leaf cut through, grounding it. You eat it with gnocchi — never pasta, always gnocchi — small pillowy dumplings that exist solely to soak up that impossible sauce. Find it at family-run konobas in the neighborhoods behind the Riva, where the menus are handwritten and the portions could feed three. Expect to pay around €15–20, and expect to need a nap afterward.

#### Crni Rižot

Black risotto looks like something from another planet. The rice arrives glistening and obsidian, stained with cuttlefish ink, studded with tender rings of squid. Your teeth will turn gray. Your lips will look vaguely alarming. You won't care. The flavor is pure Adriatic — briny, deep, with an almost metallic mineral quality that tastes like the sea itself has been concentrated into each grain.

This dish exists because Dalmatian fishermen wasted nothing. The ink sac that most cuisines discard became a flavoring agent, a coloring agent, a point of pride. The best versions are loose and flowing, almost soupy, not the tight risotto balls you find in lesser restaurants. Order it at lunch from any of the waterfront restaurants in the Varoš neighborhood, when the catch is freshest. Around €12–15 will get you a portion that stains your memory as thoroughly as it stains your mouth.

#### Peka

If Split has a signature cooking method, it's this: meat or seafood, vegetables, potatoes, and herbs, all sealed under a cast-iron bell and buried in hot coals. The peka traps everything inside — the steam, the smoke, the fat, the time — and what emerges an hour later is something transcendent. Lamb peka, with potatoes that have absorbed so much rendered fat they're nearly translucent. Octopus peka, the tentacles caramelized and yielding, curled around potatoes that taste more of the sea than the sea itself.

You can't order this dish on a whim. Restaurants require you to call ahead, sometimes hours, sometimes a full day. This isn't pretension — it's physics. The coals need time to reach the right temperature, the meat needs time to surrender. Venture outside the palace walls to the restaurants along the road to Kaštela, where smoke rises from outdoor hearths and the peka arrives at the table still crackling. Budget €20–30 per person, and bring people to share it with. This is not a solo dish.

#### Soparnik

The oldest fast food in Dalmatia. Two thin layers of dough — made with nothing but flour, water, and salt — embracing a filling of Swiss chard, onion, garlic, and parsley. The whole thing gets slapped onto a hot stone and cooked until the edges char and blister. No cheese. No meat. Just greens and dough and olive oil drizzled generously over the top.

Soparnik is protected by the EU as a traditional specialty, which tells you everything about how seriously Croatians take this humble pie. It originated in the villages of the Dalmatian hinterland, where shepherds needed something portable and sustaining. Today you'll find it at the Green Market just outside Diocletian's Palace, sold by women who've been making it since before you were born. A generous slice costs around €2–3. Eat it warm, standing up, watching the chaos of the market swirl around you.

#### Brudet

Every fishing village on the Adriatic makes some version of this fish stew, and every village claims theirs is the authentic one. Split's brudet is democratic — whatever the fishermen brought in that morning goes into the pot. Scorpionfish. Conger eel. Monkfish. Sometimes a lonely mussel or two. Everything simmers together in tomato, wine, vinegar, and enough garlic to ward off vampires, until the house fillings fall away and the broth turns rust-red and complex.

The vinegar is the secret. It cuts through the richness, makes the stew feel lighter than it has any right to be. You eat brudet with polenta — soft, creamy, almost pourable — which serves as both utensil and sponge. The konobas along the Matejuška harbor, where fishing boats still tie up each morning, serve the most honest versions. Around €14–18 for a bowl that will remind you that fish stew, done right, is one of humanity's greatest achievements.

#### Fritule

These tiny fried dough balls appear at every celebration in Dalmatia, but especially during the colder months when the smell of hot oil and sugar drifts through the narrow palace streets. Imagine doughnut holes, but better — flecked with lemon zest and raisins, sometimes soaked in rakija, always dusted with powdered sugar that gets everywhere. They arrive hot enough to burn your tongue, light enough to eat ten of them before you realize what you've done.

Fritule trace back to Venetian rule, cousins to Italian frittelle, but they've become something distinctly Croatian. Find them at the Christmas market in the palace basement, or at any festival throughout the year. A paper cone of ten costs around €3–4, and you will want another cone immediately.

#### Rakija

No meal in Split ends without someone producing a bottle of this fruit brandy, usually homemade, usually strong enough to strip paint. The most common varieties are travarica (infused with wild herbs from the Dalmatian hills) and orahovac (made with green walnuts, almost black in color, sweet and bitter at once). Your host will pour you a glass before you can refuse. You will drink it. This is not optional.

Rakija is ritual as much as drink. It seals friendships, closes business deals, honors the dead, celebrates the living. The good stuff comes from someone's uncle's farm, aged in oak barrels in a cellar somewhere. The less good stuff still does its job. A shot at a bar costs around €2–3, but the best rakija is always free, poured by someone who insists you stay for just one more.

#### Rozata

Split's answer to crème caramel, though locals would argue this version came first. The custard is denser than its French cousin, perfumed with rose liqueur (the "roza" that gives it its name), topped with caramel that shatters under your spoon. The rose isn't overwhelming — just a whisper of floral sweetness that lifts the richness of the egg and cream.

You'll find rozata on the dessert menu of nearly every traditional restaurant, but the best versions come from konobas where they still make it fresh each day. A single serving runs €4–6, and it arrives in the same small ceramic ramekin your grandmother probably owned.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main event in Split — arrive hungry between noon and 2 PM, when restaurants serve their freshest preparations and the pace slows to accommodate proper eating. Dinner is lighter, later, often just cured meats and cheese on a terrace as the sun drops into the sea. Share everything. Ordering multiple dishes for the table isn't just acceptable; it's expected. Let the waiter guide you toward what's freshest — the catch of the day, the vegetables that arrived at the market that morning. Say "hvala" (thank you) often. Linger over coffee. Accept every offered glass of rakija. And remember: in Split, eating is never just eating. It's sitting with strangers who become friends, learning history through flavor, and understanding why this city has been feeding people well for two thousand years.
