The khachapuri arrives at my table at 9 AM, still bubbling from the tone oven, and I understand immediately why Georgians eat this for breakfast. The cheese stretches in long, obscene strings as I tear off a piece of the boat-shaped bread, drag it through the runny egg yolk pooling in the center, and watch it all collapse together into something that tastes like Sunday morning feels.

I've spent three weeks eating through Tbilisi, and I've come to a conclusion: Georgian food doesn't just feed you—it claims you. Every meal here is an argument that flavor should be maximized, that hospitality is a competitive sport, and that bread and cheese and wine might actually be the three pillars of civilization.

Here's what you absolutely need to eat.

---

#### Khachapuri Adjaruli

Let's start with the bread boat that launched a thousand Instagram posts—though no photo captures what it actually does to you. Khachapuri Adjaruli comes from the Adjara region on the Black Sea coast, and it's designed for people who wake up and choose chaos. A canoe of freshly baked dough, its edges golden and slightly charred, holds a molten lake of sulguni and imeruli cheeses. A raw egg yolk sits in the center like a dare. A knob of butter melts alongside it.

You don't cut this. You tear. You mix the egg into the cheese with your fork, creating a fondue-like pool, then rip off the bread edges and dunk. The first bite is stretchy, salty, rich in a way that makes you close your eyes. The dough is chewy but soft, slightly tangy from the yogurt in the recipe.

Find this at any traditional *tone* bakery—the ones with the cylindrical clay ovens you can see from the street. Puriselebis Duqani near Abanotubani does a perfect one for around $4–5. Order it fresh; wait the ten minutes. Cold khachapuri is a tragedy.

---

#### Khinkali

If khachapuri is Georgia's heart, khinkali is its soul—specifically, the soul of mountain shepherds who needed portable, filling food that could survive a long day. These pleated dumplings look like twisted pouches, each one sealed with a topknot that serves as a handle. Inside: spiced meat (traditionally lamb or pork-beef mix) suspended in hot, peppery broth.

Here's the ritual, and you must follow it: hold the dumpling by its knot, bite a small hole in the side, sip the broth first (carefully—it's hot enough to brand your tongue), then eat the rest. The knot itself is too tough to eat; you leave it on your plate, and at the end of the meal, your pile of knots tells the story of how hungry you were.

The filling should be juicy, never dry, seasoned with black pepper, cumin, and fresh herbs. The dough should be thin enough to be tender but thick enough to hold the broth prisoner.

Zakhar Zakharich in Vera serves excellent khinkali in a no-frills setting where construction workers eat alongside tourists. Five khinkali will cost you around $3 and will fill you completely. Order at least eight—you'll regret stopping at five.

---

#### Badrijani Nigvzit

This is the dish that converts people who think they don't like eggplant. Thin slices of fried eggplant, tender and silky, rolled around a filling of crushed walnuts, garlic, and herbs—usually coriander, fenugreek, and blue fenugreek (a spice that tastes like nothing else, somewhere between maple and grass). A scattering of pomegranate seeds on top adds bursts of tartness.

The walnut paste is rich, almost creamy, with a slight bitterness that balances the sweetness of the cooked eggplant. Each roll collapses gently when you bite into it. The garlic is present but not aggressive—a whisper, not a shout.

This is standard supra (feast) food, appearing on nearly every Georgian table as part of the opening spread. You'll find it at every traditional restaurant in the Old Town. Shavi Lomi in Vera makes a particularly good version, though at $5–6 it's a bit pricier than average. Street-side restaurants near Dry Bridge Market serve simpler versions for $2–3.

---

#### Lobiani

If khachapuri is the glamorous sibling, lobiani is the dependable one—less famous internationally but equally beloved locally. It's flatbread stuffed with spiced kidney beans, mashed and seasoned with onions and *svanuri marili* (Svan salt, a Georgian spice blend that includes garlic, fenugreek, and dried marigold petals).

The result is earthy, warming, deeply satisfying in a way that requires no explanation. The beans are creamy but textured, the bread golden and slightly crisp on the outside, soft within. It's peasant food elevated by centuries of refinement.

Lobiani is traditionally eaten during Barbaroba (St. Barbara's Day) in winter, but you'll find it year-round at bakeries and casual restaurants. The tone bakeries in Abanotubani sell it straight from the oven for around $1.50. Eat it hot, standing on the sidewalk, watching the steam rise.

---

#### Churchkhela

Hanging in every market stall like strange candied candles, churchkhela looks like it shouldn't be food. Strings of walnuts or hazelnuts, dipped repeatedly in thickened grape juice (*tatara*), then hung to dry until they form a chewy, waxy exterior around the crunchy nuts inside.

The flavor is concentrated grape sweetness—not cloying, more like wine reduced to its essence—with the richness of the nuts breaking through. The texture shifts as you chew: from firm and slightly rubbery to soft and grainy to the satisfying crunch of the nut.

This was energy food for Georgian warriors and travelers along the Silk Road, sustenance that wouldn't spoil. Today it's the snack you buy at Dezerter Bazaar or from the vendors near Narikala Fortress, eat walking through cobblestone streets, and wonder why the rest of the world hasn't caught on. A single string costs around $2–3.

---

#### Chacha

Not a food, but you cannot eat in Tbilisi without encountering Georgia's firewater. Chacha is grape pomace brandy—what's left after winemaking, distilled into something that ranges from 40% to 65% alcohol. The good stuff is smooth, with a faint fruitiness beneath the burn. The bad stuff is moonshine that could strip paint.

At a supra, the toastmaster (*tamada*) will pour chacha for important toasts—to Georgia, to ancestors, to love. You drink it in one shot, and the warmth spreads through your chest like a second heartbeat.

Don't buy the tourist bottles. Find a restaurant where the owner makes their own, or visit a wine house in Kakheti region for the real thing. A shot rarely costs more than $1–2.

---

#### Pkhali

A family of dishes rather than a single one, pkhali are vegetable pâtés made with walnuts, garlic, and herbs. Spinach pkhali is most common—bright green, smooth, shaped into small rounds or logs—but you'll also find versions made with beets (deep purple, earthier), beans, or cabbage.

The walnut base gives all pkhali a richness that makes them feel more substantial than their vegetables-and-nuts origin suggests. They're served cold, part of the opening spread at any proper Georgian meal, and they're the dishes that make vegetarians realize Georgia is their paradise.

Most restaurants serve a pkhali sampler for $4–6. Café Littera, in a gorgeous garden setting, offers an elegant version; humbler spots in the Vera neighborhood serve earthier, more rustic interpretations.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Georgian meals are not quick. Accept this. A proper lunch stretches two hours; dinner might go four. Dishes arrive in waves—cold salads and pkhali first, then khachapuri and lobiani, then meat dishes, then dessert you didn't order but cannot refuse.

Share everything. Individual portions exist, but the culture favors communal tables covered in too much food. Order more than you think you need; waste is preferable to scarcity in Georgian hospitality.

Eat khachapuri and lobiani in the morning or early afternoon—they're breakfast and lunch foods, heavy enough to carry you through a long day. Save khinkali for when you can focus on the ritual. And if someone invites you to a supra, clear your schedule. You're about to eat for hours, toast to everything you've ever loved, and leave with friends you didn't have that morning.
