Three days in Ghent taught me that this medieval Flemish city punches absurdly above its weight when it comes to eating. Between the canals and guild houses, I found a food culture that takes its classics seriously — stoofvlees simmered for hours, waffles made on century-old irons, and a vegetarian tradition that predates trends by decades. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me at the train station.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Ghent runs on European meal timing — lunch from noon to 2pm, dinner rarely before 7pm. Most restaurants accept cards, but smaller bakeries and market stalls prefer cash (euros). Tipping isn't obligatory but rounding up or leaving 5-10% for good service is appreciated. Weekend reservations at popular spots should be made 2-3 days ahead. And one crucial detail: Thursday through Saturday mornings are prime market time.

## Day 1: The Historic Center & Flemish Foundations

### Morning

Start where Ghent starts — in the shadow of Saint Bavo's Cathedral in the Old Town, where the smell of fresh bread cuts through the morning mist. Walk to a traditional bakery for *koffiekoeken*, the catch-all term for Flemish breakfast pastries. You want the *mattentaart*, a local tart filled with sweetened curd cheese that's been made in this region since medieval times. Pair it with a strong Belgian coffee — they pour it small and dark here.

The morning pace in Ghent's historic core is unhurried. Take your pastry to a bench overlooking the Graslei waterfront and watch the city wake up. This isn't a grab-and-go culture; breakfast is meant to be slow.

**Estimated cost:** €8–€12

### Afternoon

Lunch demands *waterzooi*, and there's no negotiating this. The dish — a creamy, brothless stew originally made with fish from the rivers, now more commonly with chicken — was invented here in Ghent. You'll find it in nearly every restaurant in the City Center, but seek out a place that simmers their version for hours, not minutes. The broth should coat your spoon, the vegetables soft but holding their shape, the meat falling apart with suggestion rather than force. Served with crusty bread for soaking up every drop.

After lunch, walk north toward the Groentenmarkt (Vegetable Market) near the Groot Vleeshuis, the medieval butchers' hall that's been feeding Ghent since the 15th century. The market runs mornings, but the surrounding streets hold specialty food shops worth exploring — aged Gouda wheels, local mustards, and East Flemish charcuterie. This is reconnaissance for later meals.

Pick up a *neuzekes* — literally "little noses," cone-shaped raspberry gummy candies that Ghent claims as its own. They cost almost nothing and taste like a better version of your childhood.

**Estimated cost:** €22–€35 (lunch + market snacks)

### Evening

Dinner in the Old Town should be an event, not just a meal. Find a restaurant with white tablecloths and a kitchen that treats *stoofvlees* — Flemish beef stew braised in beer — as a point of pride. The best versions use dark abbey ales, cook the meat until it dissolves into the sauce, and serve it with twice-fried frites that shatter when you bite them. This is not fast food made slow; it's slow food made honest.

The mid-range version runs around €28–€35 for the stew with frites and a Belgian beer. The splurge version, at a restaurant with canal views and a chef who sources specific farms, pushes €55–€70. Both are worth it in different ways.

**Estimated cost:** €35–€70

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~€65 | Mid ~€95 | Splurge ~€120

## Day 2: The Arts Quarter & Hidden Corners

### Morning

Shift neighborhoods to the Arts Quarter, where university students and young professionals have shaped a different food culture — more contemporary, slightly irreverent, but still rooted in local ingredients. Brunch here means *croque monsieur* done properly: thick-cut ham, gruyère bubbling and browned, béchamel that actually tastes like something. Or go sweet with *Liège waffles* from a stand where you can watch them pressed to order. These aren't the Brussels waffles tourists expect — Liège waffles are denser, studded with pearl sugar that caramelizes into crunchy pockets. Eat them plain, warm, standing up.

**Estimated cost:** €10–€16

### Afternoon

Lunch today should come from Ghent's hidden cafe scene — the kind of place where locals actually eat, not where tourists are steered. Tucked in the residential streets between the main squares, these spots serve hearty plates: vol-au-vent (puff pastry filled with creamy chicken and mushrooms), *boulettes* (meatballs in tomato sauce), and always a vegetarian option because Ghent takes meatless eating seriously. The city holds a "Donderdag Veggiedag" (Thursday Veggie Day) that's been running since 2009.

If it's Thursday, lean into it — Ghent's vegetarian restaurants outperform most cities twice its size. Try a vegetable stoemp (mashed potatoes with seasonal greens), or a North African-influenced tagine at one of the Arts Quarter's modern spots.

After lunch, consider a cooking class if you want hands-on time with Flemish technique. Several small operations near the Waterfront offer afternoon sessions focused on regional classics — making speculoos biscuits, perfecting stoofvlees, or learning the alchemy of Belgian chocolate. Book ahead; these fill up.

**Estimated cost:** €25–€40 (lunch) + €60–€90 (cooking class, optional)

### Evening

For dinner, cross into a local neighborhood away from the tourist spine. The streets behind the Design Museum hold restaurants where the menu changes weekly based on what's good at the market. This is where Ghent's food scene shows its contemporary side — local ingredients, lighter preparations, an international accent that doesn't lose its Flemish identity.

Order the regional charcuterie plate to start. Then something seasonal — asparagus in spring (Flemish white asparagus is a religion), game in fall, mussels whenever they're running. Close with *dame blanche*: vanilla ice cream drowned in warm Belgian chocolate sauce, the kind of dessert that sounds simple until you taste it done right.

**Estimated cost:** €40–€75

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~€75 | Mid ~€130 | Splurge ~€180

## Day 3: Best of Everything & The Farewell Feast

### Morning

Your last morning deserves the full classic breakfast treatment. Head back to the City Center for a proper *ontbijt* at a cafe with checkered tablecloths and servers who've worked there for decades. This means bread and more bread — grainy, crusty, sliced thick — with butter, jam, aged cheese, sliced ham, a soft-boiled egg, and strong coffee. Maybe a small glass of fresh orange juice if you're feeling extravagant. Belgians don't fuss with breakfast, but they don't skimp either.

**Estimated cost:** €12–€18

### Afternoon

Spend your final afternoon on a grazing walk through Ghent's best food streets, assembling a moveable lunch from vendors who do one thing well. Start at the Groentenmarkt again, then wind through the Old Town's side streets toward the Waterfront.

Your checklist: *cuberdons* (those purple nose-shaped candies, Ghent's signature sweet), a paper cone of frites from a proper *frietkot* (fry stand) with mayo or stoofvleessaus, a square of praline from a chocolatier who tempers their own, and a *jenever* — Belgian gin, often flavored with fruit — at a canal-side bar. This isn't lunch; it's a walking anthology of everything Ghent does well.

**Estimated cost:** €20–€30

### Evening

The final meal should be the one you remember. Return to the Historic Center for a dinner that earns its price — somewhere with a tasting menu or a chef whose name people mention when they talk about eating in Ghent. This is the splurge, the meal where you stop thinking about cost per plate and start thinking about memory per bite.

Expect refined takes on regional classics: eel in green herb sauce (a Flemish tradition), North Sea fish with beurre blanc, beef from Belgian Blue cattle, and a cheese course featuring abbey cheeses aged by actual monks. The wine list should lean French but include Belgian beers as pairing options — a good kitchen here treats Westmalle and Orval as seriously as Burgundy.

For budget-conscious eaters, an excellent mid-range restaurant will serve similar quality at half the price, with à la carte options instead of set menus. The food culture here supports both.

**Estimated cost:** €50–€120

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~€80 | Mid ~€115 | Splurge ~€170

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The Thursday rule is real.** Ghent's vegetarian options on Donderdag Veggiedag are genuinely better than most days — restaurants feature special menus, and the variety expands. Plan around it if you eat plant-based.

- **Frites hierarchy matters.** Chain spots serve frozen potatoes; proper *frietkoten* use fresh Bintje potatoes, double-fried in beef tallow. Ask if you're unsure. The difference is not subtle.

- **Cuberdon candies have a shelf life.** They're best within days of being made — the exterior firms while the inside stays gooey. Buy them from market vendors who make small batches, not tourist shops selling month-old stock.

- **Reservations aren't always necessary, but asking helps.** Even casual-looking restaurants appreciate a call ahead for dinner, especially Friday and Saturday. Phone numbers are usually on the door.
