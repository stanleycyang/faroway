# Hong Kong Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Hong Kong

Three days in Hong Kong means roughly nine meals, plus the snacks you'll inevitably accumulate while walking between them. This city doesn't do modest eating — it does dim sum carts rattling past at 7 AM, roast meats hanging in windows that make you late for everything, and cha chaan tengs where the milk tea hits harder than espresso. Here's how to eat it properly.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Most local restaurants are cash-only, especially in Mong Kok and Sham Shui Po — hit an ATM before you start grazing. Tipping isn't expected at casual spots, though upscale restaurants add 10% service. Lunch rushes run 12:30–1:30 PM and dim sum halls fill by 10 AM on weekends, so either go early or embrace the wait. Tap the table twice when someone pours your tea — it's the Cantonese "thank you" that marks you as someone who knows what they're doing.

## Day 1: Central & The Harbor — Dim Sum, Roast Meats, and the Classics

### Morning: Dim Sum Done Right

Start in **Central** before the financial district wakes up. You want a proper yum cha experience — the kind where carts roll past and you point at what looks good, or where the menu runs forty items deep and you over-order because restraint isn't the point.

**What to eat**: Har gow (crystal shrimp dumplings), siu mai, cheung fun (rice noodle rolls), and char siu bao if they're doing them right — the baked version with its lacquered top, not the steamed white buns tourists expect. Get the chicken feet if you're feeling brave; they're better than they sound.

**Where**: The old-school halls with fluorescent lighting and formica tables are disappearing, but the ones that remain serve dim sum that tastes like it did thirty years ago. Look for places where the aunties are eating, not photographing.

**Cost**: HK$150–250 (~$19–32) for a proper spread with tea.

### Afternoon: Roast Meat Pilgrimage + SoHo Wander

Lunch is about one thing: **roast goose**. The Cantonese do roast meats better than anywhere else, and goose is the pinnacle — crispy skin that shatters, fat that renders into the meat, flesh that stays juicy. You'll find the best versions hanging in windows throughout **Central** and nearby, the glaze darkening under heat lamps.

Order it over rice with the shop's sweet soy, or go for a three-combination plate if you want to add char siu (honey-glazed pork) and soy sauce chicken to the mix. This is a fast meal — in, seated at a communal table, eating within minutes. Don't linger; the turnover is part of the system.

After, walk uphill into **SoHo** — the stretch of galleries, wine bars, and antique shops that runs along the Central–Mid-Levels escalator. It's not a food destination itself, but it burns off lunch and lets you scope out evening options. Duck into **PMQ**, the old police quarters turned design hub, where local designers sell everything from ceramics to pastries in a colonial courtyard setting.

**Cost**: HK$80–150 (~$10–19) for roast meat rice.

### Evening: Harbor Views and a Proper Cantonese Spread

Take the **Star Ferry** across to **Tsim Sha Tsui** as the light drops — it costs almost nothing and gives you the skyline from water level, which beats any rooftop bar. Time it for sunset if you can.

Dinner should be Cantonese seafood done properly: steamed fish with ginger and scallion, salt-and-pepper squid, maybe a clay pot rice if they're making them. TST has everything from Michelin-starred hotel restaurants to neighborhood spots where families gather around lazy Susans piled with food. Pick based on your budget, but either way, order too much — that's how it's done here.

**Cost**: Budget HK$200 (~$26) for a solo meal at a local spot; Mid-range HK$400–600 (~$52–77) for a full spread; Splurge HK$1,200+ (~$155+) at a white-tablecloth Cantonese institution.

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~HK$430 ($55) · Mid-range ~HK$800 ($103) · Splurge ~HK$1,650 ($213)

## Day 2: Mong Kok & Sham Shui Po — Street Food, Noodles, and the Real City

### Morning: Cha Chaan Teng Breakfast

Forget Western breakfast. This morning you eat like a Hong Konger running late for work: **pineapple bun** (bo lo bao) with a slab of cold butter melting into the warm bread, and **milk tea** strong enough to recalibrate your entire nervous system. The bun isn't pineapple-flavored — it's named for the crackled sugar topping that looks like pineapple skin. The tea is brewed through a cloth filter until it's dark as coffee, then sweetened with evaporated milk.

Find a **cha chaan teng** in **Mong Kok** — the diner-style cafes with plastic menus, irritable service, and food that tastes like someone's grandmother made it. You can add macaroni soup with ham or a French toast stuffed with peanut butter if you're hungry, but the bun and tea are non-negotiable.

**Where**: The older the cha chaan teng, the better. Look for condensation on the windows and a crowd of office workers eating fast.

**Cost**: HK$40–60 (~$5–8) for breakfast set.

### Afternoon: Wonton Noodles + Sham Shui Po Market Crawl

Lunch is **wonton noodles** — specifically, the kind with shrimp wontons the size of your thumb, thin alkaline egg noodles that bounce back when you bite them, and a clear broth that looks simple but took hours. This is Hong Kong's everyday luxury, the dish people argue about most intensely. Good versions exist across the city, but the old shops in **Mong Kok** still make their own noodles daily.

After, take the MTR one stop to **Sham Shui Po** — the working-class neighborhood that hasn't gentrified yet. This is where you'll find fabric markets, electronics hawkers, and food that costs almost nothing: curry fish balls on sticks, cheung fun from carts, egg puffs fresh off the griddle. Walk Apliu Street for the chaos, then duck into the covered wet market for fruit you've never seen before.

Pick up an **egg waffle (gai daan jai)** from a street vendor — the bubble-shaped batter cooked between cast-iron molds, crispy outside, custardy inside. Eat it hot, walking, like everyone else.

**Cost**: Wonton noodles HK$50–70 (~$6–9); Sham Shui Po snacking HK$50–80 (~$6–10).

### Evening: Seafood the Lei Yue Mun Way

For dinner, make the trip to **Lei Yue Mun** — a waterfront fishing village on the eastern edge of Kowloon where the process works like this: you walk through the wet market, pick your seafood live from the tanks (prawns, crab, razor clams, whatever looks good), negotiate the price, then carry it to a restaurant that cooks it for a fee. It's theatrical, participatory, and the seafood couldn't be fresher.

This isn't a quick dinner — budget two hours for the full experience. The village is strung with lights reflecting off the water, the restaurants are loud and crowded, and you'll leave smelling like garlic and ginger.

**Cost**: HK$300–600 (~$39–77) depending on what you pick from the tanks.

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~HK$440 ($57) · Mid-range ~HK$730 ($94) · Splurge ~HK$1,280 ($165)

## Day 3: Wan Chai & Beyond — The Greatest Hits

### Morning: Congee and the Slow Start

Your last morning should be gentle. Head to **Wan Chai** for **congee** — rice porridge cooked until it breaks down into silk, served with century egg, pork, or fish depending on your tolerance for adventure at 9 AM. This is comfort food, the thing Hong Kongers eat when they're sick, hungover, or just need something warm and easy before the city starts demanding things.

Pair it with a **yau ja gwai** — fried dough sticks meant for dunking — and more milk tea, because three days isn't enough to get tired of it.

**Cost**: HK$50–80 (~$6–10).

### Afternoon: Temple Street Setup + Final Snacking

Spend the afternoon in transit between snacks. Start in Wan Chai's markets for **dai pai dong** eating — the open-air food stalls that used to define Hong Kong's street food scene. They're rarer now, pushed out by regulations and rising rents, but a few survive. Grab whatever's cooking.

As evening approaches, position yourself in **Mong Kok** near **Temple Street Night Market**. The market itself is more about shopping than eating, but the streets around it come alive after dark with food carts, neon signs, and the density of choice that defines this city. This is good grazing territory: siu mai from a street vendor, a final egg waffle, or whatever catches your eye.

**Cost**: HK$100–150 (~$13–19) for afternoon grazing.

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your final dinner should be definitive. Return to the style of eating Hong Kong does best: a table crowded with dishes, family-style service, and flavors that remind you why Cantonese cuisine has spread across the world.

Go back to a dim sum hall if you didn't get enough on Day 1, or find a Cantonese restaurant in **Tsim Sha Tsui** or **Central** that does clay pot rice, crispy chicken, and XO sauce fried rice. Order the things you loved earlier in the trip, plus one dish you haven't tried yet. Leave full enough that you won't need the airplane food.

**Cost**: Budget HK$200 (~$26); Mid-range HK$500 (~$64); Splurge HK$1,500+ (~$193) for the farewell feast.

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~HK$350 ($45) · Mid-range ~HK$730 ($94) · Splurge ~HK$1,730 ($223)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Bring cash in small bills.** The best street food vendors and old-school noodle shops don't take cards, and breaking a HK$500 note for a $40 bowl of noodles will not make you popular.
- **Eat early for dim sum.** By 10:30 AM on weekends, the good places have hour-long waits. Arrive at opening — around 7 or 8 AM — and you'll walk straight in.
- **Never stick chopsticks upright in your rice.** It resembles incense at a funeral altar. Lay them across your bowl or use the rest.
- **The MTR is your friend, but so are your feet.** Neighborhoods reveal their best food when you walk them — the roast meat shop you'd miss from the train, the cart that only appears at 4 PM.
