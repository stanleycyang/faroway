# Siem Reap Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The aroma hits you before you see anything—charcoal smoke carrying the sweet caramelized edges of pork belly, mingling with lemongrass and the earthy funk of prahok fish paste. It's 6 AM in Siem Reap, and somewhere near the Old Market, a woman is ladling green curry over rice noodles while her neighbor flips strips of pork on a grill that's been in the family for three generations. This is how mornings begin here, and this is why you need to wake up hungry.

## A Kitchen Shaped by Empire and Resilience

Siem Reap's food tells a story that stretches back a thousand years to the Khmer Empire, when Angkor was the largest city on Earth and its kitchens fed over a million people. The flavors here are gentler than neighboring Thailand or Vietnam—less aggressive heat, more aromatic depth. French colonialism left behind crusty baguettes and pâté. Chinese traders contributed stir-fry techniques and noodle soups. But the soul of Khmer cuisine remains distinctly its own: the holy trinity of lemongrass, galangal, and kaffir lime; the umami punch of fermented fish; the cooling contrast of raw herbs piled high on every plate.

What makes eating in Siem Reap remarkable isn't just the food—it's the context. This is a city that was nearly emptied during the Khmer Rouge years, its culinary traditions carried forward by grandmothers who remembered recipes in their bones. Today, those recipes are experiencing a renaissance. Young chefs are returning to traditional techniques while street vendors maintain unchanged recipes. Every meal here feels earned, both by the cook and by history itself.

## The Essential Dishes You Cannot Skip

**Amok** is the dish that defines Khmer cooking, and you'll understand why after your first spoonful. Fish—usually catfish—arrives steamed inside a banana leaf cup, suspended in a custard-like coconut curry thickened with slok ngor leaves that lend a faint bitterness to balance the richness. The texture is somewhere between mousse and soufflé, silky enough to coat your tongue while the lemongrass and turmeric wake up your palate. Find the best versions in the quieter restaurants around Wat Bo, where family-run kitchens take their time.

**Bai Sach Chrouk** is breakfast perfection. At dawn, street-side grills near the Old Market area are already turning out thin-sliced pork marinated in coconut milk and garlic, charred at the edges and impossibly tender within. It arrives over broken rice with a fried egg, pickled vegetables, and a bowl of clear broth for sipping. For $2, you'll eat like royalty.

**Lok Lak** proves that simplicity wins. Cubes of beef are flash-fried until caramelized, then served atop lettuce and tomatoes with a dipping sauce of black pepper, lime juice, and salt that makes the meat sing. The best plates come from open-air restaurants where you can watch the wok work.

**Nom Banh Chok** is Cambodia's answer to the breakfast noodle, and locals are fiercely loyal to their preferred vendor. Fresh rice noodles—bouncy, slightly tacky, made that morning—swim in a delicate green curry made from pounded fish and lemongrass. A mountain of raw vegetables arrives alongside: banana flower, cucumber, bean sprouts, long beans. You build each bite yourself.

**Fresh spring rolls** here aren't the Vietnamese versions you might know. Khmer rolls are thicker, stuffed with shrimp, pork belly, herbs, and sometimes mango, served with a peanut dipping sauce that's sweeter and nuttier than its neighbors' versions.

And then there's **fried tarantula**—yes, the spider. Harvested near Skuon and deep-fried until the legs shatter like potato chips while the abdomen stays creamy. The taste is somewhere between soft-shell crab and earth. You don't have to try it. But you'll never forget it if you do.

## Street Food: The Heart of the Matter

The real eating in Siem Reap happens between proper meals, standing at plastic tables or perched on impossibly small stools. The street food scene pulses strongest in the early morning and after dark.

Near the Old Market, vendors set up by 5:30 AM, catering to locals heading to work. This is when you'll find the freshest bai sach chrouk and the most authentic nom banh chok. By 7 AM, the best stuff is gone.

Night markets transform after sunset. Look for grilled meats on sticks—pork meatballs, chicken hearts, beef skewers lacquered with sweet soy. Fried insects appear on carts for the adventurous. Fruit smoothies made with dragon fruit or custard apple cost less than a dollar and taste like vacation.

The unwritten rules: point at what you want, smile, and pay in cash (small bills preferred). Eat what locals are eating. If a stall has a line of Cambodians waiting, join it. If it's empty at dinner rush, walk on.

## Eating Well at Every Price Point

**Budget (Under $15):** You can eat extraordinarily well for almost nothing. A full breakfast of bai sach chrouk runs $2–3. Street noodle soups hover around $1.50. Near Pub Street, avoid the tourist-trap restaurants with English menus and seek out the side-street spots where $4 gets you amok that rivals restaurants charging four times as much. The Old Market food stalls serve complete lunches for under $3—just point at the curries that look best and say "bai" (rice).

**Mid-Range ($15–40):** This is where Siem Reap shines. Restaurants around Kandal Village serve elevated Khmer cuisine in gorgeous restored buildings, with multi-course meals running $20–30. You'll find creative takes on lok lak, fresh spring rolls with unexpected ingredients, and desserts incorporating palm sugar and local fruits. The Wat Bo neighborhood hosts family-style restaurants where $25 feeds two people into satisfied silence.

**Splurge ($45+):** When temples have worn you down and you crave air conditioning and white tablecloths, Siem Reap delivers. High-end restaurants along Charles de Gaulle Boulevard offer tasting menus that reinterpret Khmer classics—amok made tableside, beef from Kampot pepper farms, desserts involving smoked coconut and seasonal fruits you've never heard of. Budget $50–80 per person with drinks. Worth it once, especially if you've been traveling hard.

## For Vegetarians and Vegans

Here's the honest truth: traditional Khmer cooking relies heavily on fish sauce, shrimp paste, and pork fat. "Vegetarian" dishes sometimes arrive with oyster sauce or fish-based broths.

That said, Siem Reap has adapted. Restaurants in Kandal Village and the Wat Bo area often have dedicated vegetarian menus, and the international restaurant scene includes fully plant-based spots. Morning glory stir-fried with garlic, vegetable curries, and tofu amok exist—you just need to ask specifically about fish sauce. Fresh spring rolls can be made vegan if you request. The phrase "saht" means vegetarian in Khmer, though miming "no fish, no meat" often works better.

Buddhist temple food occasionally appears at morning markets—simple, meat-free, and deeply satisfying.

## Where to Eat: A Neighborhood Guide

**Old Market (Psar Chas)** is ground zero. The market itself holds food stalls selling noodles, grilled items, and sweets, while the surrounding streets pack in restaurants ranging from backpacker cheap to genuinely excellent. Come for breakfast or an early lunch before the heat peaks.

**Pub Street** is loud, tourist-heavy, and sells $0.50 beers—but skip the pad thai and bland curries. Venture one block in any direction and prices drop while quality rises.

**Kandal Village** has emerged as the spot for cafe culture, boutique restaurants, and contemporary Khmer cooking. This is where young Cambodian chefs are experimenting. The pace is slower, the coffee is excellent, and the spring rolls are architectural.

**Wat Bo** offers quieter residential dining—the kind of restaurants where the owner's grandmother is cooking and the menu changes based on what looked good at the market.

**Charles de Gaulle Boulevard** is hotel territory, but the upscale restaurants here justify the prices with ingredients and technique you won't find elsewhere.

## How to Eat Like You Belong

Cambodians eat with a fork and spoon—fork in left hand to push food onto the spoon in your right. Chopsticks appear only for noodle soups. Rice is sacred; don't leave grains on your plate if you can help it.

Meals are communal. Several dishes arrive at once and everyone shares, building individual bites from the spread. Soup is sipped throughout the meal, not as a separate course.

Tipping isn't traditionally expected at local restaurants, but service workers at tourist-oriented spots appreciate 10%—wages are low. At street stalls, round up or leave small change.

Cambodians eat early. Breakfast happens by 7 AM, lunch by 11:30, dinner by 6:30. Adjust your stomach accordingly, or you'll miss the best food at its freshest.
