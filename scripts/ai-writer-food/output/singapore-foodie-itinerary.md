# Singapore Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Singapore

Three days. Thirty-odd meals and snacks. One city-state that punches so far above its weight in food that a single hawker stall earned a Michelin star. Singapore isn't a place where you "find time to eat" — eating *is* the itinerary, and everything else happens between meals.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Singaporeans eat early and often. Hawker centres peak at noon and 7 PM — arrive thirty minutes before to claim a seat. Tipping isn't expected anywhere. Cash is still king at hawker stalls (many don't take cards), so carry small bills. Fine dining requires booking weeks ahead; everywhere else, just show up. Queue orderly and seriously — cutting the line is social death here. And never, ever eat or drink on the MRT. The fine is real.

## Day 1: The Hawker Initiation — Chinatown & Tiong Bahru

**Morning: Kaya Toast and Soft-Boiled Eggs**

Start where Singaporeans start: with kaya toast, runny eggs, and thick coffee at a traditional kopitiam. Head to **Tiong Bahru**, an art-deco neighborhood tucked between the shophouses and indie bookshops. The charcoal-grilled bread at the old-school coffee shops here has a smokiness that toast racks can't replicate. Order your eggs "soft" — they'll arrive in a small bowl, barely set, meant to be seasoned with soy sauce and white pepper, then spooned over toast or slurped straight. The kopi (local coffee with sweetened condensed milk) is thick enough to wake the dead.

*Estimated cost: $4–6*

**Afternoon: Chinatown Hawker Crawl**

Walk or take a short MRT ride to **Chinatown** for the main event: your first proper hawker centre meal. Maxwell Food Centre is the most famous (this is where Tian Tian Hainanese Chicken Rice built its legend), but I'd steer you toward Hong Lim Market & Food Centre instead — fewer tourists, same quality, and you'll actually get a seat.

**Hainanese Chicken Rice** is non-negotiable here. Silky poached chicken, fragrant rice cooked in chicken fat and pandan, served with ginger-scallion paste, dark soy, and sambal. The best plates look deceptively simple — pale chicken, mounded rice, a few cucumber slices — but the flavor depth is staggering. After eating, walk the covered streets of Chinatown's heritage shophouses, ducking into the Chinatown Complex nearby for a second round: order char siew (barbecued pork) from a different stall, or a bowl of bak kut teh (peppery pork rib soup) if you want something warming.

*Estimated cost: $8–15 (depending on how many stalls you hit)*

**Evening: Chilli Crab at Clarke Quay**

Tonight, you go big. Take the MRT to **Clarke Quay** and settle into a riverside seafood restaurant for **Chilli Crab** — Singapore's messiest, most satisfying dish. Sri Lankan mud crabs, wok-fried in a sweet-spicy tomato-chilli sauce that's more savory than fiery, with just enough egg ribbon to give it body. Order mantou (fried buns) to mop up the sauce. You will destroy napkins. You will get sauce on your forearms. This is correct.

Budget diners can find respectable versions at hawker centres, but the full experience — cracking shells on the riverfront, cold Tiger beer, lights reflecting off the water — belongs at a proper seafood restaurant. Expect to pay $60–80 per person for crab at mid-range spots like Jumbo or No Signboard, or $120+ at the high-end establishments.

*Estimated cost: Budget $35 | Mid $80 | Splurge $150*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$50 | Mid ~$100 | Splurge ~$170

## Day 2: The Diversity Day — Little India, Kampong Glam & Katong

**Morning: Roti Prata in Little India**

Singapore's food story is really three food stories — Chinese, Malay, Indian — braided together. Today, you explore the other threads. Start in **Little India** with **roti prata**: flaky, laminated flatbread cooked on a griddle, served with curry for dipping. The best versions are crisp on the outside, almost crepe-thin in places, with layers that pull apart like phyllo. Order it plain (kosong) or stuffed with egg, onion, or cheese if you're feeling indulgent. Wash it down with teh tarik — pulled milk tea with a frothy top.

Walk off breakfast through Little India's spice markets. The air here smells like turmeric and cardamom. Produce stalls overflow with bitter gourd and okra. This is Singapore's most sensory neighborhood.

*Estimated cost: $5–8*

**Afternoon: Laksa Hunt in Katong**

For lunch, head east to **Katong** (sometimes called Joo Chiat), a Peranakan neighborhood with pastel shophouses and one of Singapore's great food obsessions: **laksa**. Katong laksa is specific — rich coconut curry broth, cut noodles short enough to eat with a spoon, topped with prawns, cockles, fish cake, and a crown of laksa leaf. The spice builds slowly; by the third spoonful, your forehead is sweating.

328 Katong Laksa and the original Janggut Laksa have been feuding for decades over who invented the style. Try both (they're on the same street) and pick a side. While you're here, stop at a Peranakan bakery for pineapple tarts and kueh — colorful coconut-based sweets that look like art and taste like someone's grandmother spent hours on them.

*Estimated cost: $12–18*

**Evening: Arab Quarter Dining in Kampong Glam**

End the day in **Kampong Glam**, the Arab Quarter, where Sultan Mosque anchors a neighborhood of hip cafes and Middle Eastern restaurants. This is where Singapore's Malay-Muslim heritage shines. For dinner, seek out **nasi padang** — a Malay-Indonesian spread where you point at dishes behind a glass counter and build your own plate. Rendang (dry-braised beef in coconut), sambal goreng (spicy fried vegetables), fried chicken with crackling skin, all ladled over steamed rice.

For a mid-range sit-down, look for contemporary Malay restaurants that elevate these dishes with better ingredients and plating. For splurge-level, book a table at Candlenut (the world's first Michelin-starred Peranakan restaurant), where Chef Malcolm Lee turns grandmother recipes into tasting menus.

*Estimated cost: Budget $15 | Mid $45 | Splurge $150*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$35 | Mid ~$75 | Splurge ~$180

## Day 3: The Greatest Hits — Satay, Char Kway Teow & the Farewell Feast

**Morning: One More Kaya Toast (You've Earned It)**

Return to what works. This time, try a different kopitiam in a different neighborhood — maybe near your hotel, or back in Tiong Bahru if you've fallen for it. Order kaya toast again, but add a side of chee cheong fun (rice noodle rolls in sweet soy) or chwee kueh (steamed rice cakes with preserved radish). Singaporean breakfast is a genre worth exploring fully.

*Estimated cost: $5–8*

**Afternoon: Char Kway Teow at Lau Pa Sat, Then Satay at Sunset**

For your final hawker centre meal, head to **Marina Bay** and find the best **char kway teow** you can. This dish separates great hawkers from good ones — flat rice noodles, wok-fried over furious heat with dark soy, cockles, Chinese sausage, bean sprouts, and enough wok hei (smoky breath of the wok) to make your eyes water. The noodles should be slightly charred in places, glistening with rendered pork fat. Old-school hawkers refuse to cook more than one or two portions at a time because the wok must stay screaming hot.

Come back to **Lau Pa Sat** in the evening, when Boon Tat Street closes to traffic and becomes an outdoor **satay** paradise. Dozens of stalls grill skewers over charcoal — chicken, beef, mutton — served with chunky peanut sauce, rice cakes, and raw onion. Order ten sticks minimum; you'll eat twenty. The smoke, the bustle, the $0.70-per-stick math — this is Singapore at its most democratic and delicious.

*Estimated cost: $18–25*

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your final dinner should be the one you'll remember. Three options, depending on your budget:

**Budget:** Return to a hawker centre you loved earlier in the trip. Order the dish you've been thinking about since Day 1. Add a dessert — ice kachang (shaved ice with red beans and syrup) or durian if you're brave. Total: $15–20.

**Mid-range:** Book a table at a modern Singaporean restaurant that riffs on hawker classics — places like Burnt Ends (Australian barbecue with Singaporean soul) or Labyrinth (local ingredients, contemporary technique). These restaurants take familiar flavors and make them feel new. Budget $80–120 per person.

**Splurge:** Go to Odette, Zén, or Les Amis — Singapore's Michelin three-stars, among the best restaurants in Asia. This isn't about Singaporean food specifically; it's about experiencing what the city can do at the highest level. $350–500 per person, wine extra.

*Estimated cost: Budget $18 | Mid $100 | Splurge $400*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$135 | Splurge ~$430

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Queue strategically.** Long lines usually mean good food, but lunchtime queues shrink dramatically by 1:30 PM. Arrive at 11:15 or wait until the office crowd clears.

- **Use the tissue paper system.** At crowded hawker centres, Singaporeans "chope" (reserve) seats by leaving a tissue packet on the table. Respect the tissue. Find an empty table, claim it, then go order.

- **Hydrate between meals.** The heat and humidity are relentless. Drink water or fresh coconut between eating sessions, or you'll fade by dinner.

- **Download the Burpple or Google Maps apps.** Local food blogs are helpful but outdated. Check recent reviews — hawker stalls change hands, quality fluctuates, and the best intel is from people who ate there last week.
