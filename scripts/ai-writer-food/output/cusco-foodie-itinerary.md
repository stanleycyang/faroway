# Cusco Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Cusco

Three days in Cusco, one mission: to eat this city properly. Not the tourist menus, not the safe choices — the real food, from market stalls where señoras have been serving the same dishes for decades to restaurants reimagining Andean ingredients at 3,400 meters. This is the itinerary I built bite by bite, walking steep cobblestones until my legs burned and my stomach begged for mercy.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cusqueños eat lunch as the main meal, typically between 12:30pm and 2:30pm — plan your appetite accordingly. Most market stalls and smaller restaurants are cash-only (soles preferred), though upscale spots take cards. Tipping 10% is appreciated but not expected at casual places. The altitude will shrink your appetite on day one — don't fight it. Drink coca tea constantly. Book any restaurant with a tasting menu at least 48 hours ahead; the best ones fill up.

## Day 1: Plaza de Armas & San Pedro — Market Foundations

Your first day stays low-key geographically, keeping you near the historic center while your body adjusts to breathing at altitude. This is about building your Cusco food vocabulary.

### Morning: Coca Tea & Choclo con Queso

Start slow. Your body is working harder than it knows, and a heavy breakfast will punish you. Head to any café on the colonial arcades surrounding **Plaza de Armas** and order mate de coca — this bitter, grassy tea is non-negotiable for acclimatization. Pair it with **choclo con queso**: massive kernels of Andean corn, each the size of your thumbnail, served with a thick slice of fresh queso fresco that squeaks against your teeth.

The corn here isn't the sweet variety you know. It's starchy, almost creamy, and the cheese is mild enough to let the corn's earthy flavor dominate. This is peasant food elevated by ingredients that simply don't exist at lower altitudes.

**Where:** Any café on the plaza arcades, or the breakfast counter at Organika (if you want something fancier with the same ingredients).
**Cost:** $4–$8

### Afternoon: San Pedro Market Immersion

By noon, walk ten minutes to **San Pedro Market** — the sprawling covered market that feeds Cusco's working class. This isn't sanitized for tourists. It's loud, crowded, and smells like a dozen different meals happening simultaneously.

Your lunch mission: find the row of **juguerías** (juice stands) first. Point at whatever fruit looks unfamiliar — lúcuma, chirimoya, tumbo — and watch them blend it into something revelatory. Then navigate to the cooked food section and sit at one of the communal counters for a bowl of **sopa de quinoa**. This isn't the sad, health-food quinoa you've had elsewhere. The broth is rich with chicken or beef bones, the vegetables are local and seasonal, and the quinoa itself is nuttier, more textured, grown on these mountains.

Afterward, wander. Buy a bag of dried habas (fava beans) for snacking. Watch someone's abuela portion out rocoto peppers. Note which stalls have the longest lines — that's your research for tomorrow.

**Where:** San Pedro Market, any busy food counter (look for steam and crowds)
**Cost:** $6–$12 including juice and snacks

### Evening: First Proper Dinner

You've earned a real sit-down meal. Stay in the **Plaza de Armas** area and find a restaurant with a balcony overlooking the cathedral — the view at dusk, when the stone turns golden, is part of the meal.

Order **lomo saltado**, the Peruvian-Chinese stir-fry that shouldn't work but absolutely does. Strips of beef, soy sauce, tomatoes, onions, and crispy fries — all tossed in a screaming-hot wok. The good versions have beef that's been marinated, fries that stay crisp under the sauce, and a slight char from proper wok technique. The bad versions taste like cafeteria food. If your plate arrives with soggy fries, you chose wrong.

**Where:** Cicciolina, Pachapapa, or Limo (all near the plaza, all competent)
**Cost:** $18–$35 depending on restaurant tier

**Day 1 Total:** Budget ~$28 | Mid ~$45 | Splurge ~$85

## Day 2: San Blas & The Heights — Artisan Quarter Eating

Today climbs. **San Blas** sits above the plaza, up steep cobblestone streets that will test your altitude-adjusted lungs. The neighborhood is quieter, more residential, full of artisan workshops — and some of the city's most interesting cooking.

### Morning: San Blas Bakery Crawl

Start with bread. The Spanish brought wheat to the Andes, and Cusco's bakers have been perfecting it for centuries. Walk up to San Blas and find a bakery selling **t'anta wawa** (if it's near Day of the Dead) or any of the dense, slightly sweet rolls locals eat for breakfast.

Pair it with a proper coffee — San Blas has several specialty cafés now, and Peruvian highland coffee rivals anything in the world. The beans grown in the Urubamba Valley are bright and fruity.

**Where:** The Meeting Place or Jack's Café for coffee; any corner bakery for bread
**Cost:** $5–$10

### Afternoon: Cooking Class & Market Shopping

This is the day to understand *why* Cusco food tastes the way it does. Book a morning cooking class that includes a market visit — you'll return to San Pedro Market with a guide who can explain the fifty varieties of potatoes, the different grades of aji peppers, the fermented corn used for chicha.

Most classes teach you to make a few classics: perhaps an aji de gallina (creamy chicken in walnut-pepper sauce), causa (layered potato terrine), or a ceviche if the class sources fresh fish from the coast. You'll eat what you cook for lunch.

**Where:** Cusco Culinary (solid reputation), Marcelo Batata, or Sky Kitchen
**Cost:** $35–$75 including market visit and lunch

### Evening: Alpaca Done Right

Your dinner tonight focuses on the protein that defines Andean cooking: **alpaca**. This isn't a gimmick — alpaca is remarkably lean, tender, and slightly sweet. It's been eaten here for thousands of years, and the best preparations respect that heritage.

Head to a restaurant that grills it simply over open flame, or serves it in a traditional adobo stew. Avoid anywhere that treats alpaca like a novelty ("Alpaca Burger!" — no). You want it pink in the center, with local herbs and perhaps a smear of huacatay (black mint) sauce.

**Where:** Pachapapa (wood-fired grill in a courtyard) or Chicha (Gastón Acurio's Cusco restaurant)
**Cost:** $25–$50

**Day 2 Total:** Budget ~$65 | Mid ~$95 | Splurge ~$135

## Day 3: Sacred Valley Flavors & The Final Feast

Your last day ventures slightly beyond the city center for lunch, then returns for the meal that will define your Cusco memory.

### Morning: Tamales & Emoliente

Find a street vendor selling **tamales cusqueños** — the Cusco version is wrapped in banana leaves and steamed until dense and savory, filled with chicken or pork and aji panca pepper. Eat it standing on a corner near **San Cristóbal** church, looking down at the terracotta rooftops below.

Chase it with **emoliente**, the warm herbal drink sold from carts throughout the city. It's brewed with barley, flaxseed, dried fruits, and a squeeze of lime — medicinal-tasting but strangely addictive. Locals drink it to cure everything.

**Where:** Street vendors near San Cristóbal or the morning market near San Pedro
**Cost:** $3–$6

### Afternoon: The Cuy Question

You've been avoiding it. Today you answer the question.

**Cuy** — roasted guinea pig — is the most significant traditional food in Andean culture. It's been eaten here for over 5,000 years, served at celebrations, used in religious ceremonies, and yes, it tastes good. The meat is dark, rich, somewhere between rabbit and duck. The skin, when properly roasted in a clay oven, shatters like chicharrón.

You can find cuy at the market (cheap, often deep-fried, hit or miss) or at a restaurant that specializes in traditional preparation (expensive, worth it). A whole cuy serves one to two people and arrives looking exactly like what it is — this is not food for the squeamish.

Make a trip to a restaurant near the **Tipon** archaeological site if you want the full experience. The irrigation terraces there are stunning, rarely crowded, and several family restaurants nearby have been roasting cuy for generations.

**Where:** Quinta Eulalia (near Tipon) or Kusikuy (in the city)
**Cost:** $20–$40 for a whole cuy with sides

### Evening: The Farewell Feast

Your final dinner should be the best meal of the trip. This is the night to book a tasting menu — to let a chef who understands these ingredients tell you a story through ten or twelve courses.

The new Peruvian cuisine movement has reached Cusco, and chefs here are working with ingredients found nowhere else: freeze-dried potatoes from 4,000 meters, wild herbs from the Sacred Valley, cacao from the jungle beyond. The best restaurants source obsessively, cook respectfully, and charge accordingly.

Order the chicha morada to start — this purple corn drink, spiced with cinnamon and cloves, tastes like fruit punch's sophisticated older sister. Then surrender to the menu.

**Where:** MIL (in Moray, if you can get there), Ciro, or KION
**Cost:** $80–$180 for tasting menu with pairings

**Day 3 Total:** Budget ~$85 | Mid ~$120 | Splurge ~$220

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The altitude changes everything.** Your digestion slows, your appetite shrinks, and alcohol hits twice as hard. Eat smaller portions than normal, especially on day one. The locals aren't being stingy with serving sizes — they've adapted to these conditions over millennia.

- **Market lunch is the move.** San Pedro Market's food stalls serve menú del día (soup, main, drink) for around $3–$5, and it's often better than what you'd pay $25 for in the tourist zone. Point at what looks good, sit down, and trust the process.

- **Coca leaves are sacred — don't treat them as a joke.** When offered coca tea or leaves to chew, accept graciously or decline politely. Making cocaine jokes marks you as exactly the kind of tourist nobody wants to serve their best food.

- **Learn to say "está riquísimo" (it's delicious).** Quechua would be better, but even this Spanish phrase, said genuinely to the person who cooked your meal, opens doors that stay closed to silent eaters.
