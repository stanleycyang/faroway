Three days in Bogotá with nothing on the agenda but eating — that's the assignment I gave myself, and I emerged heavier, happier, and with a notebook full of addresses I'd trust with my last meal. This city at 2,600 meters cooks like it has something to prove: warming soups for the altitude chill, grilled meats for celebration, and a coffee culture so ingrained that refusing a tinto feels almost rude. Here's how to do it right.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Bogotanos eat lunch as the main meal (1–3pm), and dinner happens late — 8pm is early. Most restaurants accept cards, but markets and street vendors are cash-only; pull pesos before heading to Paloquemao or any plaza food stall. Tipping is 10% and often included as a "propina sugerida" on the bill — you can decline, but don't. Reserve dinner in Zona Rosa and Parque 93 on weekends or you'll wait. And pace yourself on drinks: altitude makes alcohol hit faster and harder than you expect.

## Day 1: La Candelaria — The Historic Heart

### Morning: Changua in the Colonial Quarter

Start where Bogotá started. La Candelaria's narrow streets smell like fresh bread by 7am, and you're here for changua — the breakfast that separates tourists from the committed. This is milk soup. With poached eggs floating in it. And stale bread soaking at the bottom. It sounds wrong until you taste it: warming, sustaining, exactly what 2,600 meters of altitude and colonial stone walls demand.

Find it at any traditional café along the streets near Plaza del Chorro de Quevedo, where elderly Bogotanos have been eating this for decades before work. The versions in tourist-facing restaurants are fine, but the best bowls come from places with plastic chairs and no menu — just changua, chocolate santafereño, and maybe tamales if you're lucky.

*Estimated cost: $3–$5 for changua with bread and a tinto*

### Afternoon: Paloquemao Market at Full Roar

By late morning, make your way to Paloquemao, the massive working market that feeds the city. Skip the Instagram flower section (fine, take one photo) and head for the food stalls in the back. This is lunch.

The fritanga vendors here serve mixed grill platters — chorizo, morcilla (blood sausage), chicharrón, grilled chicken — with arepas, papas criollas (tiny yellow potatoes), and ají on the side. You'll point at what looks good, someone will pile a styrofoam plate embarrassingly high, and you'll eat standing at a counter next to construction workers on break. The juices section deserves a stop: lulo, maracuyá, guanábana, things you've never heard of, blended fresh for under a dollar.

This market is completely local, especially at dawn when the flower section runs at full chaos. Go hungry.

*Estimated cost: $6–$10 for a serious fritanga plate and fresh juice*

### Evening: The Soup That Defines Bogotá

Dinner on Day 1 has to be ajiaco. This is Bogotá's signature dish — a chicken soup made with three types of potato (criolla, pastusa, sabanera), corn on the cob, and guasca, a local herb that makes the soup taste like nowhere else on earth. It comes with capers, cream, and avocado on the side; you add them yourself, customizing each spoonful.

The best ajiaco I found wasn't in Zona Rosa — it was in La Perseverancia, the working-class neighborhood just uphill from La Candelaria. This barrio has fed politicians, artists, and locals for generations, and the ajiaco restaurants here serve bowls the size of your head for prices that feel like theft. The neighborhood has a reputation, but during dinner hours it's full of families. Go before 8pm, eat slowly, and walk downhill after to a Candelaria bar for aguardiente.

*Estimated cost: Budget $8–$12 / Mid $15–$25 with drinks / Splurge $30–$40 for a full dinner with appetizers and wine*

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~$20 | Mid ~$45 | Splurge ~$55

## Day 2: Chapinero — The Bohemian Sprawl

### Morning: Coffee as Religion

Chapinero is where Bogotá's creative class lives, and they take coffee with obsessive seriousness. Forget the tinto — today you're drinking specialty Colombian coffee prepared by someone who can tell you the exact finca it came from.

Find a third-wave café in Chapinero Alto (there are dozens) and order a pour-over or a flat white made with beans from Huila or Nariño. Pair it with an arepa de choclo — sweet corn arepa with cheese — or almojábanas, the cheesy bread balls that exist specifically to dunk in hot drinks. This neighborhood doesn't wake up early, so 9am feels like dawn here.

*Estimated cost: $5–$8 for proper coffee and breakfast*

### Afternoon: Tamales and a Cooking Detour

For lunch, hunt tamales. These are not Mexican tamales — Colombian tamales are larger, wrapped in banana leaves, stuffed with rice, chicken or pork, chickpeas, and vegetables, all steamed until the masa absorbs everything. The best ones require two hands and come from vendors who make them at home and sell from coolers.

In La Macarena, the arts district between Chapinero and La Candelaria, you'll find a mix of galleries, small restaurants, and home cooks selling tamales from doorways on weekend mornings. Buy one, find a bench near the Quinta de Bolívar gardens (Simón Bolívar's former home — quiet, colonial, worth the detour), and eat slowly.

If you want hands-on, book an afternoon cooking class in Chapinero. Several small operations run 3–4 hour workshops where you'll make ajiaco or arepas from scratch, visit a local market for ingredients, and eat everything you cook. It's the most effective way to understand Colombian flavor profiles: the three-potato technique, the guasca that makes ajiaco unique, why corn masa behaves the way it does.

*Estimated cost: Tamal $3–$4 / Cooking class $40–$70*

### Evening: The Chapinero Splurge

Dinner tonight should stretch. Chapinero has some of Bogotá's most ambitious restaurants — chefs doing modern Colombian cuisine that takes everything you've eaten in markets and elevates it without losing the soul.

Look for tasting menus that feature regional ingredients: Amazonian fish, Andean potatoes, Caribbean coconut rice reimagined. The restaurants along Calle 69 and up into Chapinero Alto range from casual-inventive to full white-tablecloth experiences. Reserve ahead, arrive hungry, and tell them you want to taste Colombia.

After dinner, walk to the Bogotá Beer Company rooftop in Chapinero Alto. The craft beer scene here is real, the rooftop views over the city are properly good, and you'll need a digestif anyway.

*Estimated cost: Budget $20–$30 / Mid $50–$70 / Splurge $100–$150 for tasting menu with wine*

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~$35 | Mid ~$85 | Splurge ~$175

## Day 3: Usaquén and the Farewell Feast

### Morning: Sunday Market Grazing

If you've timed this right and Day 3 is Sunday, you're in luck. Usaquén, the charming northern neighborhood with colonial architecture and a famous antique market, transforms into a sprawling food carnival every Sunday.

Start with empanadas from the vendors near the church — beef or chicken, fried fresh, eaten with ají picante. Then wander: you'll find obleas (wafer cookies with arequipe, the Colombian dulce de leche), fresh fruit with lime and salt, and aguapanela con queso — hot sugar cane water served with chunks of fresh white cheese that you drop in and fish out with a spoon. It tastes like nothing and everything at once: sweet, salty, warm, strange, completely Colombian.

This is grazing, not a meal. Eat small, sample widely, and let the market dictate your pace.

*Estimated cost: $8–$15 for extensive grazing*

### Afternoon: The Chicha Detour

Before your final dinner, seek out chicha — the fermented corn beverage that Colombians drank for centuries before the Spanish arrived. It's mildly alcoholic, slightly sour, and not served everywhere. In La Candelaria, a few traditional spots still make it the old way; ask around or look for chicherías tucked into side streets. One glass is cultural education; two is a nice buzz.

For a more substantial lunch, return to La Perseverancia or find a corrientazo — the set lunch that working Bogotanos eat daily. For around $4–$6, you get soup, a main plate with rice, meat, beans, plantain, and salad, plus juice. It's not exciting, but it's honest, and it's how this city actually eats when no one's watching.

*Estimated cost: $5–$10 for corrientazo and chicha*

### Evening: The Meal Worth Remembering

Your last dinner should be the one you came for. Two options:

**The Theatrical Choice**: Andrés Carne de Res in Chía (30 minutes outside the city) is not a restaurant — it's a fever dream. Multiple floors, live music, decorations covering every surface, servers in costume, and some of the best grilled meat in Colombia. It's wildly fun, romantically chaotic, and absolutely worth the taxi ride. Go hungry, stay late, dance if they make you.

**The Refined Choice**: Zona Rosa and Parque 93 have Bogotá's highest-end restaurants. Book somewhere doing contemporary Colombian cuisine — there are several doing it brilliantly — and let them feed you a final survey of everything this country grows, raises, and ferments. Expect regional fish, heritage corn, Andean tubers you've never seen, and plating that belongs in magazines.

Either way, this is the meal that should cost more than the others. You've eaten Bogotá properly; end it like you mean it.

*Estimated cost: Budget $25–$35 / Mid $60–$90 / Splurge $120–$200*

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~$40 | Mid ~$85 | Splurge ~$225

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Altitude kills appetite the first day** — drink water constantly and don't force big meals until Day 2. The soup-heavy cuisine exists partly because warming, liquid-based foods are easier to digest up here.

- **Tinto is served constantly and refusing it is weird** — it's tiny, sweet, black coffee offered as a gesture of hospitality. Just drink it, or at least hold the cup warmly while you chat.

- **The best food isn't in Zona Rosa** — it's in Paloquemao, La Perseverancia, and the corrientazo spots where office workers eat. Follow the plastic chairs.

- **Carry cash in small bills** — market vendors and traditional restaurants often can't break 50,000 peso notes. Get change at your hotel before you eat.
