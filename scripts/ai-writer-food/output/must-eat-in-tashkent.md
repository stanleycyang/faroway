The steam rises from the kazan in soft, fragrant clouds, and I'm watching a man who has been cooking plov since before I was born. It's 11 AM at Chorsu Bazaar, and he's about to serve me a plate of rice so perfect, so saturated with rendered lamb fat and caramelized carrots, that I'll think about it for weeks. This is Tashkent eating: unhurried, communal, built on recipes that have survived empires.

#### Plov (Osh)

There is no dish more central to Uzbek identity than plov—called *osh* here, which literally means "food," as if nothing else quite qualifies. The Tashkent version is distinctive: the rice stays relatively loose, each grain separate and glistening, stained gold from carrots and the rendered fat of lamb shoulder. Beneath the rice, you'll find whole heads of garlic, soft enough to spread like butter, and chunks of meat that have given everything to the pot.

The flavor is what gets you. It's not spicy—it's deep. Cumin dominates, followed by coriander, then something almost sweet from the slow-cooked carrots that have essentially melted into the fat. The rice itself has absorbed hours of simmering lamb broth, so each bite carries that savory weight.

Plov is traditionally cooked in a *kazan*, a heavy cast-iron cauldron set over open flame, and it's almost exclusively a morning dish. This isn't arbitrary—it's practical. Plov is heavy, meant to fuel a day of work, and Uzbeks believe the digestive system handles fat better before noon. You'll find the best versions at *oshxonas* (plov centers), simple cafeterias where the dish is made in industrial quantities and served by the kilo. The Central Asian Plov Center on Iftihor Street is famous, but honestly, follow the smoke at any neighborhood bazaar around 10 AM and you'll eat well. A generous plate runs $2–4, and it will hold you until dinner.

#### Shashlik

The smell finds you before you see the grill—charred fat dripping onto coals, meat crackling, smoke curling through the evening air. Shashlik in Tashkent is a serious matter. The skewers are thick, the cuts generous, and the preparation minimal: salt, maybe some onion juice, fire. That's it.

Lamb is king, specifically cuts with fat still attached—*jigar* (liver) is popular, as is *dumba*, the famously rich fat from the sheep's tail, threaded between pieces of meat so it bastes everything as it melts. The texture when done right is exterior char giving way to pink, impossibly juicy interior. You eat it with raw onion rings, a scattering of fresh herbs, and flatbread to catch the drippings.

Shashlik stalls cluster around any park after sunset, and near the Alisher Navoi metro station, you'll find a particularly good concentration. This is evening food, social food—you stand around plastic tables with strangers, everyone slightly greasy-fingered and happy. Expect to pay $3–5 for a satisfying serving of several skewers.

#### Samsa

If plov is Tashkent's soul, samsa is its heartbeat—the thing people grab on the way to work, eat standing at bus stops, bring home in paper bags. These aren't the small, neat triangles you might know from Indian cuisine. Uzbek samsa are substantial: fist-sized parcels of dough wrapped around spiced lamb and onion, baked until the pastry shatters.

The magic is in the *tandyr*, the cylindrical clay oven where samsa are stuck directly to the walls, baking in fierce heat that puffs the dough and renders the fat inside. The best ones have a bottom crust that's almost cracker-crisp, a top that's flaky and bronzed, and a filling that floods your mouth with lamb juice on the first bite. The onion is key—it's roughly chopped, generous, and goes almost jammy during cooking.

You'll find samsa everywhere, but the freshest come from bakeries with their own *tandyr*. Arrive mid-morning when a new batch emerges, still too hot to hold comfortably. The standard meat version costs about $0.50–1; pumpkin-filled samsa (*samsa kadi*) appear in autumn and are worth seeking out.

#### Lagman

Pulled noodles swirl in a bowl of rust-red broth, and I'm trying to figure out where Uzbekistan ends and China begins. Lagman is Central Asia's great fusion dish, a descendant of Chinese hand-pulled noodles that traveled the Silk Road and picked up local flavors along the way.

The noodles are the star—thick, chewy, slightly irregular from being stretched and slapped by hand. They swim in a tomato-based broth loaded with bell peppers, onions, celery, and chunks of beef. The spicing walks a line between familiar and foreign: garlic, cumin, and a gentle warmth from dried chili. Some versions are soupy, others (*kovurma lagman*) are stir-fried until the noodles crisp slightly at the edges.

Watch the noodle-making if you can—it's performance art. A ball of dough becomes a skein of noodles in about forty seconds of twisting and pulling. Old-town restaurants near the Khast Imam complex do this in full view. Lagman is lunch food, filling and affordable at $2–4 per bowl.

#### Manti

These dumplings are architectural. Large, pleated pouches of thin dough hold spiced lamb and onion, steamed until the wrapper goes translucent and the meat releases its juices into an interior pool of broth. You bite one corner, slurp carefully to avoid scalding, then eat the rest in a single bite.

Manti tell a story of Mongol influence, of nomadic cooks who needed efficient one-pot meals. Each region of Central Asia has its own shape and filling, but Tashkent manti tend toward the generous—these are not delicate bites. They're served with a drizzle of sour cream and sometimes a ladleful of the cooking liquid. Uzbek families make them together, an activity that can occupy an entire afternoon.

Specialty manti houses serve them by the portion (usually five to six dumplings), often with katnyk, a tangy yogurt drink. Around $3 buys a filling plate.

#### Non (Lepyoshka)

The bread is cooked until it blisters, stamped in the center with a decorative pattern, and it emerges from the tandyr almost too beautiful to tear. Non—called *lepyoshka* in Russian—is eaten at every meal, used to scoop, to wrap, to soak up sauces. It's never cut with a knife, only broken by hand, which Uzbeks consider a sign of respect.

The texture is dual: the center stays soft and slightly dense, while the edges char and crisp into something almost cracker-like. Standard non is brushed with egg wash; festive versions are sprinkled with sesame or nigella seeds. The smell is intoxicating—yeasty, warm, faintly smoky from the clay oven.

Never step over bread, never place it upside down, never throw it away. These aren't superstitions so much as deeply held beliefs about the sacredness of grain. A fresh round costs less than $0.50.

#### Norin

This dish looks almost austere—pale hand-cut noodles tossed with shredded boiled meat, nothing more—but the flavor runs deep. Norin is horse meat in its most traditional form, though lamb versions exist for the hesitant. The noodles are rolled thin and cut into short strips, the meat is pulled by hand, and everything is dressed with nothing but its own cooking broth.

The result is subtle, savory, and surprisingly addictive. Horse meat has a particular sweetness, a slight gaminess that tastes like open steppe. This is winter food, restorative and rich, often served at celebrations. You'll find it at traditional restaurants specializing in Uzbek cuisine, typically priced around $4–6.

#### Halva

Tashkent's halva crumbles when you bite it, leaving sesame sweetness coating your tongue. Unlike Middle Eastern tahini-based versions, Uzbek halva is drier, more granular, threaded with pistachios or walnuts. It's sold by weight at bazaars, sliced from dense blocks with wire cutters.

#### Ayran and Green Tea

You wash it all down with *ayran*, a salty yogurt drink that cuts through fat like nothing else, or *kok choy*, the green tea served in every chaikhana (teahouse). Tea is poured into shallow bowls, refilled constantly, and always offered to guests first. A pot costs nearly nothing and buys you hours of shade and conversation.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main meal. Tashkentis eat plov by noon, keep dinners lighter, and view breakfast as an afterthought—bread, tea, perhaps some fruit. When you sit at a table, bread will arrive first; tear it and share it before anything else comes. If you're invited to someone's home, bring sweets and expect to eat three times what seems reasonable. Refusing food is mildly offensive; taking small portions of everything is the graceful move.

At restaurants, ordering is communal. Nobody gets their own dish—you order for the table and everyone shares. Tea is constantly replenished by the youngest person present. And always: the guest eats first, gets the best seat, and receives the choicest piece of meat.
