Three days in Yerevan taught me that Armenian food is built on patience — slow-simmered meats, fermented vegetables aged through winter, lavash baked in underground tonirs that have been firing for generations. This isn't a city where you graze; you sit down, you eat seriously, and someone's grandmother is probably watching to make sure you finish. Here's how to do it right.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Armenians eat late — lunch rarely starts before 1pm, dinner after 8pm. Most restaurants accept cards, but cash (Armenian dram) is essential for markets and hole-in-wall spots. Tipping 10% is standard at sit-down restaurants; round up for casual places. Book weekend dinners at popular spots a day ahead. Learn "barev" (hello) and "shnorhakalutyun" (thank you) — it changes how people feed you.

## Day 1: The Center and Its Foundations

**Morning — Start Where the City Starts**

Begin in the City Center at a classic Armenian breakfast spot before the heat and crowds arrive. You're looking for *zhingyalov hats* — flatbread stuffed with a dozen wild herbs, a specialty brought by Artsakh Armenians that's become a Yerevan staple. The bread should be thin enough to see through, the herbs slightly bitter and intensely green. Pair it with thick Armenian coffee and a glass of tahn (salted yogurt drink) if you're feeling brave. Cost: $5–8.

**Afternoon — The Market Education**

Lunch belongs to the GUM Market on Mashtots Avenue — a Soviet-era covered market now packed with cheese vendors, spice mountains, and dried fruit stacked like jewels. Don't eat before you arrive. Walk the full circuit first: taste the aged cheeses (ask for *chanakh* or *motal*), sample the paper-thin *basturma* (air-dried beef crusted in fenugreek and paprika), grab a handful of sour *lavashak* (fruit leather). Then find a stall selling *khorovats* — Armenian barbecue. Order pork or lamb ribs, charred properly and served with nothing but raw onion, herbs, and lavash. This is lunch. Stand at the counter, eat with your hands, watch the market move around you.

After, walk to Republic Square to let it settle, then find somewhere selling *gata* — a sweet bread with a filling of butter and sugar that tastes like shortbread went to finishing school. Cost: $12–18 total for market eating.

**Evening — The First Proper Dinner**

For dinner, stay central but go upscale. The City Center has several serious Armenian restaurants where families celebrate birthdays and the kitchen takes no shortcuts. You want *dolma* to start — grape leaves stuffed with spiced meat and rice, served warm with cold yogurt. Then *ghapama* if it's in season (fall/winter) — a whole pumpkin stuffed with rice, nuts, and dried fruit, baked until it collapses sweetly. If not, order *khashlama* — a simple beef stew that's all about the quality of meat and the depth of broth. Finish with Armenian brandy (they call it cognac, the French disagree, drink it anyway). Cost: Budget $25 / Mid $40 / Splurge $70.

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$65 | Splurge ~$95

## Day 2: Old Town and the Edges

**Morning — Bread and History**

Start in the Old Town, near the Hrazdan Gorge, where the city feels older and quieter. Find a *tonir* bakery — the clay ovens are often underground, and women slap lavash onto the curved walls where it bakes in seconds. Buy it warm, tear it, eat it plain. This is the bread that UNESCO protected, and you'll understand why when it's still steaming. Add some local honey and a chunk of white cheese from any corner shop. Sit in a courtyard somewhere and eat slowly. Cost: $4–7.

**Afternoon — The Neighborhood Deep Dive**

Lunch takes you to Kond, Yerevan's oldest neighborhood — a hillside maze of crumbling Soviet blocks and older stone houses where the tourist trail doesn't reach. There's no GPS-friendly restaurant here; you're looking for a home-kitchen spot or a small *khinkali* place (Georgian dumplings adopted enthusiastically by Armenians). Order them by the dozen, grab the twisted top, bite a hole, drink the broth, then eat the rest. The dough should be thick but not gummy, the meat loose and peppery. A local beer and some pickled vegetables on the side.

Walk it off through the Arts Quarter afterward — galleries, street art, and inevitable coffee. Find somewhere doing soorj (Armenian coffee) properly: brewed in a *jezve*, served with the grounds still swirling, alongside some dried apricots. Cost: $10–15.

**Evening — Learning to Cook It**

For dinner, consider a cooking class — several spots in the City Center offer evening sessions where you learn to make *harissa* (the ancient wheat-and-meat porridge, not the chili paste) or properly stuffed vegetables. You eat what you make, you drink wine while you cook, and you leave understanding why Armenian food takes the time it does. If cooking isn't your speed, find a restaurant specializing in *ishkhan* — Sevan trout from the massive lake outside the city. It should be simply grilled, served with butter and herbs. Cost: Cooking class $45–70 / Restaurant dinner Budget $25 / Mid $45 / Splurge $80.

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~$55 | Mid ~$85 | Splurge ~$150

## Day 3: The Farewell Circuit

**Morning — The Full Armenian Breakfast**

Your last morning demands the complete spread. Head to an Old Town café known for traditional breakfast and order the works: *omelette* with tomatoes and herbs, *jajik* (cucumber-yogurt dip), fresh lavash, several cheeses, sliced *sujuk* (dry sausage), and vegetables pulled from someone's garden. Armenian breakfasts are not small — they're fuel for a day of physical labor that you won't actually be doing. Coffee, tahn, maybe a digestive Armenian moonshine if you're feeling committed. Cost: $10–15.

**Afternoon — The Grand Market Circuit**

Return to the markets for a final sweep, but this time hit the Vernissage flea market area first, then work back toward food. You're looking for provisions to take home: dried herbs, bags of *sumac*, pomegranate molasses, vacuum-packed basturma if your airline allows it. At the food stalls, eat a final *lahmajun* — Armenian flatbread spread thin with spiced minced meat, rolled around herbs and lemon juice. It should shatter slightly when you bite, never soggy. Find *sukhindz* (dried fruit and walnut "sausage" dipped in grape must) for dessert. Wander. Taste. Buy things for people back home. Cost: $15–20 eating, budget extra for gifts.

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Dinner is your last act. Go to the Business District or a rooftop somewhere with a view toward Ararat — the mountain that dominates Yerevan's skyline and Armenian identity. Order as if you won't eat Armenian food again for years: *manti* (tiny dumplings in garlic yogurt), a full spread of *mezze* (hummus, muhammara, baba ganoush, all slightly different than you've had elsewhere), the best *khorovats* you can find — ask for *kaburga* (bone-in ribs) if available. Finish with *pakhlava* — the Armenian version, soaked in honey and heavy with walnuts. Brandy. Coffee. Sit until they start stacking chairs. Cost: Budget $35 / Mid $55 / Splurge $100.

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~$65 | Mid ~$95 | Splurge ~$140

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Pace yourself with bread** — lavash is endless and refilled constantly. Leave room for the meat.
- **Accept hospitality** — if a vendor offers you a taste, take it and stay to chat. Armenians feed strangers seriously, and refusing is worse than not buying.
- **The brandy matters** — Ararat is the famous brand, but ask servers what they'd actually drink. The smaller producers are often better.
- **Lunch is the big meal** — Armenians eat their heaviest in the afternoon. Plan accordingly and go lighter in the evening if three large meals defeat you.
