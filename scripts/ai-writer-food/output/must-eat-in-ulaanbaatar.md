The steam rises from the bowl in front of me, carrying the scent of mutton fat and wild onion through the cramped dining room of a khuushuur shop near the State Department Store. It's 7 AM in Ulaanbaatar, the temperature outside hovers at minus fifteen, and I'm learning what Mongolians have known for centuries: that a bowl of hot soup isn't just breakfast here—it's survival, it's comfort, it's the first act of care in a brutal climate. I wrap both hands around the metal bowl and let the warmth seep into my bones.

---

#### Buuz (Steamed Dumplings)

Picture this: a pleated parcel of dough, thin enough to be translucent at its crimped peak, bulging with seasoned mutton that's been hand-minced with onion and just enough fat to stay impossibly juicy. When you bite through the top—always the top, so the broth inside doesn't scald your chin—the meat releases a rush of savory liquid that tastes like the Mongolian steppe distilled into a single mouthful. The flavor is clean and direct: lamb, salt, onion, a whisper of black pepper. No soy sauce. No ginger. Nothing hiding behind anything else.

Buuz exist because of Tsagaan Sar, the Lunar New Year, when Mongolian families gather to make thousands of these dumplings together. The pleating is passed from grandmother to granddaughter, and a family's skill is measured partly by how many perfect folds crown each dumpling. But you don't need to wait for the festival—every canteen, every guanz, every food court in Ulaanbaatar serves them daily.

Find them at any local canteen by 11 AM, when they're freshest. The State Department Store food court is surprisingly excellent, or head to the working-class guanz joints near Naran Tuul market. Expect to pay around $1.50–$2.50 for a plate of four or five.

#### Khuushuur (Fried Meat Pastries)

If buuz are the elegant elder sibling, khuushuur are the rowdy younger cousin you actually want at the party. These half-moon pastries hit the hot oil and emerge blistered and golden, the dough crackling between your teeth before giving way to the same spiced mutton filling. But here, the fat has rendered slightly, the edges have crisped into something almost architectural, and there's a satisfaction in the crunch that steamed dumplings can never match.

Khuushuur became the portable food of Naadam, Mongolia's summer festival of horse racing, archery, and wrestling. You'll see families eating them from paper bags in the stands, grease staining their fingers, because nothing pairs better with watching a wrestler throw his opponent than fried meat in hand. The dish is older than the festival, though—nomadic herders needed food that traveled well and delivered maximum energy. This is that food.

Seek them out at street stalls during summer months, or at any canteen year-round. The best ones come from grandmothers cooking out of home kitchens in the ger districts, if you can find your way there. Around $0.60–$1 each.

#### Guriltai Shul (Noodle Soup)

I didn't understand Mongolian cooking until someone placed a bowl of guriltai shul in front of me and I watched the fat globules catch the fluorescent light. This is not a refined soup. This is a declaration: here is mutton broth, rich and amber, bobbing with hand-cut noodles thick as shoelaces and shreds of meat still clinging to small bones. The noodles are chewy and substantial, made from a dough that's been rolled and sliced minutes before hitting the pot. The broth coats your mouth with an unctuous warmth that lingers.

Mongolia is the second-coldest capital in the world, and this soup explains why anyone stays. When the wind cuts through six layers of clothing and your phone dies from the cold, you need a bowl of something that fights back. Guriltai shul is that weapon. The simplicity is the point—mutton, flour, water, salt—because when your ancestors were herding livestock across frozen plains, you didn't have access to thirty ingredients.

Every guanz in the city serves their version. Go early—by 8 AM—when the broth is freshest and the noodles haven't sat too long. The canteens around the central post office are reliable. About $2–$3 per bowl.

#### Khorkhog (Stone-Cooked Mutton)

Nothing I've eaten in Ulaanbaatar rewired my understanding of meat like khorkhog. The technique: a metal milk jug (traditionally a sealed container) filled with lamb, potatoes, carrots, and river stones heated until they glow. The lid goes on. The stones cook the meat from within while the metal cooks from without. When the vessel finally opens—sometimes thirty minutes later, sometimes an hour—the lamb falls apart at a touch, and the fat has gone silky, almost sweet.

There's a ritual here. Someone hands you one of the hot stones and you pass it between your palms—they say it improves blood circulation, but I think it's really about patience, about waiting while the smell of roasted lamb perfumes the entire room. The meat itself tastes cleaner than any lamb I've had, less gamy, more mineral. Maybe it's the Mongolian grass. Maybe it's the stones. Maybe I've just been eating inferior lamb my whole life.

This isn't a restaurant dish—it's a countryside dish, a celebration dish. But several traditional restaurants on Seoul Street prepare it with advance notice. Expect to pay $15–$25 for a shared pot serving three or four people. Call ahead.

#### Airag (Fermented Mare's Milk)

The first sip made me wince. The second made me curious. By the third, I was reaching for more. Airag is fermented mare's milk, fizzy and sour and unlike any dairy product I've consumed in the West. It lands somewhere between thin yogurt and a very mild beer, with an effervescence that tickles the roof of your mouth and a tang that announces its living, active cultures.

Mongolian herders have been making airag for at least a thousand years, churning fresh mare's milk in leather bags until natural fermentation transforms it into something nourishing enough to sustain warriors on horseback. The alcohol content is low—around 2%—but the probiotics are through the roof. Genghis Khan's armies supposedly ran on this stuff, and after drinking it in a ger outside the city while watching horses graze, I believed it.

Fresh airag is a summer drink—mares don't produce year-round—so timing matters. Visit between June and September, and seek it at the countryside or at traditional restaurants. Around $1–$2 per bowl.

#### Tsuivan (Fried Noodles with Meat)

If guriltai shul is the soup of survival, tsuivan is its satisfying, carb-loaded cousin. Hand-pulled noodles get stir-fried with mutton (or beef, or goat) and whatever vegetables are on hand—usually cabbage, carrots, potatoes—until everything melds into a pan-fried tangle. The noodles absorb the rendered fat and develop chewy, slightly crispy edges where they've touched the wok.

There's nothing subtle about tsuivan. It's working-class food designed to fill you up before a long day, and it succeeds completely. The dish reflects the Soviet influence that shaped Ulaanbaatar's cuisine in the 20th century—those noodles share DNA with Central Asian laghman—while remaining distinctly Mongolian in its reliance on meat and its suspicion of excessive seasoning.

Every canteen has it on the menu, usually by noon. The food court beneath Mercury Market serves a reliable version. $2–$4 per plate.

---

#### Boortsog (Fried Dough)

At every Mongolian celebration—weddings, Tsagaan Sar, the birth of a child—you'll find a tower of boortsog stacked on the table. These are fried dough shapes, sometimes sweetened, sometimes savory, always crispy on the outside and tender within. They range from small twisted knots to larger rectangular pillows, and eating one triggers an immediate craving for another.

The tower matters. During Tsagaan Sar, families build elaborate pyramids of boortsog that signify prosperity—the higher the stack, the greater the blessing. But stripped of ceremony, they're simply excellent with salted milk tea, their mild sweetness cutting through the drink's richness.

Find them at bakeries throughout the city or at any traditional restaurant. Around $0.50–$1 for a small bag.

---

#### Suutei Tsai (Salted Milk Tea)

Before you drink it, understand: this is not chai. This is not sweet. This is tea brewed with milk, water, and salt, served in bowls throughout the day, offered to every guest who enters a Mongolian home. The flavor is subtle and grounding—earthy tea leaves, rich milk, a salinity that satisfies rather than startles.

Mongolians drink suutei tsai the way other cultures drink water. It accompanies every meal, every conversation, every pause in the day. Refusing it when offered is considered impolite, and honestly, once you've adjusted to the salt, you'll stop wanting to refuse. It's the liquid equivalent of a warm blanket.

Available everywhere, all day, for under $0.50. Just say yes.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Mongolians eat lunch as the main meal—arrive at canteens between 12 and 1 PM for the freshest preparations and the most options. Breakfast is lighter (often leftover soup or tea with boortsog), and dinner happens late, sometimes past 8 PM. When sharing khorkhog or other communal dishes, the eldest is served first, and you should wait for them to begin.

Don't expect menus in English outside tourist restaurants. Point at what others are eating, or learn to recognize Cyrillic script for the key words: буузы (buuz), хуушуур (khuushuur), цуйван (tsuivan). Tipping isn't expected at casual guanz, but leaving the small change is appreciated. And bring cash—many local spots don't accept cards.

Most importantly, eat with your hands when appropriate. Grab that khuushuur. Pass the hot stone. Stop worrying about grease on your fingers. This is food built for living, not for Instagram.
