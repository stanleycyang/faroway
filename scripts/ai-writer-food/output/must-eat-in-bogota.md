The steam rises from the clay bowl and fogs my glasses before I can even lift the spoon. It's 7 AM in La Perseverancia, a working-class neighborhood where the ajiaco has been simmering since before dawn, and I'm about to understand why Bogotanos treat this soup like religion.

Bogotá sits at 2,600 meters, where the air is thin and the mornings bite with cold. This is a city that needs warming food, and over centuries, it has perfected the art of dishes that hug you from the inside. After three weeks of eating through markets, street corners, and family kitchens across this sprawling capital, I'm convinced that Colombian cuisine's soul lives here — in the high-altitude capital where Andean ingredients meet centuries of tradition.

#### Ajiaco

This is the dish. The one Bogotanos will fight you over — whose grandmother makes the best, which restaurant hasn't compromised, whether the guascas herb is absolutely essential (it is). Ajiaco is a chicken soup, technically, but calling it that feels like calling the ocean "some water."

Three types of potatoes go into the pot: criolla (small, yellow, creamy), sabanera (floury, for body), and pastusa (to thicken). As they cook for hours, the potatoes break down at different rates, creating a broth that's simultaneously clear and rich, with layers of texture from silky to chunky. Shredded chicken falls apart on your tongue. Corn on the cob adds sweetness. And then there's guascas — a wild herb that tastes like nothing else, somewhere between oregano and tarragon but earthier, the flavor that makes ajiaco unmistakably Bogotano.

The dish arrives with sides: heavy cream to swirl in, capers for salt and brine, sliced avocado for richness. You build each spoonful differently, and by the time you reach the bottom of the bowl, you've had a dozen variations of the same perfect thing.

Find it at La Puerta Falsa in La Candelaria (Colombia's oldest restaurant, serving since 1816) or at no-name spots in La Perseverancia where the bowls are bigger and the prices lower. A generous serving runs $4–$6, and you won't need dinner.

#### Changua

If ajiaco is Bogotá's Sunday best, changua is its Monday morning alarm clock. This is the traditional breakfast of the Andean highlands — a thin soup of milk and water with scallions, cilantro, and a poached egg floating on top, served with stale bread meant to soak up every last drop.

The first spoonful surprises you. It's mild, almost bland, until the egg yolk breaks and the richness floods in. The bread turns soft and porridge-like. The cilantro cuts through the cream. It's simple to the point of austerity, a dish born from cold mornings and limited ingredients, and it works in ways that shouldn't make sense.

Changua is polarizing — even some Colombians hate it. But at 6 AM in a tiled breakfast spot near Paloquemao Market, with the altitude making your head swim slightly and your body craving warmth, it becomes exactly what you need. Most breakfast spots in traditional neighborhoods serve it for $2–$3. Order it con calado (with the bread already dunked) or sin calado if you want to control the soaking yourself.

#### Tamales Bogotanos

Every region of Colombia has its tamale, and the Bogotano version is a study in restraint compared to the massive tolimense from the south. Wrapped in banana leaves that have been softened over flame, the corn masa steams into a dense, savory cake studded with chicken, pork, carrot, potato, and sometimes a boiled egg or chickpea.

Unwrapping a tamal is a ritual. The banana leaf unfolds like a present, releasing steam that smells of corn and slow-cooked meat. The masa should be moist, never dry, with a texture between polenta and dense cake. You eat it with hot chocolate — yes, savory with sweet, and yes, it works beautifully.

Sunday mornings are tamal mornings. Families buy them from neighborhood specialists who've been making them for decades. In Usaquén, vendors set up near the church plaza. In La Candelaria, look for the women with stacked banana-leaf bundles in thermal containers. A good tamal costs $3–$5 and constitutes a complete breakfast.

#### Fritanga

This is the Colombian mixed grill, and it arrives on a wooden board that barely fits on the table. Chicharrón (fried pork belly with the skin puffed and crackling), morcilla (blood sausage, soft and iron-rich), chorizo snapping with fat, longaniza (a longer, milder sausage), costillas (ribs glazed sticky-sweet), and sometimes chunchullo — intestines crisped until they shatter like chips.

Around the meat: papas criollas roasted golden, fried plantain caramelized at the edges, arepas for scooping, and yuca with that floury, almost waxy texture that makes it such a perfect foil for grease. Ají (hot sauce) and lime wedges sit alongside, but this is food that barely needs seasoning. The pork speaks loudly enough.

Fritanga is weekend food, celebration food, the platter you share with a table of friends while someone orders another round of Club Colombia. Andrés Carne de Res in Chía (a 40-minute drive north) serves a famous version amid theatrical chaos, but the same platter appears at simpler asaderos throughout the city. Budget $8–$15 per person depending on the spot and the portion.

#### Aguapanela con Queso

The simplest pleasure on this list, and possibly the most comforting. Panela is unrefined cane sugar, pressed into hard blocks that Colombian kitchens keep the way others keep salt. Dissolve it in hot water, and you get aguapanela — a drink that tastes like liquid brown sugar with grassy, molasses-like undertones.

Here's where it gets interesting: drop in chunks of fresh white cheese. The cheese doesn't fully melt; instead, it softens and turns squeaky, releasing salt into the sweetness. You fish out the pieces with a spoon and chew them while they're still warm. It's breakfast, it's an afternoon snack, it's what your grandmother makes when you're sick.

Street vendors sell aguapanela in plastic cups for less than a dollar. Cafés serve it in ceramic mugs with more ceremony. The cheese varies — some use cuajada (fresh curd), others use queso campesino. All versions remind you that the best food often comes from two or three ingredients handled with care.

#### Chicha

This one requires an open mind. Chicha is fermented corn, drunk by Indigenous Colombians for at least a thousand years before the Spanish arrived. The traditional preparation involved chewing the corn to introduce enzymes that would start fermentation — a method that colonizers banned (and stigmatized) for centuries.

Today's chicha skips the chewing, but the drink retains its earthy, tangy funk. It's served cold, thick as a smoothie, with a mild alcoholic kick that sneaks up on you. The flavor sits somewhere between sourdough starter and sweet corn, with undertones of the clay vessels it's sometimes stored in. Not for everyone, but unmistakably Colombian.

Find it in La Candelaria at the dedicated chicherías, or at Paloquemao Market where vendors sell it alongside fresh fruit. A glass costs $1–$2. Drink slowly — the altitude, remember.

#### Chocolate Completo

Not quite a dish, more like an institution. A cup of thick hot chocolate, made by dissolving tablets of Colombian cacao into hot milk and frothing it with a wooden molinillo until it foams. Alongside: fresh white cheese (again, for dipping), a tamal or almojábana (cheese bread), and pan de bono (chewy yuca rolls). This is breakfast or afternoon once, the meal that punctuates Colombian days.

The chocolate is unsweetened or lightly sweetened, intensely cocoa-forward, and the cheese adds protein and salt to balance the richness. At traditional spots like La Florida in La Candelaria, the setup runs $4–$5 and could fuel you for half the day.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Bogotá runs on different rhythms than other capitals. Breakfast matters here — arrive early at traditional spots, by 7 or 8 AM, when the tamales are freshest and the ajiaco is just reaching perfection. Lunch (almuerzo) is the big meal, served between noon and 2 PM at set-menu restaurants offering soup, main, juice, and dessert for $3–$5. Dinner is lighter, often just a chocolate completo.

Sharing is implicit. Fritanga arrives expecting multiple forks. Tamales get split between friends. And when someone offers you tinto (tiny black coffee) or aguapanela, saying no is technically allowed but will earn you looks.

One final note: altitude affects everything. Food digests slower. Alcohol hits harder. Portions that seem reasonable at sea level will defeat you here. Pace yourself, drink water, and leave room — because there's always another bowl of ajiaco waiting.
