# Rome Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Rome

Three days in Rome, eating with purpose. Not the Rome of rushed museum cafeterias and overpriced pasta near the Colosseum, but the Rome where nonnas still roll pasta by hand, where market vendors slice porchetta onto crusty bread at 10 AM, and where a plate of carbonara can be transcendent or tragic depending on which door you walk through. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I ate my way across this city.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Romans eat late — lunch from 1 PM, dinner rarely before 8 PM. Restaurants that open at 6 PM for dinner are catering to tourists, not locals. Tipping isn't expected (service is included), but rounding up a few euros is appreciated. Cash is still king at markets and older trattorias, though cards work at most sit-down restaurants. For any dinner worth having on a weekend, book ahead — even a day or two makes a difference. And please, for the love of all things Roman: no cappuccino after 11 AM.

## Day 1: Testaccio — Where Romans Actually Eat

Testaccio is Rome's working-class food district, built around the old slaughterhouse. This is where Roman cuisine was invented by butchers who took home the "quinto quarto" — the fifth quarter of offal and scraps — and turned poverty into poetry. Start here, eat here, understand here.

### Morning

Begin at Testaccio Market (Mercato di Testaccio), a covered market where Romans shop for dinner and visitors can eat an exceptional breakfast. Find a stall selling fresh-fried supplì — those golden rice balls with a heart of stretchy mozzarella that Romans eat standing up, still hot from the oil. The good ones crack when you bite through, then give way to creamy rice and that cheese pull. Pair it with a cornetto (Italian croissant) and espresso from the market's bar.

**Cost**: €5–8 for supplì, pastry, and coffee.

### Afternoon

Lunch is non-negotiable: you need proper carbonara, and you need it in Testaccio. This is the dish Rome fights about — how many eggs, which pasta shape, guanciale versus pancetta (always guanciale), pecorino versus parmesan (always pecorino). The good versions are silky, not scrambled, with enough black pepper to bite back. Look for trattorias a few blocks from the main market where construction workers eat alongside tourists who've done their research. The pasta should cost around €12–15, and it should be the richest thing you eat all week.

After lunch, walk off the carbs by exploring the neighborhood's former slaughterhouse complex, now converted into a contemporary art museum and cultural center. The contrast between industrial bones and Roman cooking traditions tells the whole story of this neighborhood.

**Cost**: €20–30 for a primo (pasta course), water, and coffee.

### Evening

Dinner in Testaccio means going deeper into Roman classics. This is the night for cacio e pepe — just pasta, pecorino cheese, and black pepper, which sounds simple until you've had a bad one (grainy, clumped, lifeless). The good ones are glossy, coating each strand of tonnarelli pasta in a cream that exists without cream. Order an antipasto of carciofi alla giudia (Jewish-style fried artichokes, crispy as chips) if you spot them on the menu — Testaccio borders the old Jewish Ghetto, and the influence shows.

A proper sit-down dinner with wine, antipasto, primo, and maybe a secondo runs €45–70 at mid-range Testaccio trattorias, less if you stick to one course and house wine.

**Cost**: €45–70 for a full dinner with wine.

### Day 1 Total
**Budget**: ~€70–100 · **Mid-range**: ~€100–150 · **Splurge**: ~€200+ (add second courses, better wine, digestivi)

## Day 2: Trastevere & Centro Storico — Cobblestones and Late Nights

Cross the Tiber into Trastevere, the neighborhood of cobblestone lanes, ivy-covered facades, and more trattorias per square meter than anywhere else in Rome. Yes, it's touristy. Yes, there are traps. But the good places are very good, and the atmosphere after dark is unmatched.

### Morning

Romans don't do elaborate breakfasts — coffee and something sweet, consumed standing at a bar. Do as they do. Find a corner bar in Trastevere, order an espresso and a maritozzo (a brioche bun split and stuffed with barely sweetened whipped cream), and watch the neighborhood wake up. This is a breakfast that costs €3–4 and takes five minutes, which leaves you time to wander before the crowds arrive.

If you want something more substantial, some bakeries in Trastevere sell pizza bianca — flatbread brushed with olive oil and salt — that you can have stuffed with mortadella or eaten plain. It's €3–5 and absurdly satisfying.

**Cost**: €3–8.

### Afternoon

Walk across the river into Centro Storico for lunch near the Pantheon. This area is dense with tourist traps, so the two-block rule applies: walk two blocks from any major monument before sitting down to eat. You're looking for pizza al taglio — Roman-style pizza sold by weight from rectangular trays. Point at what you want, indicate how much with your hands, and they'll cut it, weigh it, and charge you accordingly. The crust should be airy and crisp, not bready. Try the potato and rosemary, or whatever seasonal topping looks best that day. A few squares and a supplì make a perfect lunch for €8–12.

After lunch, get lost in the medieval streets between the Pantheon and Piazza Navona. Stop for gelato — real gelato, from a shop where the colors are muted (bright green pistachio is a red flag), where you can't see the gelato mounded in peaks (good shops cover it), and where the ingredient list is short. Pistachio and stracciatella are the litmus tests. €4–6 for two flavors in a cup.

**Cost**: €12–20 for pizza and gelato.

### Evening

Return to Trastevere as the sun drops and the piazzas fill. Dinner here should feel Roman but relaxed — maybe saltimbocca, veal wrapped with prosciutto and sage in white wine sauce, or if you're feeling adventurous, trippa alla romana (tripe in tomato sauce). The cobblestone squares come alive after 9 PM, and you can linger over wine without feeling rushed.

Consider ending the night at a wine bar in nearby Monti, the hip neighborhood of vintage shops and intimate enotecas. A few glasses of natural wine and some small plates extend the evening without the commitment of another full meal.

**Cost**: €50–80 for dinner; add €20–30 for wine bar.

### Day 2 Total
**Budget**: ~€65–110 · **Mid-range**: ~€130–180 · **Splurge**: ~€250+ (add better restaurant, wine pairings)

## Day 3: Monti & The Farewell Feast

Your last day should feel unhurried. Monti, the neighborhood of wine bars and craft cocktail lounges, offers a slower morning pace before you build toward one final Roman meal.

### Morning

Monti wakes up late, which suits your third day. Find a café with outdoor seating and order what might be your last Roman espresso — make it count. Pair it with a pasticciotto (custard-filled pastry) or return to the maritozzo if you've fallen for it.

If it's Sunday, detour to Porta Portese, Rome's sprawling flea market, which runs along the river in Trastevere. It's not about food, but the energy is pure Rome, and the vendors selling household goods and vintage clothes have likely been coming here for decades.

**Cost**: €4–8.

### Afternoon

For your final lunch, return to what Rome does best: pasta. But this time, try what you haven't yet. If you had carbonara on Day 1 and cacio e pepe on Day 2, go for amatriciana (guanciale, tomato, pecorino, a hint of chili) or gricia (essentially carbonara without egg — guanciale and pecorino in perfect balance). These are the four pastas of Rome, and leaving without trying at least two would be a mistake.

Eat in Monti, at one of the trattorias tucked behind the main streets, where the lunch crowd is locals on break and tourists who've figured things out. A proper plate of gricia, water, and a glass of house red shouldn't cost more than €18–25.

After lunch, walk to the Aventine Hill and peer through the Aventine Keyhole — the Knights of Malta door that frames St. Peter's dome perfectly in the distance. It's not food, but it's magic, and you've earned it.

**Cost**: €18–30.

### Evening

Your final dinner should be memorable. This is the night to book ahead, dress slightly better than you have been, and commit to a multi-course meal. Start with fiori di zucca (fried zucchini flowers stuffed with mozzarella and anchovy), move through a primo you haven't tried yet, add a secondo if you dare, and finish with something sweet — maybe a slice of torta ricotta or a simple panna cotta.

Eat in Testaccio if you want to end where Rome feels most authentic, or splurge on a reservation at a restaurant near the Pantheon that's earned its reputation. Budget €70–100 for a proper farewell dinner; more if you add wine pairings or choose somewhere ambitious.

Walk back through the empty streets after midnight, when the Trevi Fountain is illuminated and crowd-free. This is the Rome that will pull you back.

**Cost**: €70–120 for dinner; €100–150+ for a splurge.

### Day 3 Total
**Budget**: ~€90–160 · **Mid-range**: ~€150–220 · **Splurge**: ~€300+

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The coperto is not a scam.** Most Roman restaurants charge a €1–3 per-person cover charge for bread and table service. It's customary, not a trick.
- **Lunch is the value meal.** Many restaurants offer smaller prices at lunch, or you can eat pizza al taglio and supplì for €10 and save your euros for dinner.
- **Water from the nasoni is free and good.** Rome's little public drinking fountains run cold, clean water — refill your bottle constantly.
- **If a waiter aggressively invites you in from the sidewalk, keep walking.** Good restaurants in Rome don't need to hustle. The ones that do are usually the ones to avoid.
