# Bali Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Bali

Three days. One island. An unreasonable number of meals. This is the itinerary I built plate by plate across Bali — from predawn markets where smoke curls off spit-roasted pig to candlelit dinners overlooking rice terraces. I ate too much, regretted nothing, and now I'm handing you the map.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Balinese lunch is the main event — most warungs (local eateries) prep fresh food by 11am and run out by 2pm. Dinner is often lighter or a second lunch reheated. Cash rules at markets and small warungs; bring plenty of small bills in Rupiah. Tipping isn't expected at local spots but appreciated at restaurants (5-10%). Use your right hand when passing money or receiving food. And please — drink only bottled or filtered water, even for brushing teeth.

## Day 1: Ubud — The Cultural Heart

**Theme: Traditional Balinese cooking in the island's spiritual center**

Ubud is where you learn what Balinese food actually tastes like before tourism smoothed its edges. Start here to calibrate your palate.

### Morning: Nasi Campur for Breakfast

Skip the hotel buffet. By 8am, walk to the warungs along Jalan Goutama or near Ubud Market where locals are already seated over steaming plates of **nasi campur** — the Balinese mixed rice plate that functions as breakfast, lunch, and sometimes dinner. You'll point at what you want from glass cases: shredded chicken in coconut, long beans in sambal, fried tempeh, a boiled egg, maybe some lawar if you're lucky. A woman will arrange it all over rice with practiced speed.

The rice is the baseline; the sides are where cooks show off. Look for versions with bebek (duck) or ayam betutu (slow-cooked spiced chicken). Eat with your right hand if you want — that's how it's done.

**Where**: Warungs near Ubud Traditional Art Market, Ubud  
**Cost**: Rp 25,000–45,000 ($1.60–$3)

### Afternoon: The Babi Guling Pilgrimage

Lunch is non-negotiable: **babi guling**, the spit-roasted suckling pig that defines Balinese ceremonial cooking. The best versions have skin so crisp it shatters, meat that falls apart, and a stuffing of cassava leaves, lemongrass, and enough chilies to make you sweat through your shirt.

The famous spots in Ubud open around 11am and genuinely sell out by 1pm — this isn't marketing, it's math. Arrive by 11:30. You'll get a plate loaded with sliced pork, crispy skin, rice, lawar, and blood sausage (urutan) if they have it. The whole table shares sambal matah, that raw shallot-and-lemongrass condiment that cuts through the fat.

After lunch, walk off the pig through the **Sacred Monkey Forest** or wander the rice paddies north of town. Keep your belongings close — the monkeys are aggressive thieves.

**Where**: Warungs along Jalan Tegal Sari or Jalan Suweta, Ubud  
**Cost**: Rp 50,000–80,000 ($3.20–$5.20)

### Evening: Fine Dining Among Rice Terraces

Tonight, you splurge. Ubud has Indonesia's most ambitious restaurant scene, with chefs doing things with local ingredients that would make sense in Copenhagen or Tokyo. Book ahead for a multi-course tasting menu that might include smoked duck with young jackfruit, raw tuna with Balinese spices, and desserts built around palm sugar and coconut.

The setting matters as much as the food — terraced views, candlelight, the sound of frogs and gamelan in the distance. This is where Bali's traditional ingredients get the fine-dining treatment they deserve.

**Where**: Restaurants along Jalan Raya Sanggingan or overlooking Sayan Ridge, Ubud  
**Cost**: Rp 600,000–1,200,000 ($39–$78) for tasting menu with drinks

### Day 1 Total Food Spend
- **Budget**: ~Rp 275,000 (~$18)
- **Mid-range**: ~Rp 450,000 (~$29)
- **Splurge**: ~Rp 1,300,000 (~$85)

## Day 2: Seminyak & Canggu — Beach Towns with Range

**Theme: Modern Bali meets market eating along the coast**

Head southwest to where Bali gets more international but keeps its Indonesian backbone. The key is knowing where to dodge the overpriced smoothie bowls and find actual food.

### Morning: Nasi Goreng Done Right

**Nasi goreng** — Indonesia's national fried rice — sounds simple until you taste a great version. The wok should be screaming hot, the kecap manis (sweet soy) caramelized but not burnt, the egg with crispy edges. A proper nasi goreng comes with kerupuk (crackers), pickled vegetables, and more sambal than you think you need.

In Canggu, find the warungs tucked behind the surf shops on side streets off Jalan Batu Bolong. These are where the long-term expats and local workers eat before the yoga crowd wakes up. Get there by 8am and you'll eat elbow-to-elbow with people who know exactly what's good.

**Where**: Local warungs off Jalan Batu Bolong, Canggu  
**Cost**: Rp 30,000–50,000 ($2–$3.25)

### Afternoon: Sate Lilit and a Cooking Class

Midday, take a Balinese cooking class — the kind that starts at a morning market, involves too much coconut grating, and ends with you eating everything you've made. You'll learn to make **sate lilit**, those minced fish satays wrapped around lemongrass sticks, along with lawar, sambal, and ceremonial rice dishes.

The market visit alone is worth it: pyramids of spices, women selling perfect morning-glory greens, the chaos of everyone buying ingredients for family ceremonies. The cooking happens in open-air kitchens, often overlooking rice paddies, and the teachers actually care whether you understand the "why" behind each step.

If you skip the class, hunt down sate lilit at a warung that grills them fresh — the lemongrass should still be fragrant, the fish (usually mackerel or tuna) spiced with galangal and kaffir lime.

**Where**: Cooking schools with pickup from Seminyak/Canggu (often based near Ubud)  
**Cost**: Rp 450,000–700,000 ($29–$45) for half-day class with market tour and meal

### Evening: Sunset Drinks to Serious Dinner

Start at a beach bar in Seminyak — the upscale beach clubs do sunset well, with DJs, decent cocktails, and the kind of golden-hour light that makes everyone look better. Don't eat here; the food is overpriced and under-seasoned.

Instead, walk to one of Seminyak's serious restaurants for dinner. This neighborhood has the island's most diverse dining: Korean barbecue, wood-fired Italian, modern Indonesian. Lean Indonesian — order a rijsttafel-style spread of small plates to share: rendang, grilled prawns in sambal, vegetables in coconut milk, multiple sambals for dipping.

**Where**: Restaurants along Jalan Kayu Aya (Eat Street), Seminyak  
**Cost**: Rp 400,000–800,000 ($26–$52) for drinks and dinner

### Day 2 Total Food Spend
- **Budget**: ~Rp 180,000 (~$12) — skip the class, eat street food
- **Mid-range**: ~Rp 700,000 (~$45) — cooking class plus casual dinner
- **Splurge**: ~Rp 1,500,000 (~$98) — class, sunset drinks, upscale dinner

## Day 3: Sidemen & Back — The Quiet Side and Farewell Feast

**Theme: East Bali's tranquil rice terraces and one last perfect meal**

Leave the crowded south. Drive 1.5 hours east to **Sidemen Valley**, where the rice terraces look like Tegallalang without the Instagram crowds, and Mount Agung looms in the background. This is where food feels most connected to the land.

### Morning: Village Breakfast and Lawar

Arrange through your hotel or driver to eat breakfast at a small warung in Sidemen. The menu will be limited — whatever they made that morning. If you're lucky, there's **lawar**, the minced meat salad with grated coconut, green beans, and spices that's as close to essential as Balinese food gets. Traditional lawar includes raw blood mixed in; tourist versions often skip this. Both are worth trying.

The coffee here is strong, sweet, and served with grounds in the cup — let them settle before drinking. Sit on a terrace, look at the volcano, and accept that this beats any hotel breakfast.

**Where**: Warungs in Sidemen village, Sidemen Valley  
**Cost**: Rp 35,000–60,000 ($2.30–$4)

### Afternoon: Pisang Goreng and the Return

On the drive back toward your final night's base (Seminyak or Ubud), stop wherever you see a roadside stall frying **pisang goreng** — those battered banana fritters that are Bali's answer to the doughnut. The best are made with short, sweet pisang raja bananas, fried to order in coconut oil, and eaten still crackling. Some stalls dip them in palm sugar syrup or serve with grated cheese (surprisingly good). Pay almost nothing, burn your mouth, repeat.

Stop also at any market you pass — Sukawati is on the route and has a food section behind the famous craft market. Walk through, sample what's offered, pick up a bag of krupuk for later.

**Where**: Roadside stalls on the drive from Sidemen; Sukawati Market  
**Cost**: Rp 10,000–25,000 ($0.65–$1.60) for snacks

### Evening: The Final Feast

Your last dinner should be definitive. Return to wherever you've based yourself and book the best table you can manage. In Ubud, this might mean a tasting menu that interprets Balinese flavors through modern technique. In Seminyak, perhaps a seafood-focused meal where the fish was swimming that morning.

Order without restraint: the sambal that scares you, the dessert you don't quite understand, the rice wine or arak cocktail. End with something coconut-sweet — es campur, black rice pudding, whatever signals closure.

**Where**: Your top-tier pick in Ubud (Sayan Ridge restaurants) or Seminyak (Jalan Kayu Aya area)  
**Cost**: Rp 700,000–1,500,000 ($45–$98) for the full experience

### Day 3 Total Food Spend
- **Budget**: ~Rp 145,000 (~$9)
- **Mid-range**: ~Rp 500,000 (~$32)
- **Splurge**: ~Rp 1,600,000 (~$104)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Eat early or miss out**: The best warungs cook once in the morning. By 2pm, popular dishes are gone and you're eating yesterday's rendang reheated. Adjust your schedule — lunch at 11:30am, dinner at 6pm.

- **Carry small bills always**: Breaking a Rp 100,000 note at a warung is awkward for everyone. Exchange large bills at convenience stores before market visits.

- **Ask for "pedas" at your own risk**: When a Balinese cook asks about spice level and you say pedas (spicy), they will deliver. Start with "sedang" (medium) and escalate from there.

- **Hire a driver who eats well**: If you book a private driver for day trips, ask where they eat. The best food intelligence in Bali comes from people who drive tourists to restaurants but eat at the warung next door.
