The first bite of carbonara I had in Rome made me close my eyes. Not because I was savoring it — though I was — but because I needed a moment to process what had just happened. The pasta was impossibly silky, coated in something that wasn't quite sauce but wasn't quite anything else either. It was 2 PM in Testaccio, the light was golden through the trattoria's window, and I understood, finally, why people make pilgrimages for pasta.

Rome doesn't just feed you. It rewires your understanding of what food can be.

---

#### Carbonara

Let's start where I started. Carbonara is Rome's most famous pasta, and also its most bastardized — which is why eating the real thing here feels like a revelation. Forget the cream-laden versions you've had elsewhere. Roman carbonara is guanciale (cured pork jowl, not bacon), egg yolks, Pecorino Romano, and black pepper. That's it. The "sauce" is created when hot pasta meets raw egg yolk and cheese, emulsifying into something luxuriously rich but never heavy.

The texture is what gets you first — each strand of rigatoni or spaghetti wrapped in a glossy, golden coat that clings without drowning. Then the guanciale hits: crispy at the edges, fatty in the middle, with that particular funk of aged pork that bacon can never replicate. The pepper isn't shy here; it bites back.

Carbonara was likely invented post-WWII, when American soldiers brought powdered eggs and bacon to Rome and local cooks improvised something magnificent from military rations. Today it's the dish that Romans judge outsiders by. Order it at any old trattoria in Testaccio — the neighborhood built around Rome's old slaughterhouse, where working-class cooks perfected the "fifth quarter" cuts — and expect to pay €12–15 for a plate that will haunt your dreams.

#### Cacio e Pepe

If carbonara is Rome's showpiece, cacio e pepe is its soul. Three ingredients: pasta, Pecorino Romano, black pepper. No hiding. No forgiveness. The simplicity is the challenge — achieving that creamy, clinging sauce from just cheese and starchy pasta water requires technique that looks easy and isn't.

When it works, cacio e pepe tastes like the idea of comfort distilled into a bowl. The pecorino is sharp and sheepy, the pepper is coarsely cracked and assertive, and somehow together they create something nutty, warming, almost addictive. The pasta — traditionally tonnarelli, a thick square-cut spaghetti — should have tooth. You want resistance before surrender.

This dish is ancient, likely carried by Roman shepherds who could pack cheese and pepper for journeys across the Apennines. It's peasant food elevated to art. Eat it at a tiny spot in Monti around 8 PM, when the tables spill onto the cobblestones and the kitchen is hitting its rhythm. Budget €10–14.

#### Supplì

Before you sit down for pasta, you stand up for supplì. These fried rice balls are Rome's answer to the question nobody asked: what if risotto were portable, molten, and addictive?

Picture this: you're at a pizzeria al taglio, and behind the glass there's a row of golden, oval-shaped fritters. You ask for one. It arrives in a paper napkin, too hot to hold but too tempting to wait. You bite through a shattering crust into creamy, tomato-laced rice, and then — the mozzarella. It pulls in strings as you separate the halves, which is why Romans call these *supplì al telefono*. The cheese stretches like a telephone cord.

The rice inside is typically made with ragù, giving each bite a subtle meatiness beneath the tomato sweetness. The outside is breadcrumb-crunchy, the inside is just-set enough to hold together. Supplì exist for that golden hour between sightseeing and dinner, or for breakfast if you're being honest with yourself. Find them at pizza al taglio shops across the city. €2–3 each. Buy two.

#### Pizza al Taglio

Speaking of those pizza shops — Roman pizza is not Neapolitan pizza, and the sooner you understand that, the happier you'll be. Pizza al taglio means "pizza by the cut." It comes in rectangular trays, you point at what you want, they cut it with scissors, weigh it, and quote you a price. This is lunchtime pizza. Walking-around pizza. Late-night pizza. It's possibly the most democratic food in Rome.

The crust is the thing. Roman pizza al taglio has an airy, focaccia-like base — crisp on the bottom, pillowy in the middle, with those beautiful air pockets that come from long fermentation. Toppings range from classic (margherita, potato and rosemary) to seasonal (zucchini flowers, fresh figs with prosciutto). The potato pizza sounds wrong until you try it: thin-sliced potatoes, good olive oil, rosemary, just enough salt. It's carbs on carbs and it's perfect.

The best shops are in Trastevere and Prati. Go mid-afternoon when the trays are freshest. A slice that fills your hand costs €3–5 depending on weight and toppings.

#### Gelato

You will eat gelato in Rome. The question is whether you'll eat good gelato or corn-syrup-laden tourist gelato. Here's the cheat code: if the pistachio is bright green, walk away. Real pistachio gelato is brownish-green, the color of actual pistachios. If the mounds are piled high in dramatic peaks, that's stabilizers at work. Quality gelato sits flat in covered tins.

When you find the right place — and you will, because Rome has dozens of artisan gelaterias — the experience is transformative. Stracciatella that's just sweet cream with threads of dark chocolate cracking between your teeth. Fig gelato in September that tastes like sunshine in a cup. Hazelnut so intensely nutty it ruins you for Nutella forever. The texture is denser than American ice cream, less airy, more intense.

Romans eat gelato at any time of day, but the after-dinner passeggiata (evening stroll) is traditional. Get a small cup — the portions are rich — for €3–4. Walk. Taste. Feel the city cool down around you.

#### Saltimbocca

The name means "jumps in the mouth," and it's not an exaggeration. Saltimbocca alla Romana is a thin veal cutlet draped with prosciutto crudo and a fresh sage leaf, pan-fried until the pork fat renders slightly, then finished with a splash of white wine that deglazes into a glossy sauce.

What happens is alchemy. The veal stays tender, the prosciutto goes slightly crisp at the edges, the sage perfumes everything without dominating, and that wine sauce — butter-enriched, pan-fond-flavored — ties it together into something greater than its parts. It's simple in the way that only Italian food can be simple: a few perfect ingredients, treated correctly, yielding something that feels complete.

This is a dish for sit-down meals in traditional trattorias. It requires a table, a glass of white wine, and time. You'll find it on menus across Rome, often served with sautéed spinach or roasted potatoes. Expect €16–22. Order it when you want to feel taken care of.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Romans eat late. Lunch happens between 1 and 3 PM; dinner rarely starts before 8:30 PM. Restaurants that fill up at 7 PM are feeding tourists. The places worth finding are still empty at 7:30.

Don't order cappuccino after 11 AM — it marks you as an outsider and, frankly, confuses the barista. Espresso is all-day. Cappuccino is breakfast. This matters more than it should.

Pasta is a *primo* course, not a main. If you're hungry, order an antipasto, then pasta, then maybe a secondo like saltimbocca. Or do what I did most nights: skip the secondo and order two different pastas. Nobody will judge. They might even respect it.

The check won't come until you ask for it. *"Il conto, per favore."* Romans don't rush you out. Linger. Order another wine. Watch the street life through the window. This is eating as an event, not a task.
