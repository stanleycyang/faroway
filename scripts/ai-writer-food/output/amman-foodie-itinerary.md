# Amman Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Amman

Three days. One city. An unreasonable number of meals. Amman doesn't announce itself as a food destination the way Beirut or Istanbul do, but that's precisely why it rewards the obsessive eater. The best hummus in the city costs less than a dollar, the national dish requires you to eat with your hands from a communal platter, and breakfast here means fried chickpea balls so hot they burn your fingertips. This is your eating schedule.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Jordanians eat late — lunch rarely happens before 2pm, dinner often starts at 9pm. Cash remains king at street stalls and old-school restaurants; cards work in upscale spots. Tip 10% at sit-down restaurants, round up for street food. Use your right hand when eating from shared plates. If someone offers you coffee, accept the first cup — declining the second is how you politely signal you're finished. Friday is quiet; many places close until evening prayer ends.

## Day 1: Downtown Fundamentals

**The premise:** You cannot understand Amman's food without starting in Al-Balad, the old downtown. This is where the falafel is freshest, the hummus has been made the same way for fifty years, and nobody cares about your Instagram.

### Morning: The Falafel Breakfast

Start on Al Quds Street before 9am, when the falafel shops have been frying for an hour and the oil is at its cleanest. This is not Rainbow Street tourist territory — this is where Ammani workers grab breakfast before heading to offices. Order falafel sandwiches: four or five hot, crisp balls stuffed into fresh khubz bread with pickled turnips, tomatoes, and tahini. The falafel should crack when you bite through it, the inside bright green and herbed. Eat standing at the counter or perched on a plastic stool on the sidewalk. Wash it down with fresh-squeezed orange juice from the cart next door.

**Cost:** 0.75–1.50 JD ($1–2) for a sandwich that genuinely fills you up.

### Afternoon: Hummus and the Spice Souks

By 1pm, work your way deeper into downtown toward the hummus restaurants clustered near the Roman Theatre. Forget the notion that hummus is a dip or a side dish — here, it's a meal. A proper plate arrives drowning in green olive oil with a scattering of whole chickpeas and toasted pine nuts on top. You scoop it with warm bread, methodically working from the edges toward the center. The texture should be impossibly smooth, the tahini proportion high, the lemon sharp enough to cut through the richness.

After lunch, walk off the heaviness through Nuzha Market. Tourists rarely find it, which means the spice vendors aren't performing for you — they're actually selling sumac and za'atar to people who cook with it daily. Buy dried rose petals, whole cardamom pods, and a bag of mixed nuts roasted that morning. This is also where you'll find the best Arabic sweets by weight: baklava dense with pistachios, ma'amoul cookies stuffed with dates.

**Cost:** Hummus lunch 3–5 JD ($4–7), market snacking and supplies 5–8 JD ($7–11).

### Evening: The Mansaf Initiation

Your first night in Amman must include mansaf, the Jordanian national dish. Head to a traditional restaurant in Jabal Amman — the old-money neighborhood of the 1st to 3rd Circle zone. Mansaf is not complicated: lamb slow-cooked in jameed (dried fermented yogurt), served over a mountain of rice and paper-thin flatbread, the whole thing finished with toasted almonds and pine nuts. It arrives in a communal platter. You eat with your right hand, forming balls of rice and meat, using the bread to scoop. The jameed is tangy and rich, the lamb falling apart. This is hospitality food — the dish Jordanians serve at weddings, funerals, and any occasion worth gathering for.

**Cost:** 12–20 JD ($17–28) per person depending on restaurant tier.

**Day 1 total spend:** Budget ~35 JD ($50) · Mid-range ~45 JD ($65) · Splurge ~75 JD ($105)

## Day 2: The Artisan Quarter and Evening Exploration

**The premise:** Move west into Weibdeh, the bohemian neighborhood of artists, independent coffee roasters, and quiet residential streets. The food here is more personal — small-batch producers, chefs with creative ambitions, the kind of places where someone's grandmother is still making the bread.

### Morning: Coffee and Manakeesh

Start at a local coffee roaster in Weibdeh. Jordanian coffee culture has two tracks: the traditional stuff (bitter, cardamom-heavy, served in thimble-sized cups) and the newer specialty scene. Try both. The traditional cup is a ritual — sip slowly, leave the grounds at the bottom, accept the offer of a refill and then gently decline the third. For breakfast, find manakeesh: flatbread baked with za'atar (thyme, sumac, sesame) and olive oil, or topped with akkawi cheese that pulls into strings when you tear it. This is the Levantine answer to pizza, eaten folded in half while still hot.

**Cost:** Coffee and manakeesh 4–7 JD ($6–10).

### Afternoon: Cooking Class and Rainbow Street

Spend midday learning to make Arabic mezze — a cooking class in Jabal Amman teaches you the proportions that make hummus sing, the technique for rolling grape leaves tight enough to hold together during cooking, the exact moment to pull moutabbal (smoky eggplant dip) from the flames. You'll eat what you make, which solves the lunch question. These classes run 3–4 hours and often include a market visit at the start, where you'll shop for ingredients with someone who actually knows the vendors.

In the late afternoon, walk down Rainbow Street for a light snack and the café scene. This is more polished than downtown — boutique shops, art galleries, tourists taking photos at sunset. Grab fresh juice or a small plate of kibbeh (fried bulgur shells stuffed with spiced lamb) to tide you over until dinner.

**Cost:** Cooking class 40–65 JD ($55–90), afternoon snacking 5–8 JD ($7–11).

### Evening: Maklouba in Local Company

Tonight, seek out maklouba — the "upside-down" rice dish that requires a dramatic presentation. A pot of rice, chicken, and fried vegetables (cauliflower, eggplant, potatoes) is cooked together, then inverted onto a platter so the crispy bottom becomes the top. When done well, the rice is golden and slightly crusted, the chicken impossibly tender from steaming. Find a neighborhood restaurant in Weibdeh where families are eating, not tourists. Order sides of fattoush (bread salad with sumac dressing) and fresh tabbouleh. Finish with Arabic coffee and a plate of baklava.

**Cost:** 8–15 JD ($11–21) per person.

**Day 2 total spend:** Budget ~55 JD ($75) · Mid-range ~85 JD ($120) · Splurge ~120 JD ($170)

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

**The premise:** Your final day is about eating the best versions of everything you haven't yet tried, hitting the Friday market if timing allows, and ending with the single most memorable meal of the trip.

### Morning: The Full Jordanian Breakfast

Go back to basics. Find a traditional breakfast spread at a restaurant in Jabal Amman: fresh labneh (strained yogurt) drizzled with olive oil, za'atar in a small bowl for dipping bread, fried eggs cooked in olive oil, sliced tomatoes and cucumbers, olives, white cheese, and endless rounds of hot bread. This is how Jordanians eat on their day off — slowly, with family, refilling tea glasses until the pot runs dry. If it's Friday, the streets will be quiet; use this meal as your anchor.

**Cost:** 6–12 JD ($8–17).

### Afternoon: Souk Jara and Sweet Things

If you're here on a Friday, Souk Jara in Jabal Amman is essential. This artisan market fills a hillside street with food stalls, craft vendors, and local designers. Graze through the market: fresh saj bread being made on domed griddles, small plates of fatteh (chickpeas with yogurt and crispy bread), cups of jallab (grape molasses drink with rose water and pine nuts). This is snacking as exploration — take small portions of everything.

Save room for knafeh. This Nablus-origin dessert has become a Jordanian obsession: shredded phyllo dough layered with stretchy white cheese, baked until golden, then drenched in rosewater syrup and scattered with crushed pistachios. The best versions balance sweetness with the slight saltiness of the cheese, and the top should shatter when you press your fork through it. Find a specialist — this is not a dish to compromise on.

**Cost:** Market grazing 8–12 JD ($11–17), knafeh 3–5 JD ($4–7).

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your final dinner should be the one you remember. If you haven't yet had mansaf at its finest, tonight is the night — find a restaurant known specifically for this dish, where the lamb has been cooking since morning and the jameed is made in-house. Alternatively, seek out a high-end Levantine restaurant in Abdoun that does modern interpretations of traditional dishes: lamb cooked with dried yogurt but presented differently, mezze elevated with better ingredients and more careful technique. This is the splurge meal, the one where you order the good wine (Jordan has a small but respectable wine industry) and let the evening stretch.

End with a walk and a final Arabic coffee somewhere with a view — perhaps a rooftop in Jabal Amman looking out over the city's hills, the call to prayer echoing between buildings as the lights come on.

**Cost:** 25–55 JD ($35–77) per person for a memorable final dinner.

**Day 3 total spend:** Budget ~40 JD ($55) · Mid-range ~60 JD ($85) · Splurge ~95 JD ($135)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Falafel is a morning food.** The oil is freshest, the batches are constant, and the texture is better. Afternoon falafel is a compromise.
- **Accept the coffee ritual.** When a shop owner offers you coffee, take it. Sip slowly. This is how relationships work here, and it will often lead to better recommendations than any guidebook.
- **Friday changes everything.** Many restaurants close until evening; street food becomes harder to find. Plan a big breakfast, pack snacks, and adjust expectations.
- **Download the Careem app before you arrive.** Taxis are your main transport between neighborhoods, and having the app means no haggling, no wrong turns, and accurate pricing.
