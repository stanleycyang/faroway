Three days in Tbilisi taught me that Georgians don't eat to live—they live to eat, drink, and toast until someone starts singing. This is a city where breakfast might be a cheese-filled bread boat with a raw egg sliding into molten sulguni, lunch is twenty dumplings eaten standing at a counter, and dinner stretches past midnight with a toastmaster orchestrating wine and emotion in equal measure. Here's the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I arrived hungry.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Georgians eat late—lunch rarely before 1 PM, dinner often at 9 PM or later. Tipping is 10% at sit-down restaurants, nothing at counters or markets. Many neighborhood spots are cash-only, so carry lari. Reservations matter only at upscale places on weekends; everywhere else, just walk in. At a traditional supra feast, the tamada leads all toasts—follow their rhythm, and know that covering your wine glass is perfectly acceptable when you've had enough. Nobody will judge you, but they will judge you for refusing food entirely.

## Day 1: Old Town & the Sulfur Bath District

### Morning — Abanotubani

Start where Tbilisi started: the domed sulfur bath district tucked beneath Narikala Fortress, where brick chimneys release steam into the morning air. Before the city wakes fully, find a corner bakery and order **lobiani**—flatbread stuffed with spiced kidney beans, baked until the crust crackles and the filling goes creamy. This is Georgian breakfast: carbs, fat, salt, eaten standing or walking. Pair it with a glass of matsoni (Georgian yogurt) if you spot it, or strong Turkish-style coffee.

The neighborhood bakeries here open by 7 AM, and a lobiani costs around ₾3–4 ($1.20–$1.60). Eat it overlooking the sulfur bath domes if you can find a perch.

**Cost: $2–$4**

### Afternoon — Abanotubani to Dry Bridge Market

By noon, walk north from the baths toward the Dry Bridge Market, the daily flea market sprawling with Soviet cameras, antique daggers, and grandmothers selling homemade **churchkhela**—walnuts threaded on a string and dipped repeatedly in thickened grape juice until they form a chewy, sweet, deeply purple candy. Buy one from whoever looks like they've been making them longest. This is your walking snack, the Georgian energy bar that sustained armies.

For lunch, find a khinkali house—there are several near the market's edges. **Khinkali** are Georgia's sacred dumplings: twisted pleats of dough encasing spiced pork and beef swimming in hot broth. You eat them by hand, biting a hole in the side, drinking the soup, then devouring the rest. The twisted knob on top is a handle, not food—leave it on your plate so everyone can count how many you've conquered. Order at least five; ten is respectable. At a no-frills counter spot, you'll pay ₾1–1.50 per dumpling.

**Cost: $6–$10 for lunch and snacks**

### Evening — Vera

Take a marshrutka or walk uphill to Vera, the tree-lined residential neighborhood where old Tbilisi families live behind vine-covered balconies. This is where you want your first proper Georgian dinner, at a restaurant that feels like someone's grandmother might emerge from the kitchen. Order **badrijani nigvzit**—fried eggplant rolls stuffed with a paste of ground walnuts, garlic, and fenugreek—as a starter. They'll come fanned on a plate, each one a small flavor bomb of smoke and nuttiness.

For your main, get the **khachapuri Adjarian**, the cheese-bread boat with an egg cracked into molten cheese and a knob of butter melting into the crater. Mix it tableside with your fork, tear the bread edges, and scoop. This is the dish that made Tbilisi famous, and in Vera you'll get a version made without tourist shortcuts.

Drink amber wine from Kakheti—Georgia invented wine 8,000 years ago, and the orange-hued qvevri wines are unlike anything you've tasted. Let the server choose; they know.

A full dinner with wine runs ₾60–100 ($24–$40) at a solid neighborhood restaurant, more if you order freely.

**Cost: $25–$45**

**Budget: ~$33 | Mid-range: ~$50 | Splurge: ~$70**

## Day 2: Markets, Kitchens & Late-Night Wine

### Morning — Rustaveli & Deserter's Bazaar

Today is about going deeper—into a market, into a kitchen, into the rhythm of how Tbilisi actually feeds itself. Start on Rustaveli Avenue with coffee at any of the European-style cafés lining the grand boulevard, then head to the Deserter's Bazaar (near Station Square), a covered market where vendors have sold vegetables, cheese, spices, and churchkhela since Soviet times.

Walk slowly. Buy warm tone bread from a bakery stall, get a hunk of fresh sulguni cheese sliced to order, grab a handful of tarragon for the smell of it. This is breakfast by accumulation: bread, cheese, pickles, a glass of pomegranate juice. Vendors expect haggling on bulk items, not on small purchases.

**Cost: $5–$8**

### Afternoon — Cooking Class in Old Town or Fabrika Area

Book a hands-on Georgian cooking class—there are several near the Old Town or in the Fabrika creative hub. For ₾80–150 ($32–$60), you'll spend three hours learning to pleat khinkali (harder than it looks), roll out khachapuri dough, and make walnut sauce from scratch. You'll eat everything you make.

This replaces lunch entirely. You'll leave overfull and with skills you'll use for years. The best classes include wine and chacha tasting; the worst still involve eating a kilogram of cheese-bread you made yourself.

**Cost: $35–$60**

### Evening — Shardeni Street After Dark

By dinner, you won't be hungry—which is perfect, because tonight is about drinking and snacking. Head to Shardeni Street in the Old Town, which transforms after midnight into a narrow garden of late-night wine bars with tables spilling onto cobblestones.

Order a bottle of Rkatsiteli or Saperavi, plus a plate of pkhali (vegetable pâtés made from beets, spinach, or beans, each topped with pomegranate seeds) and some Georgian cheese. This isn't a meal; it's the Georgian approach to evening: wine, small bites, long conversation, more wine. If someone at the next table offers a toast, accept it.

**Cost: $20–$35**

**Budget: ~$60 | Mid-range: ~$85 | Splurge: ~$120**

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

### Morning — Fabrika Courtyard

Your last morning belongs to Fabrika, the Soviet-era sewing factory turned creative hub where Tbilisi's artists, digital nomads, and late-risers gather. The courtyard cafés serve proper coffee and a mix of Georgian and European breakfast options. Order **eggs with adjika** (Georgian chili paste) or, if you spot it, **chirbuli**—eggs scrambled with tomatoes and walnuts, served in a clay pot.

Sit outside if the weather holds. Watch the city wake up slowly. This is Tbilisi's new energy, and it tastes like strong espresso and spice.

**Cost: $8–$12**

### Afternoon — Narikala Walk & Abanotubani Snacking

Walk up to Narikala Fortress for the panoramic view of terracotta rooftops and sulfur bath domes, then descend back into the Old Town for a final round of street food. Stop at a churchkhela vendor for one last strand. Find a bakery for a final lobiani or a fresh-from-the-oven shotis puri (long, canoe-shaped bread).

Near the souvenir stalls below the fortress, you'll find women selling homemade tkemali (sour plum sauce) and adjika in recycled jars. Buy some; they're better than anything in stores.

**Cost: $5–$10**

### Evening — The Supra

End with the meal Tbilisi was made for: a full Georgian supra, the feast-format dinner with endless toasts, endless food, and a tamada orchestrating the emotional arc of the evening. Book at a restaurant in Vera or Mtatsminda that offers traditional supra service, or if you've made Georgian friends, get yourself invited to a home table.

Expect: bread, cheese, eggplant rolls, khinkali, khachapuri, roast pork, grilled vegetables, fresh herbs piled high, wine in quantities that seem medical, and chacha—the grape pomace brandy that closes every feast. The tamada will toast to God, to Georgia, to ancestors, to love, to you. You will toast back. You will eat more than you thought possible.

A supra at a restaurant runs ₾100–200+ ($40–$80) depending on wine and group size. Worth every lari.

**Cost: $45–$80**

### Day 3 Total Food Spend
**Budget: ~$58 | Mid-range: ~$85 | Splurge: ~$140**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Count your khinkali knobs.** Leaving the twisted tops on your plate isn't waste—it's tradition, and it's how the table tracks who's winning.

- **Amber wine takes adjustment.** Georgian qvevri wines fermented with grape skins taste tannic and complex, not fruity. Give it three glasses before deciding.

- **Cash is king in markets and old restaurants.** Cards work at newer spots, but the best lobiani comes from places that have never seen a POS terminal.

- **Supras run 3–4 hours minimum.** Don't book anything after. Cover your wine glass when you're done—it's the polite way to tap out without insulting the host.
