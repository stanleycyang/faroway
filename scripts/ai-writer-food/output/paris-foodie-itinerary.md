Three days in Paris taught me that eating well here isn't about finding the "best" restaurant—it's about finding the right croissant at 8 AM, the right wine bar at 4 PM, and the right steak frites at 10 PM when the dining room finally fills with actual Parisians. This itinerary is what I wish someone had handed me before I started. It would have saved me one mediocre crêpe, two overpriced tourist traps, and the heartbreak of arriving at a boulangerie five minutes after they sold their last pain au chocolat.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Lunch runs 12–2 PM, dinner rarely starts before 8 PM—arrive earlier and you'll eat alone or find locked doors. Most places take cards, but smaller bakeries and market stalls prefer cash. Tipping isn't expected, but rounding up a few euros is appreciated. Book dinner reservations 2–3 days ahead for popular bistros, a week or more for anything with a reputation. And please: say "Bonjour" when you enter anywhere. It costs nothing and changes everything.

## Day 1: Le Marais — The Classic Parisian Food Day

**Morning: The Croissant Hunt**

Start in Le Marais around 8:30 AM, before the medieval streets fill with tourists. Your mission: a croissant that shatters when you bite it, leaving golden flakes on your coat and butter on your fingers. The best ones come from neighborhood boulangeries where the line is short but steady—locals grabbing breakfast before work.

Order a croissant and a café crème, eat standing at the zinc counter or perched on a bench in Place des Vosges, Paris's oldest planned square with its hidden arcades and morning quiet. The croissant should be warm, deeply caramelized on the outside, with visible honeycomb layers inside. If it bends instead of breaks, keep walking.

*Estimated cost: €6–10 for croissant and coffee*

**Afternoon: Falafel, Markets, and the Art of Snacking**

By 12:30, you'll be ready for the famous falafel of Rue des Rosiers. Yes, it's crowded. Yes, there's a line. But the warm pita stuffed with crispy falafel, tahini, pickled vegetables, and a punch of harissa is worth the wait. Get it to go and eat it walking—this is acceptable, even encouraged, on this one street.

After lunch, walk north toward the Canal Saint-Martin, about 20 minutes through quieter streets. This tree-lined waterway with iron footbridges has the Paris you imagined: locals reading on benches, small wine bars with afternoon regulars, and zero tour buses. Stop at a fromagerie along the way and buy a small wedge of Comté and a demi-baguette. Eat on the canal edge. This is your afternoon snack, and it will cost less than a bad sandwich at the airport.

*Estimated cost: €15–22 for falafel lunch, cheese, bread, and a glass of wine*

**Evening: Steak Frites Done Right**

Dinner should be steak frites at a proper bistro—not because it's fancy, but because Paris does this dish better than anywhere else. Head back to Le Marais or the 11th arrondissement edges for a neighborhood spot where the menu is short and the wine list is chalked on a board.

Order entrecôte, cooked saignant (rare) unless you have objections. It should arrive with a pile of thin, crispy frites and a simple salad. A carafe of house red. Maybe a cheese course if you're still standing. The room will be loud by 9:30, packed with friends catching up over plates and pitchers. Stay until you're the last table talking.

*Estimated cost: €45–80 depending on wine and whether you add cheese*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€65 | Mid ~€100 | Splurge ~€150

## Day 2: Left Bank — Literary Cafés and Market Mornings

**Morning: Café Culture, Properly**

Cross to the Left Bank and plant yourself at a café in Saint-Germain-des-Prés by 9 AM. This neighborhood runs on intellectual pretension and excellent pastry—embrace both. Order a tartine (toasted bread with butter and jam) or eggs if you need protein, plus a café allongé to stretch the morning.

Sit outside if weather allows, facing the street. Watch Paris walk by: the women in scarves, the men with their small dogs, the students arguing about philosophy or their phones. You're not being lazy; you're doing this correctly.

*Estimated cost: €12–18 for a full breakfast with coffee*

**Afternoon: Latin Quarter Wandering and French Onion Soup**

Walk to the Latin Quarter, just east, where the Sorbonne students have kept bistro prices reasonable and bookshops plentiful. By 1 PM, find a table at a traditional spot—look for handwritten menus and older waiters who seem mildly annoyed by your existence (this is a good sign).

Order French onion soup: a dark crock of caramelized onion broth, deeply sweet from hours of cooking, topped with a raft of bread and gratinéed Gruyère. Eat it slowly. This is lunch, and it's enough.

Afterward, browse the bouquinistes along the Seine—the green bookstalls selling vintage prints and dog-eared paperbacks. Buy nothing or everything; either approach works.

*Estimated cost: €18–28 for soup, bread, and wine or coffee*

**Evening: A Cooking Class or a Splurge Dinner**

For Day 2 evening, you have a choice. Option one: book a cooking class in the late afternoon (3-hour sessions run €100–150) and learn to make a proper French sauce or debone a duck. You'll eat what you cook, surrounded by other travelers who also thought this sounded like a good idea. It is.

Option two: skip the class and save your budget for a splurge dinner. Head to a wine bar in Saint-Germain-des-Prés where natural wines meet serious small plates—jamón carved to order, oysters on ice, terrines with cornichons. This isn't a full meal; it's strategic grazing, and it's how Parisians eat when they're not performing a three-course dinner.

*Estimated cost: €100–150 for cooking class with dinner included, OR €60–90 for wine bar grazing*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€90 | Mid ~€140 | Splurge ~€250

## Day 3: Montmartre to Pigalle — The Farewell Feast

**Morning: Hilltop Pastry and Views**

Take the Metro to Abbesses and climb to Montmartre before the portrait artists set up shop. By 8 AM, this hilltop village feels almost quiet—narrow streets, ivy-covered walls, a church bell somewhere.

Find a boulangerie near Sacré-Cœur and get a pain au chocolat (or two—you're leaving tomorrow). Eat on the steps of the basilica, looking out over the Paris rooftops. This is the postcard view, and it's better with chocolate and butter.

*Estimated cost: €8–12 for pastries and coffee*

**Afternoon: Rue Lepic Market Walk and Crêpe Stop**

Walk down Rue Lepic, where an open-air market sells cheese, charcuterie, and produce to locals who've been shopping here for decades. This is where you assemble lunch: a few slices of saucisson, a container of olives, a small round of goat cheese, bread from the baker at the corner.

Find a bench in Square Louise-Michel or keep walking toward Pigalle. Along the way, stop for a crêpe from a street stand—Nutella and banana if you're basic (I am), or sugar and butter if you're a purist. Eat it immediately, before the edges cool.

*Estimated cost: €15–25 for market picnic and crêpe*

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your last dinner in Paris should be escargot, a carafe of Burgundy, and whatever else the waiter recommends. Head to Pigalle—specifically the "SoPi" (South Pigalle) stretch where cocktail bars and serious bistros share the sidewalk.

Escargot arrives in a dimpled metal tray: six or twelve snails swimming in garlic-parsley butter, eaten with a tiny fork and torn bread to soak up what's left. It tastes like the ocean and a garden and a grandmother's kitchen, all at once. Follow with whatever the kitchen does best—duck confit, perhaps, or a roast chicken for two—and finish with a cheese plate or a simple chocolate mousse.

Order a digestif. Stay late. Walk back to your hotel through empty streets, past shuttered boulangeries that will open again at 7 AM, starting the whole cycle over for someone else.

*Estimated cost: €70–120 for a full farewell dinner with wine*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€95 | Mid ~€150 | Splurge ~€200

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Bakeries sell out.** The best croissants and baguettes are gone by 10 AM on weekends. Set an alarm or accept disappointment.

- **"Formule" is your friend.** At lunch, most bistros offer a formule (set menu) that includes two or three courses for €15–25. It's always the best value and usually the best food.

- **Markets close early.** Outdoor markets typically wrap up by 1–2 PM. If a market visit is your plan, make it a morning plan.

- **Wine by the glass is fine.** Don't feel pressured to order bottles. Parisians drink pichet (pitcher) wine at lunch and glasses at wine bars. The house red is almost always good.
