Three days in Osaka broke me in the best way. I came with a notebook, stretchy pants, and a single mission: eat everything worth eating in Japan's kitchen capital. What follows is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed — every meal mapped, every neighborhood covered, every yen accounted for.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cash still rules at street stalls and smaller restaurants, though major spots take cards. Lunch runs 11:30am–2pm, dinner from 6pm — arrive early or queue. Tipping doesn't exist; don't leave money on the table. Most importantly: never eat while walking. Find a counter, a bench, or stand at the stall. This isn't just etiquette — it forces you to actually taste what you're eating.

## Day 1: Dotonbori and the Neon Heart

### Morning: Kuromon Market Grazing

Start at Kuromon Ichiba Market by 9am, before tour groups flood the narrow aisles. This isn't breakfast in the Western sense — it's a progressive feast through 170 stalls. Begin with grilled scallops from one of the seafood vendors near the market's east entrance, shells blackened over charcoal, meat still quivering. Move to a tamagoyaki (rolled omelet) stand for something sweet and eggy to balance the brine.

The goal isn't to fill up — it's to warm your stomach for the day ahead. Budget ¥1,500–2,500 ($10–17) for a handful of bites.

### Afternoon: Takoyaki Pilgrimage in Dotonbori

By noon, walk north to Dotonbori — fifteen minutes through covered arcades that smell like grilling meat and sugar. This is takoyaki territory, and you need to understand something: those octopus-filled batter balls vary wildly between stalls.

Skip the places with the longest tourist lines. Instead, find a stall where the cook works a copper takoyaki pan with a metal pick, rotating each ball at precisely the right moment. The exterior should shatter slightly, the interior staying almost liquid. Mayo, sauce, bonito flakes dancing in the heat.

Eat two or three orders from different vendors. Compare. Argue with yourself about which was better. This is the correct way to spend an afternoon in Osaka.

For a proper lunch, duck into one of the okonomiyaki restaurants along the canal. Sit at a teppan counter and watch the cook build your savory pancake — cabbage, pork belly, batter, more cabbage — pressing and flipping until the edges crisp. Get the pork and squid version if available. The cook will slice it into wedges; you eat directly from the griddle.

Lunch cost: ¥1,200–1,800 ($8–12) for okonomiyaki, plus ¥800–1,200 ($5–8) for takoyaki rounds.

### Evening: First Night Feast

Return to Dotonbori after dark when the Glico running man and a thousand other neon signs turn the canal into liquid color. This is when the neighborhood transforms from busy to electric.

For dinner, find a kushikatsu restaurant — breaded and fried skewers of everything from lotus root to quail eggs to wagyu. The ritual matters: dip once into the communal sauce, never twice. Vegetables, meats, even cheese — everything goes into the fryer and emerges golden and absurdly satisfying.

A proper kushikatsu dinner with beer runs ¥3,500–5,500 ($24–37) at a mid-range spot. Splurge for a counter seat where you can watch the fry cooks work.

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~¥7,000 ($47) · Mid ~¥12,000 ($80) · Splurge ~¥22,000 ($147)

## Day 2: Shinsekai and the Old Osaka

### Morning: Kissaten Coffee Culture

Start in Tennoji with coffee at a kissaten — one of those wood-paneled, smoke-stained cafés that haven't changed since 1975. Order a morning set: thick toast, hard-boiled egg, tiny salad, coffee strong enough to wake the dead. This is how Osaka salarymen have started their days for decades.

The Hozenji Yokocho alley, tucked behind Namba, has several old kissaten worth finding. Look for the moss-covered statue of Fudo Myoo — the alley runs alongside it, lantern-lit even in morning shadow.

Breakfast cost: ¥600–900 ($4–6).

### Afternoon: Deep Shinsekai

Take the subway to Shinsekai by 11:30am. This neighborhood peaked in the 1930s, and that's exactly when it feels stuck — Tsutenkaku Tower looming over streets lined with kushikatsu joints and pachinko parlors. It's gloriously weird.

For lunch, this is where kushikatsu was invented, and the competition is fierce. Look for places with handwritten menus and older clientele — they've survived on quality, not tourist traffic. Order the omakase course and let the chef decide what gets fried.

After lunch, walk the side streets. Find a stand selling dango (rice dumplings) or imagawayaki (red bean pancakes). The afternoon here isn't about one meal — it's about continuous snacking while watching Osaka at its most unguarded.

Lunch cost: ¥2,000–3,500 ($13–23). Snacks: ¥500–800 ($3–5).

### Evening: Ramen and Night Walking

For dinner, find Osaka-style ramen. The local version runs lighter than Hakata's heavy tonkotsu — thicker noodles, less overwhelming pork fat, often finished with a splash of soy. Look for a shop with a ticket machine outside (you order before sitting) and no more than ten seats.

A bowl runs ¥850–1,200 ($6–8). Add gyoza — pan-fried dumplings with crispy bottoms, served in groups of six or twelve. You'll want twelve.

End the night in Namba's backstreets, where standing bars pour whisky highballs for ¥300 and izakayas spill noise onto the sidewalk. This isn't planned eating anymore — it's following your nose.

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~¥6,500 ($43) · Mid ~¥11,000 ($73) · Splurge ~¥20,000 ($133)

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

### Morning: Market Returns

Go back to Kuromon Market, but differently this time. You know the layout now. Head straight for the wagyu vendors and get a few skewers of A5 beef, seared tableside and finished with just salt. This is an absurd breakfast and you should feel no guilt.

Follow it with fresh uni (sea urchin) if you can stomach the sweetness at 9am — and you can, because this is your last full day.

Morning cost: ¥2,500–4,000 ($17–27) for the good stuff.

### Afternoon: Nakazaki-cho and Negiyaki

Subway to Nakazaki-cho, the vintage district that most visitors miss. Antique shops, indie cafés, narrow lanes with potted plants — a different Osaka entirely. Find lunch at a small teppan restaurant serving negiyaki, the leaner cousin of okonomiyaki. It's essentially a green onion pancake, less heavy, more aromatic, perfect for a warm afternoon.

Wander after. Get soft-serve from one of the hip shops. Buy a coffee from a third-wave roaster. This neighborhood is about slowing down before the final meal.

Lunch and snacks: ¥1,500–2,500 ($10–17).

### Evening: The Last Supper

Your final Osaka dinner should be the best version of what this city does. That means finding a serious kappo or teppanyaki counter — somewhere with a chef who's been doing this for thirty years and cooks like it's personal.

In Namba or Kitashinchi (the upscale dining district), look for places with counter seating and omakase menus. You'll eat seasonal sashimi, grilled fish, perhaps wagyu, a soup, pickles, rice — the progression of a proper Japanese meal.

This is the splurge. Budget ¥8,000–15,000 ($53–100) for dinner with drinks. If you've been responsible all trip, go higher. Order the sake pairing.

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~¥8,000 ($53) · Mid ~¥14,000 ($93) · Splurge ~¥28,000 ($187)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Lunch is often the value play.** Many high-end restaurants offer lunch sets at half the dinner price — same kitchen, same quality, smaller portions.

- **Queue timing matters.** Show up at 11:15am or 5:45pm — fifteen minutes before standard meal times — and you'll often skip the worst lines.

- **Osaka people talk.** If you're at a counter and the person next to you looks friendly, ask what they ordered. This is how you find dishes that aren't on the English menu.

- **Convenience store backup is real.** A 7-Eleven onigiri at 2am is a perfectly valid Osaka meal. Don't be precious about it.
