The steam rises from the bowl in lazy curls, carrying the scent of slow-braised lamb and wild thyme across the wooden table. Outside, a January wind howls down Laugavegur, but inside this basement café, I'm wrapped in the kind of warmth that only comes from a proper Icelandic lamb soup — the kind that's been simmering since morning, the kind that makes you understand why people have survived here for over a thousand years.

Reykjavik's food scene is having a moment, but its soul still lives in dishes shaped by volcanic soil, icy North Atlantic waters, and the pragmatic creativity of people who learned to make something extraordinary from very little. After three weeks of eating my way through this compact, colorful capital, here's what you absolutely cannot miss.

#### Lamb Soup (Kjötsúpa)

This is not delicate restaurant food. This is survival in a bowl — hearty, humble, and deeply satisfying. Traditional Icelandic lamb soup starts with free-range lamb that has spent its summer wandering mountainsides, grazing on wild herbs, moss, and berries. That terroir translates directly into the meat: clean, slightly gamey, more complex than any lamb I've tasted elsewhere.

The soup itself is a meditation on simplicity. Chunks of bone-in lamb simmer for hours with rutabaga, carrots, potatoes, and sometimes cabbage. The broth turns silky from the rendered fat, tasting of herbs that seem to belong to this landscape — a whisper of thyme, occasionally caraway. What arrives at your table is a bowl of pure sustenance: meat falling off the bone, vegetables soft but not mushy, and a broth you'll want to drink straight from the rim.

Find it at old-school cafeterias and traditional restaurants throughout downtown. Café Loki, perched across from Hallgrímskirkja, serves a version that feels like eating at an Icelandic grandmother's table. Expect to pay around $15–20 for a generous bowl, often served with dense, dark rúgbrauð (rye bread) smeared with butter. Order it for lunch on a cold day. Order it for dinner after a day on the Golden Circle. Just order it.

#### Pylsur (Icelandic Hot Dog)

Forget everything you think you know about hot dogs. The Icelandic pylsur is something else entirely — a snappy, smoky revelation that has converted skeptics and vegetarians alike (though not for long, obviously).

The magic is in the meat: a blend of lamb, pork, and beef that gives the dog a distinctive sweetness and depth. The casing snaps audibly when you bite through, giving way to juicy, well-seasoned meat. But the real alchemy happens on top. The classic order is *eina með öllu* — "one with everything" — which means raw white onion, crispy fried onion, ketchup, sweet mustard, and remoulade, that creamy, slightly tangy sauce that ties everything together.

The most famous stand is Bæjarins Beztu Pylsur near the harbor, which has been serving hot dogs since 1937 and counts Bill Clinton among its devotees. But honestly? The pylsur at any gas station in Iceland is going to be better than most gourmet hot dogs you've had elsewhere. At around $5, it's also the cheapest meal in a notoriously expensive city. Grab one at 2 AM stumbling out of Kaffibarinn. Grab one sober at noon. Grab two.

#### Plokkfiskur

If lamb soup is Iceland's mountain soul, plokkfiskur is its coastal heart. The name translates roughly to "mashed fish," which sounds deeply unappetizing until you taste it — and then you understand why Icelanders have been making this dish for centuries.

Traditionally, plokkfiskur was invented to use every scrap of the catch. Cod or haddock gets poached, then flaked and folded into creamy mashed potatoes with sautéed onions, butter, and béchamel. The texture is like the most comforting fish pie you've ever had, minus the crust. The flavor is mild, sweet from the onions, with the clean ocean taste of fresh North Atlantic fish cutting through the richness.

What makes plokkfiskur special is its honesty. There's nowhere to hide with this dish — you taste the quality of the fish, the freshness of the butter, the hand that mashed the potatoes. It's poverty food elevated by excellent ingredients. Look for it at traditional restaurants and lunch spots around downtown; Icelandic Fish & Chips in the Old Harbour does a modernized version, while Þrír Frakkar offers something closer to what your Icelandic great-grandmother would recognize. Expect to pay $18–25 for a proper portion.

#### Fresh Langoustine

Let me be direct: if you leave Reykjavik without eating langoustine, you have made a mistake.

These sweet, delicate creatures — somewhere between shrimp and lobster — come from the cold waters around Iceland's southern coast. The cold slows their growth, concentrating flavor in their tender, almost buttery flesh. Crack open the shell of a simply grilled langoustine tail, dip it in garlic butter, and you'll experience seafood purity — that clean, briny sweetness that warm-water shellfish can only dream of.

The Old Harbour is your destination. Restaurants like Grillið (upscale, tasting menu territory) and Messinn (more accessible, family-style pans) both showcase langoustine beautifully. Some places serve them whole, some in bisques, some tossed with pasta. The classic preparation is the soup at places around the harbor — a creamy, coral-colored broth loaded with tail meat, served with bread for soaking up every drop. Fresh langoustine dishes range from $25 for soup to $50+ for elaborate preparations, but this is money well spent.

#### Skyr

You've probably seen skyr in your home grocery store by now, marketed as Icelandic yogurt. I'm here to tell you that eating skyr in Iceland is a different experience entirely.

Technically, skyr is a fresh cheese, not yogurt — made by straining skim milk with live cultures until it reaches a thickness that holds a spoon upright. The texture is impossibly dense and creamy, like Greek yogurt's more sophisticated cousin. The flavor is tangy but clean, with a slight sweetness that makes it equally at home at breakfast or dessert.

Icelanders eat skyr constantly and have for over a thousand years. You'll find it in cafés topped with fresh blueberries and a drizzle of honey, or served as *hræringur* — skyr blended with cream until fluffy and light. At grocery stores like Bónus, grab a tub of Ísey Skyr for about $3 and eat it in your hotel room with a spoon. At restaurants, look for skyr appearing in desserts, often with rhubarb compote or crowberries. This is one of those ingredients that tastes truer to itself in its homeland.

#### Arctic Char

If you're accustomed to salmon, Arctic char will feel familiar but elevated — a more subtle, delicate fish with flesh that ranges from pale pink to deep coral. These fish thrive in Iceland's cold, pristine lakes and rivers, and the best preparations let that clean flavor speak.

The texture is silky, almost melting on the tongue when properly cooked. The fat content is lower than salmon, so expect a lighter, less oily experience. I had an Arctic char at Grandi Mathöll (the food hall in the Grandi creative district) that was simply pan-seared, skin crackling, served over vegetables with a dill sauce, and I thought about it for three days afterward. Many restaurants smoke their Arctic char, which concentrates the flavor beautifully — look for it at breakfast buffets or on open-faced sandwiches. Expect to pay $25–40 for a main course.

#### Kleinur

These twisted doughnuts are Iceland's answer to the afternoon slump. Mildly sweet, spiced with cardamom, and fried until golden, kleinur are somewhere between a doughnut and a biscuit — less greasy than American doughnuts, more substantial, with a tender crumb that pairs perfectly with strong coffee.

Kleinur have been twisted and fried in Icelandic kitchens for generations, originally as a way to use up leftover dough. The cardamom — that warm, almost citrusy spice — gives them a distinctly Scandinavian character. You'll find them at every bakery and supermarket, but the best come from old-school bakeries like Sandholt, where they're fried fresh each morning. Buy a bag for about $4–6 and keep them in your coat pocket for emergency snacking. They're perfect fuel for chasing waterfalls.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Reykjavik dining follows its own rhythm. Lunch is the meal for value — many upscale restaurants offer abbreviated menus at significantly lower prices between noon and 2 PM. Dinner starts late by Scandinavian standards; don't show up before 7 PM expecting a bustling scene.

Icelanders don't really do tipping — service is included, and rounding up is generous enough. At casual spots, you often order at the counter. At nicer restaurants, pace yourself; meals here are meant to be slow, especially multicourse affairs that might stretch past two hours.

For budget survival, embrace the Bónus grocery store (the one with the pink pig logo). Stock your hotel room with skyr, bread, and cheese for breakfast, spend your money on one or two exceptional meals per day, and fill the gaps with hot dogs. This is not a city for eating three restaurant meals daily unless your budget is truly unlimited.
