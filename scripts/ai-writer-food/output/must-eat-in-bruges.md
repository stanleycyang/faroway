The first bite of a perfectly golden Belgian waffle at a tiny stand near the Markt stopped me mid-step on a gray Tuesday morning. The caramelized sugar shattered against my teeth, giving way to a pillowy interior still warm from the iron, and I stood there in the drizzle, not caring that waffle crumbs were dusting my coat, thinking: this is why I came to Bruges.

I spent three weeks eating through this medieval city of canals and cobblestones, tracking down dishes in cramped grandmother kitchens, elegant guild halls, and steamy frituurs that haven't changed their oil recipes in decades. What I found is a cuisine that doesn't shout — it murmurs, deeply satisfied with itself. Bruges food is built on centuries of trade, Flemish stubbornness, and the kind of butter-and-cream abundance that makes you grateful for all the walking the old town demands.

Here's what you need to eat.

---

#### Moules-Frites

A black pot arrives at your table, lid still on, steam curling from the edges like a secret about to be told. You lift it and there they are: a small mountain of mussels, their shells yawning open to reveal plump orange-gold meat glistening in a broth of white wine, shallots, celery, and cream. The smell hits you — briny, buttery, with that particular sweetness of shellfish that spent their lives in cold North Sea waters.

You eat them with your hands, using an empty shell as a pincer to pluck each mussel from its cradle. The texture is tender, almost custard-like, and the broth — you drink the broth, propriety be damned, lifting the pot to your lips like it's soup, which it basically is. The frites alongside are twice-fried and shatteringly crisp, perfect vehicles for whatever sauce you've chosen, but honestly, they're best dunked directly into the mussel liquor.

This dish exists because Belgium has always been at the crossroads of trade routes, and mussels came cheap from Zeeland, just north. Paired with the potato (introduced in the 18th century and immediately beloved), it became the working-class meal that transcended class entirely.

Find them at any traditional brasserie in the old town, especially along the quieter streets near Jan Van Eyckplein. Lunch or dinner, though the dinner crowds mean fresher batches. Expect to pay €18–€25 for a proper pot with frites.

#### Stoofvlees (Flemish Beef Stew)

If Bruges had a flavor, it would be this: dark, malty, slightly sweet, impossibly deep. Stoofvlees is beef braised for hours in Belgian brown ale until the meat doesn't so much fall apart as surrender completely, collapsing into a sauce the color of mahogany.

The first spoonful coats your mouth with layers — the caramel notes of the beer, the sharp sweetness of the bread spread with mustard that traditionally thickens the sauce, the silky gelatin from the slow-cooked collagen. It comes with more frites, always frites, which you'll drag through the gravy until the plate looks like it's been licked clean (because, let's be honest, you might).

This is peasant food refined over centuries, born from the necessity of tenderizing tough cuts and the happy accident of cooking with the local beer. Every family has their version; some add a touch of apple syrup, others swear by a specific abbey ale.

Seek it out in brown cafés — those dim, wood-paneled establishments where the walls have absorbed decades of cigarette smoke and philosophy. 't Brugs Beertje or De Garre will serve you well. Around €16–€20 for a hearty portion.

#### Belgian Frites

Yes, they deserve their own section. Yes, they're just fried potatoes. No, you don't understand until you've had them here.

The exterior shatters. The interior is fluffy, almost creamy, with that deep potato sweetness you forgot potatoes could have. The secret is the double-fry — once at a lower temperature to cook through, then again at high heat to crisp — and the insistence on the Bintje potato, a variety with exactly the right starch-to-moisture ratio.

You'll get them in a paper cone, standing at a frituur while locals argue about whether you should choose andalouse (mayonnaise with tomato and pepper), stoofvleessaus (beef stew gravy), or samurai (spicy mayo). The correct answer is to try all of them across multiple visits.

Belgium and France have feuded over who invented the frite for centuries. I don't care. I only care that at midnight on a Friday, the line at a frituur near the Burg stretches down the street, and everyone in it knows exactly what they're about to experience.

Any frituur will do, but the ones that have been family-run for generations — look for the worn counters, the no-nonsense service — are where the magic lives. €3–€5 for a cone with sauce.

#### Waterzooi

Ghent claims this dish, but Bruges serves it with the quiet confidence of a city that knows good food doesn't need a birth certificate.

Waterzooi is a soup that's also a stew, a cream bath for chicken (or sometimes fish) surrounded by a garden of carrots, leeks, celery, and potatoes. The broth is enriched with egg yolks and cream until it reaches a consistency that hovers between liquid and velvet. You eat it with a spoon, mostly, though the chicken requires a knife, and by the end, you're tipping the bowl toward you to capture every last pale-gold drop.

The name means "watery mess," which tells you everything about Flemish humility. What it doesn't tell you is how the gentle poaching keeps the chicken impossibly moist, or how the vegetables retain just enough bite to punctuate the richness, or how a bowl of this on a rainy Bruges afternoon feels like being wrapped in your grandmother's favorite blanket.

Restaurant De Stove serves a beautiful version in an intimate dining room. Lunch is ideal. €20–€28.

#### Speculoos

You've had speculoos, probably — those thin, spiced cookies that come with coffee on airplanes. You've never had them like this.

In Bruges, speculoos are baked fresh, and the spice blend — cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves, ginger, cardamom — hits your nose before the cookie hits your lips. The texture is simultaneously snappy and sandy, crumbling on your tongue into buttery, caramelized sweetness with that warm spice undercurrent. They're often pressed into wooden molds depicting windmills or saints, edible folk art.

The tradition dates to the feast of St. Nicholas, but Bruges has decided every day deserves speculoos. They're correct.

Find them at bakeries like Banketbakkerij Vermeire, where they've been making them the same way for over a century. A bag of cookies runs €4–€7.

#### Belgian Chocolate Pralines

I won't insult you by explaining that Belgian chocolate is good. But I will tell you about the moment a pistachio praline from a small chocolatier near the Begijnhof melted on my tongue — the shell cracking into bittersweet darkness, the filling blooming into something silky and green and faintly, impossibly, tasting of actual pistachios instead of whatever chemical approximation you're used to.

Belgian pralines have a specific anatomy: a chocolate shell surrounding a filling that might be ganache, gianduja, or various creams and nuts. The key is the balance — the shell should be thin enough to snap cleanly but thick enough to contribute its own flavor. The filling should be assertive without overwhelming.

Skip the touristy chains. The Chocolate Line (owned by a self-described "shock-o-latier") does wild flavors — wasabi, cola, Havana tobacco. Dumon offers pristine classics. €6–€10 for a small box that will not survive the walk back to your hotel.

#### Belgian Abbey Beer

It's not food, but you cannot eat in Bruges without drinking this.

The monks who brewed within these abbey walls centuries ago weren't trying to get drunk — they were making "liquid bread" for fasting periods, nutritious and complex. What they created, almost accidentally, was beer so layered it requires the attention you'd give a Burgundy.

A Westmalle Tripel glows golden in the glass, its head thick and persistent, the aroma yeasty and floral with hints of banana and honey. The first sip is deceptively light before the alcohol warmth blooms and the finish goes dry and peppery. A Rochefort 10 is something else entirely — dark as maple syrup, flavors of fig, toffee, dark fruit, the kind of beer you sip over an hour while rain streaks the window.

Every café serves abbey beers; the best ones will guide you through the list based on what you're eating. €4–€8 per glass.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main event in Bruges — restaurants offer their best deals and freshest preparations between noon and 2 PM, and locals take their time. Dinner can be leisurely to the point of glacial; expect to spend two hours minimum, and don't wave down the server for the check (they'll find it rude). Tip is included in the price, but rounding up a few euros is standard for good service.

Don't eat on the Markt. The square is beautiful, the food is middling, and you're paying triple for the view. Walk five minutes in any direction and the quality doubles while prices halve. If a restaurant has laminated photos of its dishes outside, keep walking.

Embrace the rhythm: coffee and something sweet mid-morning, a proper seated lunch, frites from a frituur in the late afternoon, a long dinner anchored by beer. And always, always end the night with chocolate, eaten slowly, in your hotel room, with the windows open to the canal air.
