# Tbilisi Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The steam rises from a clay pot as the server cracks it open tableside, releasing the perfume of tarragon, fenugreek, and lamb that's been bragging about itself for the past three hours. Around you, a table of Georgian men raises glasses of amber wine while an elderly tamada delivers a toast so poetic you almost forget you're about to demolish your fourth khachapuri of the day. This is lunch in Tbilisi—and you haven't even started drinking yet.

## A City That Eats Like It Invented Eating

Georgia might have invented wine 8,000 years ago, but Tbilisi perfected the art of building an entire civilization around the table. This is a city where dinner regularly becomes a four-hour philosophical debate punctuated by mandatory toasts, where refusing a second helping is considered a minor diplomatic incident, and where the phrase "just a quick bite" doesn't translate into Georgian.

The food here sits at the crossroads of empires—Persian, Ottoman, Russian, and Silk Road influences layered over millennia into something distinctly, fiercely Georgian. You'll taste cumin and coriander seeds from the east, walnut-garlic pastes that echo Persian cooking, and bread traditions that predate most European nations. But Georgian cuisine refuses to be anyone's footnote. The cheese pulls differently here. The herbs grow wilder. The dumplings require a technique you'll spend three meals perfecting.

What makes Tbilisi's table exceptional isn't fusion or innovation—it's stubborn tradition executed with religious devotion. The grandmothers making khinkali have been folding dumplings with the same 18-pleat technique their grandmothers used. The clay qvevri vessels fermenting wine underground haven't changed in eight millennia. When a food culture is this old, it doesn't need to impress you. It knows exactly what it is.

## The Essential Dishes: What You Cannot Leave Without Eating

**Khachapuri Adjaruli** commands your attention first. This boat-shaped bread arrives bubbling with molten cheese, a raw egg yolk floating in the center like a small golden sun, with a knob of butter sliding into the volcanic mess. You tear the pointed ends, stir everything together, and use the bread to scoop the stretchy, salty, rich disaster into your mouth. The best versions in Abanotubani have a crust that crackles, cheese that pulls in threads, and a richness that will make you question every pizza you've ever loved. Budget ₾12-18 for one that serves as a meal.

**Khinkali** are the dumplings that will test your ego. These twisted parcels of spiced meat (usually beef, pork, or lamb) swim in their own hot broth, trapped inside a pleated dough wrapper. You grip the topknot, bite a small hole, slurp the soup without burning your chin (good luck), then devour the filling. The knot itself? Discarded—eating it marks you as an amateur. The best khinkali in the Vera neighborhood have thin, elastic skins and a broth so savory it makes you close your eyes involuntarily. Expect ₾1-2 per dumpling, and you'll want at least five.

**Badrijani Nigvzit** appears on every table and never disappoints. Thin slices of fried eggplant rolled around a paste of crushed walnuts, garlic, coriander, and fenugreek, crowned with pomegranate seeds that burst tart against the earthy richness. The texture is silky, the garlic assertive, the walnut paste creamy enough to spread on your bread. This is Georgia's go-to appetizer, and at ₾8-12 for a generous plate, it's your best introduction to the Georgian spice cabinet.

**Lobiani** is khachapuri's humbler cousin—flatbread stuffed with kidney beans mashed with onions, herbs, and enough spice to make you forget it's vegetarian. The best versions have a charred, slightly crispy exterior and a filling that's creamy and smoky. Street vendors throughout the Old Town sell them hot for ₾3-5, and they make an ideal walking breakfast.

**Churchkhela** hangs in every market stall like strings of candles—walnuts or hazelnuts threaded onto string, dipped repeatedly in thickened grape juice until they form a chewy, sweet exterior. The texture is somewhere between fruit leather and caramel, the flavor deeply grapey with an almost wine-like tang. At ₾3-6 per string, it's the perfect pocket snack for your Narikala Fortress climb.

## Street Food: The Pulse of Hungry Tbilisi

The real action happens in the narrow lanes around Abanotubani and the alleys branching off Shardeni Street, where tiny stalls and hole-in-the-wall bakeries produce khachapuri and lobiani fresh every twenty minutes. Morning is prime time—arrive between 9 and 11 AM when the ovens are hot and the cheese hasn't been sitting.

Look for the places with a line of locals and a woman who looks like she's been making the same bread for forty years—because she has. The rhythm is simple: point at what you want, pay in cash (₾5-15 for most items), eat standing or perched on a ledge nearby. Nobody lingers. The food is too hot and too good to wait.

The Dry Bridge Market offers a different experience on weekend mornings—less pure street food, more churchkhela vendors and women selling fresh cheese wrapped in leaves alongside their Soviet-era cameras and antique silverware. This is grazing territory. Buy churchkhela, cheese, and a bottle of chacha if you're feeling brave, then find a bench.

Late-night street food concentrates around Fabrika after midnight, where kebab vendors and impromptu food stalls feed the crowd spilling out of the bars. The quality is hit-or-miss, but a ₾8 lamb kebab at 2 AM surrounded by artists and travelers isn't about perfection—it's about being exactly where you should be.

## Eating Well at Every Price Point

**Budget (Under ₾55/$20):** This is where Tbilisi shines absurdly bright. A full meal of khinkali (8-10 dumplings), a plate of badrijani, fresh bread, and a half-liter of house wine costs ₾35-50 in any neighborhood restaurant outside the tourist core. In Vera, small family-run spots serve set lunches for ₾15-25 that include soup, bread, and a main dish. The food isn't "cheap for the quality"—it's genuinely excellent, prepared by people who would be insulted to serve you anything less. This is the tier where most Georgians eat daily, and you should too.

**Mid-Range (₾85-125/$30-44):** This budget unlocks proper supra territory—full Georgian feasts with multiple courses, natural wine pairings, and dishes like chahkapuli (lamb stewed with tarragon, plums, and white wine) or chicken tabaka (crispy spatchcocked chicken weighted with a brick). Restaurants in the Rustaveli area and upscale Vera spots offer tasting menus that walk you through regional Georgian cooking with some ceremony. You're paying for atmosphere, service, and the kind of meal that runs three hours.

**Splurge (₾160+/$55+):** When to spend big? For a private supra experience in a traditional restaurant with a dedicated tamada leading toasts, paired with aged qvevri wines from Kakheti. Or for modern Georgian cuisine that deconstructs traditional dishes with technical precision while respecting their soul. These meals are events—five-plus courses, rare wines, and the kind of hospitality that makes you feel like a visiting dignitary. Worth it once, particularly if you're celebrating something.

## For the Plant-Based Traveler

Here's the honest truth: Georgia is remarkably vegetarian-friendly, almost accidentally. The Orthodox fasting calendar means traditional cuisine developed hundreds of meatless dishes that nobody considers "vegetarian food"—they're just food.

**Badrijani nigvzit** (eggplant-walnut rolls), **lobio** (stewed kidney beans with herbs), **pkhali** (vegetable patés made from spinach, beet, or cabbage with walnut paste), and **ajapsandali** (ratatouille-like vegetable stew) appear on every menu. Lobiani gives you a satisfying bean-stuffed bread. Cheese-based khachapuri handles most cravings.

Vegans face more challenges—cheese is everywhere, and many dishes use butter. But specify "no cheese, no butter" (Georgian kitchens are accommodating), and you'll find the vegetable dishes, bean stews, and pickled vegetables more than sufficient. Fabrika's international restaurants offer more explicitly vegan options for days when you need a break from beans.

## The Food Geography of Tbilisi

**Abanotubani** is where food tradition runs deepest—this is Old Town territory, the sulfur bath district where khachapuri shops have operated for generations and small restaurants serve unfiltered house wine from plastic bottles. The tourists are here, yes, but so are the oldest recipes.

**Vera** is where locals eat. Tree-lined streets hide restaurants with vine-covered courtyards, chefs who learned from their grandmothers, and not a single English menu in sight. This is the neighborhood for your best khinkali, your most honest supra, your quietest dinner where the server treats you like family.

**Fabrika** offers modern Tbilisi's food culture—international influences, natural wine bars, craft beer, and late-night bites in a Soviet-era factory turned creative village. Eat here when you want variety, or when you need a break from dumplings (it happens, rarely).

The **Dezerter Bazaar** near Station Square is your market experience—vendors selling fresh cheese, herbs, spices, churchkhela, dried fruits, and enough pickled vegetables to supply the entire city. Go hungry on a Saturday morning, sample aggressively, and leave with ₾50 worth of provisions you don't strictly need.

## How to Eat Like You Belong

Georgian meals follow a rhythm. Toast, drink, eat, toast again. The tamada (toastmaster) controls the flow—wait for their lead before drinking, and always empty your glass for serious toasts (to Georgia, to family, to the departed). Wine glasses refill constantly; covering your glass with your palm signals you're pausing without offense.

Eat with your hands when appropriate—khinkali are finger food, bread tears, churchkhela doesn't require utensils. Tipping is appreciated but not mandatory; 10% acknowledges good service, rounding up handles casual meals.

Dinner rarely starts before 8 PM and often runs past midnight when wine is involved. Lunch is substantial. Breakfast is often skipped in favor of late-morning khachapuri. Adjust your schedule accordingly—restaurants peak between 9 and 11 PM, when the city truly sits down to eat.

The cardinal rule: never refuse food at a Georgian table. To decline is to insult the host's hospitality, their cooking, and possibly their ancestors. Pace yourself, but keep your plate active. They'll respect you for trying.
