The banana leaf unfolds like a gift, steam curling up and carrying that first hit of lemongrass and galangal straight into your chest. It's 7 PM in the Old Market area, the heat of the day finally breaking, and you're about to take your first bite of fish amok — the curry so impossibly silky it barely needs your teeth. This is the moment Siem Reap stops being about temples and starts being about what happens when Khmer grandmothers get hold of a coconut and some freshwater fish.

I spent three weeks eating through this town, from pre-dawn rice plates to midnight snacks on Pub Street, and what I found was a cuisine that whispers rather than shouts. Cambodian food doesn't assault you with chili heat or demand your attention with fermented funk. It seduces. It layers. It makes you lean in closer.

Here's what you need to eat before you leave.

---

#### Fish Amok

This is Cambodia's national dish, and for good reason — it's the edible version of Khmer restraint and elegance. A mousse-like curry of freshwater fish (usually catfish or snakehead), steamed inside a banana leaf cup until it sets into something between a soufflé and a custard. The curry paste underneath is where the magic lives: kroeung, a foundational Khmer spice blend of lemongrass, galangal, turmeric, and makrut lime leaves pounded in a mortar until it becomes aromatic gold. Coconut cream enriches everything, and a final drizzle of thick coconut "head" on top adds that luxurious slick of fat.

The texture is what gets you. It's not soupy. It's not solid. It trembles on your spoon, then melts across your tongue with a sweetness that's earthy rather than sugary. Amok was historically reserved for Khmer royalty and religious ceremonies — a dish that required time, skill, and precious ingredients. Today it's everywhere, but the best versions still come from places that steam each portion individually rather than batch-cook in big trays.

Find it at family-run restaurants around Wat Bo or the quieter edges of Old Market. Expect to pay $4–$7 for a properly made version. Skip the places advertising it on giant backlit signs — seek out the spots where Khmer families are actually eating.

#### Bai Sach Chrouk

Wake up at 6 AM. I know. But this is the breakfast that built a nation, and it disappears by 9.

Bai sach chrouk is elemental: thin-sliced pork, marinated in coconut milk and garlic, grilled over charcoal until the edges caramelize into sweet, smoky lacquer. It arrives over broken rice — the fractured grains that Cambodians actually prefer for their texture — with a bowl of clear chicken broth on the side, a fried egg if you're lucky, and pickled vegetables that cut through the richness like a blade.

The pork is the thing. When it's right, each slice has that char-to-tender gradient, the fat rendered but not gone, the garlic mellowed by heat into something almost sweet. The coconut milk marinade gives it a subtle richness you can't quite place until someone tells you. This is a dish born from practicality — a way to make cheap cuts of pork extraordinary, served fast to workers heading to the rice fields or construction sites.

Every neighborhood has its bai sach chrouk lady. Look for the ones with charcoal grills smoking on the sidewalk and a queue of motos parked outside. Around the Kandal Village area, you'll find versions that lean slightly more refined, but honestly, the best I had was from an unmarked stall near the night market, where a woman with forearms like a boxer sliced my pork with terrifying precision. It cost $1.50.

#### Nom Banh Chok

If bai sach chrouk is the breakfast of workers, nom banh chok is the breakfast of poets. Thin rice noodles — freshly pressed that morning, with a subtle chew that factory noodles can never replicate — swimming in a green fish curry that tastes like the jungle filtered through a kitchen.

The curry is made from pounded freshwater fish mixed with lemongrass, turmeric, and a generous handful of whatever greens are in season: banana blossom, water lily stems, cucumber, long beans, morning glory. It's served room temperature, which feels wrong until you taste it — the coolness lets the herbal flavors bloom without the heat flattening them. It's light. It's bright. It tastes green in a way that makes you realize most "green" dishes are lying to you.

This is women's food, in the historical sense — made almost exclusively by female vendors who learned the recipe from their mothers and grandmothers. The noodle-pressing alone is a skill that takes years. Nom banh chok vendors set up in the early morning around local markets (Psar Leu market is a reliable spot), serving from enormous aluminum pots to a crowd of regulars who eat fast and leave. By 10 AM, they're gone.

Around $1–$2 a bowl. Eat it standing or crouched on a plastic stool. This is how it's meant to be.

#### Lok Lak

Here's the dish that bridges Cambodia and the world: tender cubes of beef, stir-fried hard and fast in a screaming hot wok with garlic and black pepper, served over lettuce and tomato with a dipping sauce that could bring the dead back to life.

That sauce. Lime juice. Black pepper — aggressive amounts of it. A pinch of salt. Sometimes a raw egg yolk in the center for you to swirl in. It's elemental, violent, perfect. When the beef hits it, still sizzling, the acid cuts through the char and the pepper blooms in your sinuses and suddenly you understand why Cambodians put this sauce on everything.

Lok lak is French-colonial heritage in edible form — the love child of steak frites and Khmer spice sensibility. The name likely comes from the Cantonese "luc lac" (shaking beef), which came through Vietnamese traders, which came through Chinese immigrants, which tells you everything about how food actually travels through empires. Cambodia absorbed the influences and made something entirely its own.

Find lok lak everywhere from Pub Street tourist spots ($5–$6) to local Khmer restaurants around Wat Bo ($3–$4). The key tell: does the beef have crust? Is the sauce freshly made with visible pepper granules? If yes, stay.

#### Fresh Spring Rolls

Not Vietnamese. The Khmer version is its own thing — fatter, more generous, less fussy about perfect rolling technique. Rice paper wrapped around rice vermicelli, fresh herbs (mint, basil, lettuce), sometimes sliced pork or shrimp, served with a peanut dipping sauce sweetened with palm sugar and spiked with chili.

The pleasure here is textural: the slight resistance of rice paper, the cool crunch of vegetables, the soft give of noodles, then the rich coating of peanut sauce tying it all together. It's a dish designed for Siem Reap's heat — refreshing, light, requiring no cooking and no hot food sweat. Street vendors sell them pre-rolled from glass cases for around $1–$2 for several pieces. The Old Market area has multiple stalls, but the freshness varies — look for rolls made within the hour, not sitting since morning.

#### Fried Tarantula

Let's address the spider in the room.

Yes, fried tarantula is partly a tourist gimmick now. But it started as genuine survival food — during the Khmer Rouge era, when famine swept the country, Cambodians in Skuon and other regions ate whatever protein they could find, including the large Thai zebra tarantulas that burrow in the red earth. What began as desperation became tradition, and now it's a snack you can find at night markets throughout Siem Reap.

Here's the truth: they're good. Deep-fried in oil with garlic and salt until the legs are crispy as potato chips and the abdomen is... well, let's call it "creamy." The flavor is somewhere between soft-shell crab and fried chicken skin, with a faint earthiness that reminds you this creature lived underground. The legs are the easy entry point — pure crunch, almost no spider flavor. The body is where it gets real.

Night markets around Old Market and Pub Street have tarantula vendors charging around $1–$2 per spider. They'll often offer scorpions and crickets too, but the tarantula is the main event.

#### Prahok Ktis

A dish most tourists never order because nobody explains it properly. Prahok is fermented fish paste — the backbone of Khmer cooking, used the way fish sauce is used in Thailand or Vietnam, but more aggressive, more pungent, more *alive*. Prahok ktis takes that paste, stir-fries it with minced pork and coconut cream, adds sliced lemongrass and makrut lime, and serves it as a dip with raw vegetables and rice.

It's funky. It's salty. It's exactly what this cuisine tastes like when tourists aren't watching. The fermented fish hits first, then the coconut rounds it out, then the herbs lift everything into something almost elegant. This is ancestral Khmer food, the flavor that older Cambodians mean when they talk about what their grandmothers cooked.

Find it at traditional Khmer restaurants around Wat Bo village, or ask specifically at places that cater to local families. $3–$5. Be brave.

---

#### Num Pang

Cambodia's answer to the bánh mì, and proof that French colonialism left at least one good thing behind. A crispy baguette — the kind with a shatteringly thin crust and soft interior — stuffed with pâté, pickled vegetables, cucumber, cilantro, and your protein of choice: grilled pork, chicken, sardines, or pork meatballs.

It's a breakfast sandwich, a lunch sandwich, a 2 AM sandwich. The bread is the key — Siem Reap bakers still make baguettes the old way, baked in small batches in wood-fired ovens, crusty enough to scrape the roof of your mouth if you're not careful. The fillings are more generous than Vietnamese versions, messier, more thrown-together. This is a sandwich for eating over a napkin while walking, dripping sauce onto the street.

Street vendors throughout Old Market and Kandal Village sell num pang for $1–$1.50. The morning is prime time, when the bread is freshest.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Siem Reap eats early. Breakfast vendors are often packing up by 9 AM, and dinner starts around 5:30–6 PM. If you show up at 8 PM expecting a leisurely meal at a local Khmer spot, you might find them closing.

Ordering is communal — Cambodians share plates rather than eating individual portions. Order one dish per person plus one extra for the table, and everyone eats from everything. Rice is the centerpiece; everything else is a condiment to make the rice interesting.

Don't fear the markets. Psar Chas (Old Market) has an entire food section where pointing at things is perfectly acceptable, and vendors will guide you if you seem genuinely curious rather than squeamish. The plastic stools are always smaller than you expect. Your knees will hit the table. This is correct.

And drink sugar cane juice. It costs fifty cents, it's pressed fresh in front of you, and it's the only thing that makes noon in Siem Reap bearable.
