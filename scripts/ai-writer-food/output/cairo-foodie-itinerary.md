Three days in Cairo taught me that this city doesn't just feed you — it overwhelms you. The portions are generous, the flavors are layered with centuries of technique, and everyone from taxi drivers to museum guards has an opinion about where you should eat. This is the itinerary I pieced together after too many meals, not enough sleep, and the kind of obsessive note-taking that made waiters nervous.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cairo runs late. Breakfast spots open around 7 AM, but lunch rarely happens before 2 PM and dinner often starts at 9 PM or later. Carry small bills — 10 and 20 EGP notes — for tipping (baksheesh is expected everywhere, including at restaurants where service isn't included). Cash dominates at street stalls and markets; upscale restaurants take cards. Book ahead for Friday lunch at popular spots, as families flood in after prayers. And please: bottled water only, no exceptions.

## Day 1: Giza — Pyramids, Street Food, and the Meal That Started It All

### Morning: Fuel Before the Monuments

Start early in Giza, near the pyramids plateau, with a breakfast that Egyptians have eaten for millennia. Find a street cart or small shop serving **ful medames** — slow-cooked fava beans mashed with cumin, lemon juice, and a pour of green olive oil. The best versions come in battered aluminum pots that have been seasoning for decades. Pair it with fresh baladi bread, still warm, and a side of **ta'meya** — Egyptian falafel made with fava beans instead of chickpeas, greener and lighter than anything you've had in the Levant. The exterior shatters; the inside stays creamy.

**Where**: The small shops along Pyramids Road (Sharia Al-Haram) within walking distance of the Sphinx entrance. Look for the ones packed with construction workers and taxi drivers at 7 AM — they know.

**Cost**: $3–5 for a full breakfast with tea.

### Afternoon: Market Lunch and the Koshari Hunt

After the pyramids (go at 8 AM opening to beat the buses), head to **Downtown (Wust al-Balad)** for the dish that defines Cairo street food. **Koshari** is chaos in a bowl: lentils, rice, macaroni, chickpeas, and crispy fried onions, all doused in spiced tomato sauce and a garlic-vinegar kick that hits the back of your throat. Every Cairene has their koshari shop, and they will argue about it.

The famous spots — Koshary Abou Tarek, with its multi-story operation and assembly-line efficiency — deliver a reliable version at tourist-friendly prices. But the small shops in the alleys behind Talaat Harb Square often outperform them, with crispier onions and more aggressive garlic. Order medium (wasat) unless you're sharing; the large portions could feed a family.

After lunch, walk through Downtown's crumbling colonial-era buildings to Café Riche or one of the old coffeehouses for Turkish coffee thick enough to read your fortune in.

**Cost**: $4–8 for koshari and coffee.

### Evening: Nile Views and Grilled Everything

Cross to **Zamalek**, the leafy island in the Nile, for dinner. This is where Cairo's moneyed class eats, and the restaurant scene shows it. For your first night, go for grilled meats done properly: kebab, kofta, and lamb chops at one of the established grill houses. The better spots bring complimentary mezze — baba ghanoush, tahini, pickled vegetables — before the main event arrives.

Sit outside if the weather allows. The Nile at night, with feluccas drifting past and the city glittering on both banks, makes the inevitable traffic noise fade into atmosphere.

**Where**: The restaurants along Sharia Abu El Feda have Nile views; book ahead for terrace seating.

**Cost**: Budget $15–25; Mid-range $35–50; Splurge $80–120 with wine.

**Day 1 total food spend**: Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$55 | Splurge ~$135

## Day 2: Islamic Cairo — Medieval Streets and Ancient Recipes

### Morning: Beans and History

Start in **Islamic Cairo**, the medieval heart of the city, with another ful breakfast — but this time, seek out a shop that serves it with preserved lemon and a hit of cumin that makes yesterday's version seem restrained. The narrow streets around Al-Azhar Mosque wake up slowly; you'll share the sidewalk with shopkeepers hosing down storefronts and cats who own the territory.

Add feteer, Egypt's layered pastry, in its savory form: stuffed with cheese and herbs, the dough pulled impossibly thin and folded into buttery, flaky layers. Watch the feteer maker work if you can — it's performance art.

**Cost**: $4–7 for a full spread.

### Afternoon: Khan el-Khalili and the Spice Merchants

Spend your afternoon in **Khan el-Khalili bazaar**, where you'll eat as much as you shop. The spice stalls alone justify the visit: mountains of cumin, coriander, dried hibiscus for karkade, and spice blends for koshari and molokhia that you should absolutely buy and bring home.

For lunch, find **shawarma** in the streets surrounding the market — chicken or beef shaved from the rotating spit, stuffed into pita with tahini, pickled turnips, and a smear of garlic paste. The good shops have a line; the great shops have a line that moves fast because they've been doing this for generations.

Duck into **El Fishawi**, allegedly the oldest café in Cairo, for mint tea and shisha if you want the full tourist experience — the tea is serviceable, the atmosphere is the point.

For something hidden, walk south to the **Tentmakers Market (Khayamiya)** past the fabric stalls to find small eateries serving home-style food to the craftspeople. The molokhia here, when available, is made the old way.

**Cost**: $6–12 for shawarma, tea, and market snacks.

### Evening: Molokhia Done Right

Tonight is about **molokhia**, the polarizing green jute leaf stew that separates Cairo lovers from Cairo likers. When done well, it's silky and vegetal, served over rice with garlicky fried coriander crackling on top and chicken or rabbit alongside. When done poorly, it's slimy and bitter. The difference is technique and freshness.

Seek out a traditional restaurant in Islamic Cairo or head back to Downtown for one of the old establishments that specialize in Egyptian home cooking. Felfela has served molokhia to tourists and diplomats for decades; the quality remains consistent. For something less expected, ask your hotel concierge for a family-run spot — the best molokhia I had was in a restaurant that held maybe eight tables, where the owner's mother still supervised the kitchen.

**Cost**: Budget $12–18; Mid-range $25–40; Splurge $60–90 with appetizers and drinks.

**Day 2 total food spend**: Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$60 | Splurge ~$110

## Day 3: Downtown to Zamalek — The Best of Everything

### Morning: The Breakfast Spread

Your last Cairo breakfast should be comprehensive. Find a fuul and ta'meya shop in **Downtown** and order everything: ful medames with extra cumin, ta'meya fried to order, eggs scrambled with tomatoes and peppers (shakshuka-adjacent but lighter), cheese, olives, and enough bread to transport it all. Wash it down with sahlab, the thick orchid-root drink dusted with cinnamon and nuts — winter comfort in a cup.

This is the breakfast you'll remember on the plane home.

**Cost**: $5–10 for the full spread.

### Afternoon: Al-Azhar Park and Final Grazing

Walk off breakfast with a visit to **Al-Azhar Park**, the landscaped gardens built on centuries of rubble with views across Islamic Cairo to the Citadel. The park has its own restaurant, Lakeside Café, where you can eat salads and grilled meats with the medieval city as your backdrop. But for a true final food walk, head back down into the streets.

Graze through the afternoon: fresh sugarcane juice from a cart, roasted sweet potatoes in winter months, grilled corn with lime and chili. Pick up a final bag of spices in Khan el-Khalili. Find one more shawarma if you have room — you won't find this version anywhere else.

**Cost**: $8–15 for park lunch and snacks.

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Finish in **Zamalek** at a restaurant where Egyptian cooking meets fine dining — not fusion, but traditional dishes treated with reverence and better ingredients. You want stuffed pigeon (hamam mahshi), the rice-stuffed bird that's been celebratory food here for centuries. You want lamb cooked until it falls apart. You want every mezze plate they offer.

Book a rooftop table if available. The Nile at night, one last time.

For dessert, insist on **Om Ali** — the warm bread pudding soaked in milk, layered with nuts and raisins, gratinéed until bubbling. It's named for a sultan's wife, allegedly, and it tastes like something worth fighting a medieval court battle over.

**Cost**: Budget $18–25; Mid-range $40–60; Splurge $100–180 with cocktails and wine.

**Day 3 total food spend**: Budget ~$35 | Mid ~$70 | Splurge ~$200

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Eat when Egyptians eat**: Lunch after 2 PM, dinner after 9 PM. Showing up at noon or 7 PM means eating alone with reheated food.

- **Watch the bread**: Fresh baladi bread is transcendent; stale bread ruins everything. If the bread basket feels cold or stiff, ask for fresh — no one will be offended.

- **The garlic sauce matters**: Every shawarma and grilled meat spot makes their own garlic toum. The great ones are creamy and aggressive; the mediocre ones taste like mayo with garlic powder. Ask for extra if it's good.

- **Carry small bills religiously**: You'll tip the guy who hands you napkins, the guy who shows you to your table, and the guy who just exists near the restaurant door. It's the system. 5–10 EGP per small service, 10–15% at restaurants.
