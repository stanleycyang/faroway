Three days in Tokyo. One mission: eat everything worth eating. I've done the walking, the waiting in line, the pointing at plastic food displays, and the sitting at counters so small my elbows touched strangers who became temporary friends over shared bowls of broth. This is the itinerary I built from all of it — the one I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Tokyo runs on cash. Many of the best places — the eight-seat ramen shops, the yakitori alleys, the market stalls — don't take cards. Carry ¥10,000–20,000 daily. Don't tip anywhere; it confuses people and can feel insulting. Lunch starts at 11:30, dinner at 6. Popular spots fill fast, but breakfast is yours for the taking. Remove shoes when you see a raised floor or a shoe rack. And please, don't walk while eating — find a spot, stand still, finish your food, then move on.

## Day 1: The Classic Tokyo Stomach Reset

**Theme: Tsukiji to Ginza — fish, rice, and getting your bearings**

### Morning: Tsukiji Outer Market, 7:30 AM

Start where Tokyo's food obsession makes the most sense: the outer market at Tsukiji. The inner wholesale market moved to Toyosu, but the outer market remains a dense, glorious maze of vendors who've been perfecting single items for generations.

Walk the narrow lanes first without buying. Let your appetite build. Then commit: a bowl of kaisendon (rice topped with whatever fish looked best at 4 AM) from one of the standing-only spots. The rice is warm, the fish is cold, and the ratio is all wrong in the best way — more topping than base. Pair it with a cup of tamagoyaki, the sweet-savory layered omelet sold in thick slices from a vendor who's probably been doing this since your parents were born.

**Cost: ¥2,000–3,500 (~$15–25)**

### Afternoon: Ginza Department Store Basement, 12:30 PM

Walk from Tsukiji to Ginza — it's fifteen minutes and lets you digest. Head underground into any major department store (Mitsukoshi or Matsuya work well) and descend into the depachika, the basement food halls that function as Japan's most civilized form of grocery shopping.

This isn't lunch yet. This is research. Sample freely: wagashi (traditional sweets), pickles, grilled fish on sticks, mochi in seasonal flavors. Many counters offer tastes. Buy a few things for later — an onigiri, a slice of cake, something wrapped in bamboo leaf.

For actual lunch, surface and find a tonkatsu specialist. Ginza has several that serve the breaded pork cutlet the way it should be: crust shatteringly crisp, meat still faintly pink in the center, accompanied by mountains of shredded cabbage and a mortar of sesame seeds you grind yourself into sauce. Order the rosu (fatty loin) over the hire (lean tenderloin). Fat is flavor.

**Cost: ¥2,500–4,000 (~$18–28) for tonkatsu set**

### Evening: Omakase Sushi in Ginza, 7:00 PM

Stay in Ginza for dinner. This is where you splurge if you're going to splurge anywhere. Book ahead (some places require weeks of notice; others take same-day reservations for counter seats). Sit at a sushi counter, order omakase, and let the chef decide what you eat.

Expect 12–15 pieces of nigiri, each placed directly on the counter or in your hand. Eat immediately — the temperature balance between warm rice and cool fish is the point. Don't dip in soy sauce unless invited. The chef has already seasoned it.

Budget option: Skip the reservation and find a standing sushi bar. Same fish, less ceremony, one-third the price.

**Cost: Budget ¥4,000 (~$28) · Mid ¥15,000 (~$105) · Splurge ¥35,000+ (~$245+)**

**Day 1 Total: Budget ~¥9,000 ($63) · Mid ~¥22,000 ($154) · Splurge ~¥45,000 ($315)**

## Day 2: Smoke, Char, and the Old City

**Theme: Asakusa to Shinjuku — tradition in the morning, chaos at night**

### Morning: Asakusa, 8:00 AM

Take the train to Asakusa early, before the tour groups arrive at Senso-ji Temple. The temple matters, but so does the food around it. Walk Nakamise-dori when the shutters are just rolling up. Buy ningyo-yaki — small cakes filled with sweet bean paste, shaped like the temple or local characters — from a vendor using a cast-iron mold that's been in use for decades.

For a proper breakfast, find a kissaten, one of the old-style coffee shops with velvet seats and hand-dripped coffee. Order a morning set: thick toast, a hard-boiled egg, a small salad, and coffee strong enough to reset your entire nervous system. This is how Tokyoites have started their days since before Starbucks existed.

**Cost: ¥800–1,200 (~$6–9)**

### Afternoon: Yanaka and the Old Tokyo That Remains, 12:00 PM

Train to Nippori, then walk into Yanaka — a neighborhood that survived the war and the developers, leaving narrow streets lined with temples, cemeteries, and shops that sell one thing extremely well.

Follow Yanaka Ginza shopping street downhill. Stop for menchi katsu (fried meat patty) from a shop with a line. Eat it standing. Continue to a small tempura shop — look for the places with just a counter and a fryer. Order a tempura set: prawns, seasonal vegetables, and a few pieces you won't recognize, all battered so lightly they seem to float. The batter should crackle, not crunch.

This area has cats everywhere. Watch for them between bites.

**Cost: ¥1,800–3,000 (~$13–21)**

### Evening: Yakitori Alley in Shinjuku, 7:00 PM

Cross the city to Shinjuku. Specifically, to the smoky corridors of Memory Lane (locals still call it Piss Alley, though the tourism board prefers you didn't). Squeeze into a counter seat at a yakitori joint — any one with a grill and a line — and start ordering.

Get the chicken hearts, the skin, the thigh with scallion, the cartilage, the liver. Order two of each because the first one disappears before you've processed how good it is. Drink a beer or a highball. Talk to the person next to you; the smoke and the close quarters make strangers friendly.

If yakitori alley feels too intense, walk to Golden Gai instead — dozens of tiny themed bars, each seating five to ten people. Some have cover charges, some don't. All of them have character.

**Cost: Yakitori ¥3,000–5,000 (~$21–35) · Add drinks ¥1,000–2,000 (~$7–14)**

**Day 2 Total: Budget ~¥6,500 ($46) · Mid ~¥10,000 ($70) · Splurge ~¥18,000 ($126)**

## Day 3: The Final Push

**Theme: Shibuya, ramen, and eating like it's your last day**

### Morning: Japanese Breakfast Done Right, 9:00 AM

Find a place serving a traditional Japanese breakfast set: grilled salmon, rice, miso soup, pickles, natto if you're brave, a small salad, and tea. This exists in hotel restaurants, but it's better at a local teishoku-ya — a set-meal restaurant where the morning crowd is salarymen reading newspapers.

The correct approach: eat the fish first, alternate between rice and pickles, finish with miso soup. Drink the tea throughout. Feel unreasonably healthy despite everything you've eaten in the past two days.

**Cost: ¥1,000–1,800 (~$7–13)**

### Afternoon: Shibuya and Shimokitazawa Food Walk, 12:30 PM

Start in Shibuya. Stand at the crossing, take the photo, then escape the crowds by walking to Nonbei Yokocho — a narrow drinking alley that's quiet during the day and lets you see the lanterns and signage without competing for space.

Then train two stops to Shimokitazawa, the bohemian neighborhood that feels like Tokyo forgot to modernize. This is for wandering: vintage shops, independent coffee roasters, curry houses run by obsessives. Find a curry spot — not the chain kind, but the kind where someone's grandmother's recipe became a restaurant. Japanese curry is different: thick, sweet, served over rice with pickles and maybe a fried egg.

Grab taiyaki (fish-shaped cake filled with sweet bean or custard) from a street vendor as a walking snack.

**Cost: ¥1,200–2,000 (~$8–14) for curry · ¥300 (~$2) for taiyaki**

### Evening: The Ramen You'll Think About Later, 7:00 PM

This is the meal that matters. Ramen in Tokyo deserves its own pilgrimage, and you've saved it for last.

Choose your denomination: Tonkotsu (pork bone broth, creamy, from Kyushu originally but everywhere now), shoyu (soy sauce base, clearer broth, classic Tokyo style), miso (heartier, from Hokkaido), or tsukemen (dipping noodles, broth on the side).

Find a shop with a ticket machine outside. Insert cash, press the button for what you want (photos help), take your ticket, hand it to the cook, sit at the counter, and wait. When the bowl arrives, eat fast. Ramen waits for no one — the noodles absorb broth, the egg yolk sets, the pork cools. This is not a leisurely meal. This is you against the clock, and the clock is winning from the moment the bowl hits the counter.

Order an extra egg. Get the chashu (braised pork). Drink the broth to the last drop. This is how you say goodbye to Tokyo.

**Cost: ¥1,000–1,800 (~$7–13)**

**Day 3 Total: Budget ~¥3,500 ($25) · Mid ~¥6,000 ($42) · Splurge ~¥12,000 ($84)**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Convenience stores are not a compromise.** 7-Eleven, Lawson, and FamilyMart sell onigiri, egg sandwiches, oden, and nikuman (steamed buns) that outperform fast food anywhere else in the world. A ¥500 convenience store dinner is a perfectly valid choice.

- **Line up early, or don't line up at all.** Popular ramen shops and sushi counters get lines by 11 AM. Arrive at opening or accept that you're waiting 45 minutes. There's no middle ground.

- **Lunch sets are the secret.** Many restaurants that charge ¥10,000+ for dinner offer lunch sets between ¥1,500–3,000 with similar quality. Target upscale neighborhoods like Ginza and Roppongi at midday.

- **Point at the plastic.** Most restaurants display wax or plastic food models outside. Pointing is not rude — it's expected. This is how the system works.
