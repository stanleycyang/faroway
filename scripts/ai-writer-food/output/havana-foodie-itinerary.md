# Havana Foodie Itinerary: 3 Days of Eating Your Way Through Havana

Three days in Havana taught me that Cuban food is better than its reputation — you just have to know where to look. Forget the tourist traps serving grey rice and rubbery chicken. This itinerary follows the thread I wish I'd pulled from day one: eating with intention through crumbling colonial plazas, gritty neighborhood joints, and the private restaurants (paladares) where Havana's real cooking happens.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Cubans eat late — lunch around 1pm, dinner rarely before 8pm. Bring cash in Euros or Canadian dollars; US dollars get hit with a 10% surcharge, and international cards frequently don't work at all. Tip 10% at paladares (it goes directly to staff). Book dinner reservations at popular spots by showing up earlier that day — phone lines are unreliable. Learn to say "sin prisa" (no rush) and mean it.

## Day 1: Old Havana — Colonial Plazas and the Classics

Today you eat your way through Habana Vieja, the UNESCO-protected colonial heart. Every meal happens within a 15-minute walk of Plaza de la Catedral, so leave time between courses to wander cobblestone streets lined with pastel facades and laundry-draped balconies.

### Morning: Cuban Coffee and a Proper Start

Skip the hotel buffet. Walk to a corner cafetería near Calle Obispo and order a *café cubano* — a thimble of espresso cut with raw sugar that hits like velvet lightning. Cost: $0.50. Pair it with *pan con tortilla*, the Cuban answer to a breakfast sandwich: soft bread stuffed with a thin omelet, sometimes with a slice of ham if you're lucky. The bread is slightly sweet, the eggs just set. It costs almost nothing ($1–2) and sets you up for the morning.

**Where**: Any unmarked cafetería window along Obispo or near Plaza Vieja — look for locals lined up at a counter.

**Cost**: $2–4

### Afternoon: Lunch at a Paladar and Market Grazing

By 1pm, settle into a proper lunch. This is your first encounter with *Ropa Vieja*, and it should be a good one. The shredded beef comes braised low and slow in tomato sauce with peppers and onions, served over white rice with a mound of *Moros y Cristianos* (black beans and rice cooked together — the island's essential side). At a mid-range paladar in Habana Vieja, a plate runs $12–18 and arrives with fried plantains on the side.

After lunch, walk to the **San José Craft Market** on the waterfront. Yes, it's touristy, but the food stalls tucked along the edges sell fresh coconut water hacked open with a machete ($1) and *chicharrones* — pork cracklings fried crisp and salted simply. Grab a bag and snack as you browse. Later, swing through the small produce market near Plaza del Cristo to see what fruit is in season — mamey sapote, guava, or tiny sweet bananas.

**Lunch cost**: $15–22
**Snacks**: $3–5

### Evening: Dinner in a Colonial Courtyard

As the light turns golden, head to a paladar housed in a restored mansion near Plaza de la Catedral. This is where you eat *Lechón Asado* — slow-roasted pork that's been marinating in sour orange and garlic, cooked until the meat pulls apart and the skin crackles. Order it with *tostones*, the twice-fried green plantain discs served with a garlic mojo for dipping. The pork is sweet, the tostones starchy and crisp — they were made for each other.

Linger with a mojito made tableside. In the right hands, it's transformative: fresh mint, white rum, lime, a touch of sugar, soda water. You're in the city where this drink was born.

**Cost**: $25–45 depending on drinks

### Day 1 Total Food Spend
**Budget**: ~$45 | **Mid-range**: ~$70 | **Splurge**: ~$120

## Day 2: Centro Habana — Grit, Soul, and the Best Home Cooking

Leave the polished plazas behind. Centro Habana is raw, loud, and alive — laundry strung between crumbling balconies, dominoes slapped on sidewalk tables, music bleeding from open windows. The food here is less refined but often more delicious, cooked in tiny private kitchens where someone's grandmother runs the show.

### Morning: Street Breakfast and Afro-Cuban Art

Start at **Callejón de Hamel**, the Afro-Cuban street art alley painted in wild murals and filled with shrines to Santería orishas. Arrive early before the crowds and find the small window selling *guarapo* — fresh-pressed sugarcane juice, grassy and sweet. Pair it with a *croqueta*, a fried ham-and-béchamel fritter that's pure comfort. On Sundays, rumba performances start at noon here, but any morning the alley belongs to you and the artists setting up.

**Cost**: $2–4

### Afternoon: Cooking Class or the Best Cuban Sandwich

Fork in the road: if you want to cook, book a morning class at a private home kitchen where you'll learn to make *sofrito* (the garlic-tomato-pepper base of everything), cook black beans properly, and understand why Cuban food is about technique, not complexity. Classes run $35–50 and include the meal you make.

If you'd rather eat, hunt down a *Cuban Sandwich* — the real one. Roast pork, sliced ham, Swiss cheese, pickles, and yellow mustard pressed between soft bread until the outside crisps and the cheese melts into everything. The best versions in Centro Habana come from unmarked lunch counters where the plancha has been seasoned by decades of sandwiches. Ask locals for "el mejor sándwich cubano cerca" and follow their directions. You'll pay $4–6 for something that would cost $18 in Miami.

Walk it off by heading toward the Malecón, the seafront boulevard where Havana exhales. Grab a beer from a window vendor ($1) and sit on the seawall watching fishermen and lovers share the same view.

**Lunch cost**: $8–15 (or $35–50 for cooking class)
**Snacks/drinks**: $3–5

### Evening: Paladar Dinner in a Private Home

Tonight you eat in someone's living room. The best paladares in Centro Habana seat maybe twelve people in spaces that were once (and sometimes still are) family apartments. The menu is what's fresh: maybe grilled fish rubbed with lime and garlic, whole fried red snapper if you're lucky, always rice and beans, always fried plantains. A cold *Cristal* or *Bucanero* beer cuts through the richness.

This is the closest thing to a Cuban grandmother cooking for you. The service is slow, the AC might be questionable, and it doesn't matter at all.

**Cost**: $20–35

### Day 2 Total Food Spend
**Budget**: ~$35 | **Mid-range**: ~$60 | **Splurge**: ~$95

## Day 3: Vedado — 1950s Glamour and the Farewell Feast

Your final day moves to Vedado, the neighborhood of faded 1950s mansions, wide avenues, and Havana's best restaurant scene. This is where private chefs are pushing Cuban food forward, and where you'll have your most memorable meal.

### Morning: Breakfast at a Casa Particular

If you're staying at a *casa particular* (private homestay), today you actually eat the breakfast included in your stay. Cuban home breakfasts are simple but generous: fresh tropical fruit, eggs however you like, toast with guava jam, a small pot of coffee. The fruit alone — papaya, pineapple, mango in season — is worth waking up for. Sit in the courtyard if there is one. Watch Vedado wake up.

**Cost**: Included with lodging (or $5–8 at a café)

### Afternoon: Rooftop Lunch and Neighborhood Exploration

Walk to **El Cocinero**, a restaurant and bar built inside a converted cooking oil factory — you'll know it by the chimney turned into a rooftop terrace. Lunch here is more modern than anywhere else you've eaten: think grilled octopus, ceviche with coconut milk, elevated takes on traditional dishes. It's pricier, but the view from the terrace over Vedado's rooftops justifies every peso.

After lunch, walk through Vedado's residential streets — this neighborhood rewards wandering. Stop for afternoon ice cream at **Coppelia**, the legendary outdoor ice cream park that's been serving Cubans since 1966. Tourist line or local line? Take the local line, pay in pesos, and get the same ice cream for a fraction of the price. Helado de chocolate, two scoops. You've earned it.

**Lunch cost**: $25–40
**Ice cream**: $2

### Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On

Your last dinner in Havana should be in Miramar, the diplomatic quarter where the city's most ambitious paladares have set up in converted mansions. Take a taxi ($8–12 from Vedado) and arrive hungry.

This is where you order the tasting menu if one exists — let the kitchen show off. Expect dishes that riff on Cuban classics: *ropa vieja* reimagined as a terrine, *tostones* transformed into crispy cups holding ceviche, pork slow-cooked for hours and served with a mojo that sings with garlic and sour orange. The wine list will be limited (this is still Cuba), but the rum selection will not. End with a añejo, sipped neat.

Walk out onto quiet Miramar avenues afterward, full and satisfied, knowing you ate Havana the right way.

**Cost**: $45–75

### Day 3 Total Food Spend
**Budget**: ~$40 | **Mid-range**: ~$75 | **Splurge**: ~$130

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Avoid state-run restaurants** — they're universally disappointing. If the menu is laminated and the staff looks bored, walk away. Paladares (private restaurants) are always the move.

- **Lunch is often cheaper and just as good** — many paladares serve identical dishes at lunch for 30–40% less than dinner prices. Eat your biggest meal midday.

- **Bring snacks from home for emergencies** — Havana's food supply is inconsistent. Some days a restaurant won't have fish; some days they won't have beef. Pack protein bars or nuts just in case.

- **Learn the phrase "¿Qué hay hoy?"** (What do you have today?) — it works better than asking for a menu at casual spots, and often gets you the freshest thing in the kitchen.
