Prague demands a certain surrender. Three days here taught me that the best eating happens when you stop looking for "authentic Czech food" and start following the smell of roasting pork through cobblestone alleys, the sound of beer glasses clinking at 11 AM, the sight of steam rising from a bowl of creamy soup in a wood-paneled pub where nobody speaks English. This is an itinerary built on scraped plates and loose pants.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Czechs eat lunch between noon and 2 PM — miss this window and kitchens close. Dinner starts around 7 PM. Credit cards work in most restaurants, but carry cash (Czech Koruna) for markets and old-school pubs. Tip 10% by telling your server the total you want to pay — don't leave coins on the table. Say "Dobrý den" when entering any establishment, and when someone raises a beer to you, look them in the eyes, say "Na zdraví," and mean it.

## Day 1: Old Town Foundations

**Morning — The Pastry Wake-Up**

Start in Staré Město before the crowds descend on the Astronomical Clock. By 8 AM, the bakeries are warm and the display cases are full of koláče — those beautiful round pastries with pools of fruit, cheese, or poppy seed filling pressed into the center. Find a neighborhood pekárna (bakery) away from Old Town Square itself, where a koláč costs around 25 CZK ($1) instead of tourist-inflated prices. Get two: one tvaroh (sweet cheese) and one švestka (plum). Pair with a flat white at any of the third-wave coffee spots that have colonized the medieval streets.

*Cost: $5–8*

**Afternoon — The Fried Cheese Education**

Walk toward Havelská Market, the open-air market that's been operating since the 13th century. Ignore the painted eggs and wooden toys — you're here for the food stalls and the surrounding pubs. By noon, find a seat at a no-frills hospoda (pub) within walking distance and order smažený sýr. Yes, it's a slab of Edam cheese, breaded, deep-fried, and served with tartar sauce and maybe some fries. Yes, it sounds like drunk food. It is drunk food. It's also perfect — the crunch giving way to molten cheese, cut by the tang of the sauce. Order a half-liter of whatever's on tap (Pilsner Urquell or Staropramen, around 45 CZK) and don't apologize for eating this at 12:30 PM.

After, walk off the cheese through the Jewish Quarter toward the river. The streets here are quieter, the architecture more somber.

*Cost: $12–18*

**Evening — The Svíčková Test**

Dinner is svíčková, and this is where you choose your Prague path. This dish — beef sirloin marinated for days, sliced thin, swimming in a creamy sauce of puréed root vegetables, topped with cranberries and a slice of lemon, flanked by bread dumplings — is the Czech Sunday dinner, the grandmother's specialty, the thing every restaurant claims to do best.

In Old Town, you'll pay more but get a version that's refined, perhaps too polished. The sauce should be thick and pink-beige, the meat tender enough to cut with a fork, the dumplings (houskové knedlíky) sliced fresh and ready to soak up every drop. A proper svíčková runs 250–350 CZK ($11–15) in a mid-range restaurant. Don't rush it. Order another beer. Watch the room fill with locals who've been coming here for decades.

*Cost: $25–40*

**Day 1 Total: Budget ~$45 | Mid-range ~$70 | Splurge ~$120**

## Day 2: Across the River

**Morning — Malá Strana & the Slow Start**

Cross Charles Bridge early (before 8 AM, when the tour groups arrive) and descend into Malá Strana, the baroque neighborhood tucked below Prague Castle. The streets here are narrower, the pace slower. Find a café in one of the embassy-lined streets and order a proper Czech breakfast — often a simple affair of bread, butter, jam, maybe some ham and cheese. Some places serve a version of scrambled eggs with chives.

This isn't a destination breakfast; it's fuel. You need energy for the walk to come.

*Cost: $8–12*

**Afternoon — The Soup & the Castle Climb**

Before tackling the castle, eat something that will sustain you. Duck into a restaurant along Nerudova street (the main artery climbing toward the castle) and order kulajda. This is the soup that converted me — a creamy, tangy broth of potatoes and dill, scattered with mushrooms, and always, always topped with a poached egg that you break into the soup, letting the yolk enrich every spoonful. It's served hot in a deep bowl, often with a side of bread, and it costs around 120 CZK ($5). This soup will get you up the hill.

Spend the afternoon wandering the castle complex (buy tickets online to skip security lines), then descend through the Wallenstein Palace Gardens for the view and the quiet.

*Cost: $15–25*

**Evening — The Pork, the Dumplings, the Cabbage**

Tonight is vepřo-knedlo-zelo: the holy trinity of Czech comfort food. Roast pork, bread dumplings, sauerkraut. Find it in Malá Strana or take the tram to Vinohrady, the leafy residential district where young Prague lives. Here, the restaurants are less tourist-oriented, the portions more aggressive, the beer cheaper.

A proper vepřo-knedlo-zelo arrives on an oval plate, the pork roasted until the fat renders and the skin crisps, the dumplings sliced thick and steaming, the sauerkraut cooked down with caraway until it's sweet and sour and impossible to stop eating. This is 300 CZK ($13) at a neighborhood restaurant, maybe 450 CZK ($20) at a fancier spot. Either way, you're not eating again tonight.

*Cost: $30–50*

**Day 2 Total: Budget ~$55 | Mid-range ~$90 | Splurge ~$160**

## Day 3: Markets, Neighborhoods & the Farewell Feast

**Morning — Saturday at Náplavka**

If your third day falls on a Saturday, there's only one place to be for breakfast: Náplavka farmers market, stretched along the Vltava riverbank south of the National Theatre. This is where Prague actually shops — crates of seasonal vegetables, wheels of cheese, sausages hanging from hooks, and most importantly, hot food stalls selling everything from trdelník (the cinnamon-sugar chimney cake you've seen everywhere, but here made fresh and not for tourists) to klobása sausages split and griddled until charred at the edges.

Wander with purpose. Eat a klobása with mustard straight from a paper tray. Get a square of koláč from whichever grandmother's stall has the shortest line. Buy some ořechy (roasted nuts) for later. Stand at the water with coffee and watch the swans beg for scraps.

If it's not Saturday, head to Holešovice — the converted factory district in the north — and find Manifesto Market for a similar experience in shipping containers.

*Cost: $12–18*

**Afternoon — The Žižkov Crawl**

After the market, take the tram to Žižkov, the alternative neighborhood marked by the TV Tower (with those bizarre crawling baby sculptures) and an almost comical density of dive bars. This is beer country. Find a hospoda with checkered tablecloths and order a half-liter of Kozel or Budvar — around 40 CZK ($1.75).

For lunch, hunt down a place serving old-school Czech food at old-school Czech prices. Many Žižkov pubs have simple menus: guláš (beef stew with dumplings), řízek (schnitzel), or smažák (the fried cheese again, because why not). The room might smell like cigarettes and regret, the waiter might not smile, and the meal will cost $8. This is Prague without the polish.

*Cost: $15–25*

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your final dinner should acknowledge that Prague has changed. The Czech food tradition is alive, yes, but a new generation of chefs is cooking with local ingredients and modern technique. In Vinohrady or Holešovice, find a restaurant that takes reservations (book ahead — weekends fill up).

Here, you might find svíčková reimagined as thinly sliced beef with a lighter sauce, or rabbit cooked in dark beer, or duck with red cabbage and potato rösti. The prices climb — expect 500–800 CZK ($22–35) for a main course — but the craft is evident in every bite. Pair with a Czech wine from Moravia (yes, Czech wine exists, and it's good) or end with a Becherovka, the herbal digestif that tastes like Christmas.

Leave full. Leave satisfied. Leave already planning the return.

*Cost: $50–90*

**Day 3 Total: Budget ~$80 | Mid-range ~$135 | Splurge ~$200**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Beer is food here.** Czech pilsner is world-class, cheaper than bottled water, and served at nearly every meal. Embrace it. A half-liter with lunch is not alcoholism — it's culture.

- **Dumplings come two ways.** Houskové knedlíky (bread dumplings) are for savory dishes; bramborové knedlíky (potato dumplings) often appear with sweeter preparations or stuffed with fruit. Know what you're ordering.

- **Skip the trdelník on Charles Bridge.** It's not a traditional Czech food (it's Hungarian-ish), and the tourist versions are stale. If you must try it, get one fresh from the Náplavka market or Manifesto.

- **Lunch is the value meal.** Many restaurants offer a polední menu (lunch menu) from 11 AM–2 PM with soup and a main course for 150–200 CZK ($7–9). This is how Czechs eat well for cheap.
