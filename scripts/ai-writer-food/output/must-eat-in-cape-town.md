The smoke finds you before the food does. I'm standing in a backyard in Woodstock, afternoon sun slanting through the grapevines overhead, and the smell of boerewors crackling over open coals has wrapped itself around my entire nervous system. Someone hands me a cold beer. Someone else passes a plate of thick-cut lamb chops, charred edges glistening. This is my third week eating through Cape Town, and I've learned that the city doesn't just feed you — it pulls up a chair, pours you wine from the Stellenbosch hills, and tells you stories until you forget what time zone you came from.

Cape Town's cuisine is a conversation between continents. Dutch settlers, Cape Malay spice traders, indigenous Khoisan knowledge, Indian influences that traveled up the coast from Durban, British colonialism, and the resilient creativity of people who made extraordinary food under impossible circumstances. Every dish here carries that weight and that joy. Here's what you need to eat.

#### Braai

Forget everything you know about backyard grilling. A braai is not a cooking method — it's a social contract. The fire gets lit hours before eating. The wood matters (grape vine cuttings if you're lucky, otherwise hardwood like rooikrans). And the meat hits the grill only when the coals have burned down to a shimmering white-gray heat that could forge iron.

The boerewors comes first — that long coiled sausage, beef and pork and coriander seed, fat rendering into the flames with a hiss that makes your mouth water against your will. You eat it with your hands, maybe rolled into a piece of bread with chakalaka (a spicy vegetable relish) and sweet-hot tomato sauce. Then the lamb chops, which in South Africa come thick-cut and marble-streaked, seasoned with nothing but salt and fire. The exterior chars to a crust while the interior stays pink and impossibly tender. I've had Michelin-starred steaks that didn't move me like a lamb chop eaten standing up, paper plate bending under the weight.

You'll find braai everywhere — it's the national pastime — but the best ones happen at someone's house. Barring that, hit the Mzoli's butchery in Gugulethu on a weekend afternoon, where you choose your cuts, they cook them over open drums, and the whole neighborhood becomes a party. Expect to pay R150–R250 ($8–$14) for more meat than you can finish.

#### Bobotie

If Cape Town had a national dish, this would be it — a Cape Malay curried meat bake that tastes like what would happen if shepherd's pie went on a spice route voyage and came back changed. The base is minced beef or lamb, cooked with onions, garlic, curry powder, turmeric, and a secret that makes it unlike any curry you've had: dried apricots and raisins, plus a splash of vinegar and a spoonful of apricot jam. Sweet, savory, tangy, warm — all at once.

But the magic is the topping. An egg-and-milk custard gets poured over the meat and baked until it sets into a golden, silky cap with brown spots from the oven's heat. You crack through that custard with your spoon and it mingles with the spiced meat below. Bay leaves stud the top, and you eat it with yellow rice studded with raisins and cinnamon.

Bobotie came from the Cape Malay community, descendants of enslaved people brought from Indonesia, Malaysia, and other parts of Asia by Dutch colonizers. They brought their spices and their genius and created something that exists nowhere else on earth. Find it at Bo-Kaap Kombuis in the colorful Bo-Kaap neighborhood, where the portions are generous and the recipe hasn't changed in generations. Around R95–R130 ($5–$7).

#### Cape Malay Curry

While we're in Bo-Kaap, let's talk about the curries that perfume the narrow streets between those famously painted houses. Cape Malay curry is gentler than its Indian cousins — fragrant rather than fiery, built on a foundation of coriander, cumin, turmeric, and cinnamon rather than raw chili heat. The sauce is often creamy, enriched with coconut milk or yogurt, and studded with soft dried apricots that burst with sweetness against the savory meat.

Chicken or lamb shoulder works best — something that can braise slowly until it surrenders to your fork. Slivered almonds add crunch on top, and the whole thing arrives with sambal (a chili condiment for those who want heat) and roti or rice. It's comfort food in the truest sense — the kind of dish that makes you understand why people traveled oceans for spices.

Look for it in the Bo-Kaap neighborhood or at Biesmiellah, a halal institution that's been serving Cape Malay food since 1988. Curries run R85–R140 ($5–$8), and you should order the samoosas to start.

#### Bunny Chow

The name makes no sense until you see it: a hollowed-out quarter loaf of white bread, filled to overflowing with curry, the bread lid balanced on top. This is bunny chow, and while it originated in Durban's Indian community, it's become beloved across South Africa. The story goes that Indian workers couldn't carry plates, so the bread became the vessel. The name might come from the Bania merchant caste who sold it, or it might be entirely apocryphal. Nobody agrees. Everybody eats.

The bread soaks up the curry from the inside while staying crusty on the outside, creating a textural journey from your first bite to your last. You eat with your hands — tear off pieces of the curry-soaked bread, scoop up the meat and sauce, repeat until you're wiping the inside clean. It's messy, satisfying, and absolutely not something you order on a first date.

In Cape Town, you'll find bunny chow at Eastern Food Bazaar in the city center, a chaotic food court where different stalls compete for your attention and your curry costs R45–R75 ($2.50–$4). Go for mutton if they have it.

#### Biltong

You'll see it hanging in shop windows like meat curtains — thick slabs of beef or game, air-dried and seasoned with coriander, salt, vinegar, and sometimes a hit of chili. This is biltong, South Africa's answer to jerky, except it's not really an answer because it's an entirely different question. Where jerky is smoked and often sweetened, biltong is cured and dried, leaving a texture that ranges from soft and chewy (wet biltong) to dry and crumbly (perfect for shaving over salads, which sounds wrong but trust me).

The flavor is concentrated beef, intensified by time and spice, with a slight tang from the vinegar cure. You can buy it sliced thin or in thick sticks, made from beef, kudu, springbok, or ostrich. It's the perfect hiking snack, the perfect road trip companion, the perfect 2 AM need-something-savory satisfaction.

Every supermarket and corner shop sells biltong, but for the good stuff, find a proper biltong shop or a butcher who cures their own. At the V&A Waterfront, Biltong & Co does custom slicing. Expect R200–R350 ($11–$19) per 500 grams, less for mass-market versions.

#### Snoek

This long, oily fish with teeth like a barracuda is Cape Town on a plate. Snoek season runs from late autumn through winter, and when the boats come in, the whole Cape Flats smells like the sea. The fish is traditionally smoked over smoldering oak chips until the flesh turns golden and pulls away in rich flakes. Locals eat it on fresh bread with apricot jam — a combination that sounds insane until the sweet-salt-smoke hits your palate and rewires your understanding of what belongs together.

Fresh snoek gets braaied whole or cooked into smoorsnoek (a flaked fish dish with potatoes, tomatoes, and chili). Either way, it's oily and assertive, the kind of fish that announces itself. Look for it at Kalky's in Kalk Bay, a harbor-side fish and chips shop where the snoek comes off boats you can see from your table. A smoked snoek pâté and bread costs around R65 ($3.50).

#### Malva Pudding

After all that meat and spice, you need something sweet that hugs you from the inside. Malva pudding is that hug. It's a sponge cake made with apricot jam, soaked in a hot cream-and-butter sauce the moment it comes out of the oven. The cake absorbs the sauce and becomes impossibly moist, almost sticky, sweet but balanced by a whisper of vinegar in the batter that keeps it from cloying.

The origins are disputed — Dutch, possibly, or Cape Malay, or some fusion lost to time. What matters is the spoonful: warm, dense, caramelized at the edges where the sauce pools and browns. It arrives with cold vanilla custard or ice cream, and the temperature contrast makes you close your eyes.

Every Cape Town restaurant with a "heritage" menu serves malva pudding, but I love the version at The Pot Luck Club in Woodstock, where it comes deconstructed with burnt butter ice cream. Traditional versions run R55–R85 ($3–$5).

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch in Cape Town is often the main event, especially on weekends when the braai fires start at noon and nobody's going anywhere. Dinner tends to be later — 7:30 or 8 PM — and booking matters at popular spots, particularly on Bree Street in the city center where the food scene clusters.

Sharing is the norm. Order more dishes than people and pass plates around the table. When someone invites you to a braai, bring drinks (beer or wine, not spirits) and don't offer to help cook unless you're willing to be gently mocked. The fire is sacred. Stand near it, glass in hand, and let the smoke get into your clothes.

Tipping runs 10–15%, and you pay at the table. Many restaurants don't take American Express. Load-shedding (scheduled power cuts) can affect kitchens, so check that your chosen spot has backup power on bad days.
