Three days in Quito with a single mission: to eat everything worth eating in this city perched 2,850 meters above sea level. The altitude affects your appetite (smaller portions feel bigger), your cooking times (everything takes longer to boil), and the way flavors hit—sharper, somehow, in the thin air. This itinerary is the one I built after making every wrong turn, eating too much in the wrong neighborhoods, and finally figuring out how to do it right.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Quiteños eat late—lunch doesn't really start until 1pm, and dinner rarely before 8pm. Tipping isn't mandatory but rounding up or leaving 10% is appreciated at sit-down restaurants. Cash is king in markets and smaller spots; cards work at mid-range and upscale places. The US dollar is Ecuador's currency, which simplifies everything. Book ahead for weekend dinners at popular restaurants—same-day reservations are often impossible.

## Day 1: Historic Center — The Foundation

**Morning: The Market Breakfast**

Start in the Old Town, where Quito's food story begins. Head to Mercado Central before 9am, when the stalls are fully stocked and the breakfast crowd is in full swing. Find a seat at one of the comedores (food stalls) on the upper level and order *llapingachos*—thick potato pancakes stuffed with cheese, griddle-fried until the edges crisp, served with a fried egg on top and a heap of curtido (pickled onion and tomato salad). The best versions here have a golden crust that shatters slightly when you cut in.

Wash it down with *morocho*, a thick, warm drink made from cracked corn and milk, spiced with cinnamon. It tastes like rice pudding in liquid form. The whole breakfast costs $3-4, and you'll be full for hours.

**Afternoon: Lunch in the Colonial Heart**

Walk the cobblestoned streets toward Plaza Grande, letting your appetite rebuild. By 1pm, duck into one of the traditional restaurants along García Moreno street for *seco de chivo*—goat stewed slowly in beer and naranjilla (a tart local fruit), served with yellow rice and a generous scoop of avocado. The meat should fall apart at the suggestion of a fork. In the Historic Center, you'll pay $8-12 for a full plate with a fresh juice included.

After lunch, explore the food shops around La Ronda, the restored colonial street now lined with small cafes. Stop at a *dulcería* (sweet shop) for *colaciones*—tiny candy-coated peanuts that have been made the same way for generations. A small bag costs $1-2 and keeps well for snacking later.

**Evening: Modern Quiteño Dinner**

End Day 1 with dinner in the same neighborhood but elevated. Several restaurants in the Old Town have taken traditional recipes and refined them without losing their soul. Look for a tasting menu featuring *ceviche de palmito* (hearts of palm ceviche), *locro de papa* (potato soup with cheese and avocado), and perhaps a contemporary take on *fritada*—crispy braised pork with hominy corn. A full dinner with wine runs $35-55 per person at these establishments; without alcohol, expect $25-35.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$45 | Mid-range ~$70 | Splurge ~$120

## Day 2: La Mariscal & North — The Diverse Table

**Morning: The Continental Crossroads**

La Mariscal, Quito's most cosmopolitan neighborhood, serves breakfast differently. Here you'll find cafes that wouldn't look out of place in Brooklyn, alongside traditional spots. Start at a corner bakery for *pan de yuca*—chewy cheese bread made from cassava flour, best eaten warm when they're still stretchy inside. Pair it with *café pasado*, Ecuador's answer to pour-over coffee, made by slow-dripping hot water through a cloth filter filled with grounds. Breakfast here runs $5-8.

**Afternoon: Market Culture and Street Eating**

Take a taxi north to the less-touristed Mercado Iñaquito (locals call it La Carolina market). This is where Quito's middle class shops and eats. The seafood section serves some of the city's best *encebollado*—a fish soup with yuca, pickled red onion, and enough chili to clear your sinuses. Traditionally a hangover cure, it's really just an excellent lunch any day. The broth is tomato-based, tangy, and deeply satisfying. $4-6 for a huge bowl with extra bread.

Walk through the produce section and buy fruit you've never seen: *naranjilla*, *taxo*, *babaco*. The vendors will cut them for you to taste. Spend $2-3 on whatever looks strange and wonderful.

If you want to learn rather than just eat, several cooking schools in this area offer afternoon classes focusing on Ecuadorian cuisine. A 3-hour class teaching you to make *ceviche*, *empanadas de verde*, and a main course runs $45-65 and includes eating everything you've made.

**Evening: The Hidden Neighborhood**

For dinner, head to La Floresta, the arts quarter east of La Mariscal. This neighborhood has quietly become Quito's most interesting dining district. Small restaurants here serve food that's creative but rooted—you might find *tigrillo* (mashed green plantains with egg and cheese, a coastal breakfast dish) reimagined as a dinner appetizer, or *corvina* prepared five different ways.

Look for restaurants tucked into converted houses with no obvious signage—ask locals or hotel staff for current favorites, as the scene shifts. Expect to spend $20-35 per person for a memorable meal, $50-70 if you're drinking natural wines (the city has developed a small but serious wine bar culture).

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$55 | Mid-range ~$85 | Splurge ~$160

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

**Morning: The Breakfast You'll Dream About**

Return to basics for your last morning. Find a *hueca*—a no-frills spot, often family-run, known for doing one or two things extremely well. In the City Center near Parque El Ejido, several *huecas* specialize in *bolón de verde*: a softball-sized mass of mashed green plantain stuffed with cheese or chicharrón (crispy pork), fried until the outside forms a shell. It's heavy, salty, perfect. Eat it with strong coffee and maybe a fresh juice. $4-6 total, and you won't need much for lunch.

**Afternoon: The Grand Market Tour**

Spend your final afternoon at Mercado de Santa Clara in La Mariscal—smaller than Central, less chaotic, and beloved by locals. This is grazing territory. Move from stall to stall: a cup of *caldo de gallina* (hen soup, deeply golden and restorative), a portion of *hornado* (whole roasted pig, served with mote—giant corn kernels—and *agrio*, a sharp onion-tomato sauce), a glass of fresh *naranjilla* juice.

Don't miss the fruit juice stands where they'll blend you anything: *tomate de árbol* (tree tomato), *mora* (blackberry), *guanábana*. Each glass costs $1-2. The *empanadas de viento*—"wind empanadas," puffed and crispy, filled with cheese, dusted with sugar—are $0.50 each and absolutely required.

Budget $15-20 for an afternoon of eating your way through the market.

**Evening: The Meal Worth Traveling For**

Your final dinner should be the meal you'll remember. Quito has several restaurants that have earned international recognition for interpreting Ecuadorian ingredients through contemporary technique. Book ahead—these places fill up.

The best of them serve multi-course menus ($65-95) that might include *chontacuro* (palm weevil larvae, if you're adventurous), Amazonian cacao in savory applications, high-altitude potatoes prepared four ways, and *cuy* (guinea pig) in forms both traditional and unexpected. Wine pairings add $35-50.

If you prefer something less formal but equally memorable, seek out a *parrillada* restaurant for grilled meats: chorizo, *lomo fino* (beef tenderloin), *chuleta* (pork chop), served with potatoes, corn, and multiple salsas. Share a spread between two or three people for $25-35 per person.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$55 | Mid-range ~$90 | Splurge ~$180

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Altitude affects everything.** You'll feel full faster and digest slower. Plan fewer, better meals rather than aggressive snacking. Drink water constantly.

- **Lunch is the main event.** Ecuadorians eat their biggest meal midday—restaurants are set up for this. You'll find better *almuerzos* (set lunches) and more energy in kitchens at 1pm than at 8pm.

- **Learn "una probadita."** At markets, asking for "a little taste" before buying is normal and expected. Point at the soup, say "una probadita," and you'll get a spoonful to try.

- **Carry small bills.** Market vendors and street food sellers often can't break a $20. Keep $1s and $5s ready, and you'll eat more smoothly.
