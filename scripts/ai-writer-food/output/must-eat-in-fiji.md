The coconut cream hits your tongue first—cool, rich, impossibly silky—before the lime cuts through with a brightness that makes your eyes widen. You're sitting cross-legged on a woven mat in a village on the Coral Coast, and the kokoda in the coconut shell bowl in front of you was swimming in the Pacific three hours ago. The grandmother who made it watches your face, waiting for the smile she knows is coming.

Fiji doesn't just feed you. It wraps you in a banana leaf, buries you in warm earth, and slow-cooks you until you understand what it means to eat in a place where the ocean is the pantry and the jungle is the spice rack.

---

#### Kokoda — The Dish That Tastes Like the South Pacific Itself

Every country with a coastline has its ceviche, but kokoda is what happens when the Pacific Islands perfect the form. Raw walu (Spanish mackerel) or mahi-mahi gets diced into glistening cubes, then "cooked" in fresh lime juice until the flesh turns from translucent to opaque white. But here's where Fiji diverges from Latin America: instead of draining the citrus, cooks fold in thick, hand-pressed coconut cream—lolo, they call it—transforming the acid into something almost decadent. Diced tomatoes, cucumbers, and a whisper of chili add crunch and warmth. Some versions include onion so sharp it makes your sinuses tingle; others stay pure and clean.

The texture is like velvet torn into soft pieces. The flavor is the ocean filtered through a tropical garden.

Kokoda exists because Fijians have always lived between reef and rainforest, and this dish marries both. The coconut palm gives its cream; the sea gives its fish; the lime tree behind the house gives its acid. No cooking required—just time and trust in the chemistry of citrus.

Find it at beachside resorts for FJ$25–40, but the real stuff comes from village women selling it at the Nadi Market for FJ$8–12, served in actual coconut shells. Saturday morning is when the fish is freshest.

#### Lovo — The Buried Treasure

Imagine this: a pit in the earth, lined with volcanic stones heated until they glow, layered with whole chickens, chunks of pork, reef fish wrapped in banana leaves, taro, cassava, and plantains. Then everything gets buried under palm fronds and earth, and you wait. Three hours. Four. However long it takes for smoke to become dinner.

When they unearth a lovo, the steam rises like the island itself is exhaling. The meat falls apart at the suggestion of a fork. The fat from the pork has melted into the taro, making the starchy root creamy and almost sweet. The fish—protected by its banana-leaf cocoon—stays impossibly moist, infused with an earthy smokiness you can't replicate in any kitchen.

This is how Fijians have cooked for ceremonies and celebrations for centuries. It's not food preparation; it's an event. The village gathers, the men tend the pit, the women prepare the accompaniments, and the children run circles until someone finally calls out that it's ready.

You'll find lovo at cultural villages along the Coral Coast (Robinson Crusoe Island does a theatrical one for FJ$150 including transfer and show), but the most memorable ones happen at family gatherings you might luck into if you stay in a village homestay. When someone invites you to a lovo, you cancel everything else.

#### Rourou — The Green That Needs No Meat

Taro leaves look like elephant ears, and when you cook them low and slow in coconut cream, they become something that exists between spinach and butter. Rourou is the Fijian side dish that makes you forget you ordered meat. The leaves break down into soft, dark-green ribbons, saturated with lolo until each bite slides across your palate with a richness that feels almost sinful for a vegetable.

The slight bitterness of the taro leaf plays against the sweetness of coconut, and if the cook adds a little onion and garlic—as many do—you get something that tastes like comfort in its purest form.

Taro is sacred in Fiji. The plant feeds the islands; its root becomes the starchy foundation of meals, and its leaves become rourou. Nothing is wasted.

Every Fijian restaurant serves it. Expect to pay FJ$5–10 for a side portion. At Mama's Pizza in Nadi (which, despite the name, serves phenomenal local food), ask for rourou with your curry and prepare to order a second bowl.

#### Fiji-Indian Curry — The Second Soul of Fijian Food

Walk into any small town in Fiji and you'll smell it: cumin sizzling in oil, turmeric staining the air yellow, the slow simmer of goat or chicken breaking down into gravy thick enough to coat the back of a spoon. Indo-Fijians make up nearly 40% of the population, descendants of laborers brought to work sugarcane fields in the 1800s, and their curries have become as Fijian as kokoda.

The curry here isn't like what you'll find in Delhi or Mumbai—it's adapted, evolved, made with local produce and decades of island influence. Mutton curry comes thick and oily, the meat soft as memory. Fish curry uses whatever came off the boat that morning. Vegetable curries overflow with eggplant, okra, and long beans. And always, always, there's roti—not naan, but the thin, griddle-cooked flatbread perfect for tearing and scooping.

Fiji-Indian restaurants cluster in Nadi and Suva, often humble storefronts with fluorescent lights and laminated menus. Singh's Curry House in Martintar serves plates for FJ$12–18 that will feed you twice. Go at lunch when the curries are freshest and the roti comes off the tawa hot.

#### Cassava Cake — The Sweet That Haunts Your Return Flight

It arrives looking unassuming—a golden-brown square, slightly jiggly, dusted with nothing. You bite in and your teeth sink through a caramelized top into something dense, sticky, and deeply coconutty. Cassava (tapioca) root gets grated, mixed with coconut cream and sugar, then baked until the edges crisp and the center stays almost pudding-like.

It's not overly sweet. That's what gets you. The coconut provides richness without cloying, and the cassava has a slightly chewy quality that keeps your jaw working happily. Some versions add a layer of caramelized coconut on top; others fold in pandan for a green tint and floral note.

You'll find cassava cake at bakeries, market stalls, and resort buffets across Fiji. At the Suva Municipal Market, vendors sell palm-sized squares for FJ$2–3. Buy two—you'll regret buying one.

---

#### Fresh Tropical Fruit — The Breakfast That Requires No Recipe

Papaya split open with a spoon, its salmon-pink flesh yielding seeds you scrape away to reveal the sweet, musky fruit beneath. Mango so ripe the juice runs down your wrist before it reaches your mouth. Pineapple that tastes nothing like what you've bought in supermarkets—here it's almost floral, the acid perfectly balanced. And coconut: young ones hacked open with a machete for drinking, mature ones grated for cream, the meat chewy and sweet.

Fiji's fruit doesn't travel well, which means you've never really tasted it. Here, it comes to you hours after it left the tree. This is the breakfast of every village, every resort, every roadside stand. A bowl of tropical fruit and a cup of tea, and you understand why nobody seems rushed.

Buy from women selling along the Kings Road—FJ$5 for a bag of mangoes, FJ$3 for a papaya, FJ$2 for a drinking coconut. Point, smile, pay, eat immediately.

### How to Eat Like a Local

Fijians eat together. Meals are shared, passed around, offered before taken. If you're invited to eat with a family, wait to be told where to sit and what to take first—your hosts will guide you. Eat with your right hand if utensils aren't offered; this isn't rude, it's traditional.

Meals happen on island time, which means breakfast can stretch until 10 a.m. and dinner might not appear until 8 p.m. Lunch is often the biggest meal, especially in villages. Markets are best early—by 7 a.m. the fish selection peaks, and by noon the day's cassava cakes are gone.

When someone offers you kava after a meal, accept. The muddy, peppery drink is an acquired taste, but sharing a bilo (cup) matters more than enjoying it. Clap once before drinking, drain it in one gulp, clap three times after. You're not a tourist anymore; you're a guest.
