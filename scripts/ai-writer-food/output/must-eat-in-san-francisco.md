The steam rises from the foil wrapper in your hands, and for a moment you're not sure where the burrito ends and the fog rolling down Mission Street begins. It's 11 a.m. on a Tuesday, and you're standing outside La Taqueria with a two-pound cylinder of carnitas, black beans, and salsa verde that cost you twelve dollars. This is breakfast. This is San Francisco.

I spent three weeks eating through this city built on impossible hills, and what I found wasn't a single cuisine — it was a collision of them. Gold Rush sourdough, Italian fisherman stews, Cantonese dim sum palaces, Irish coffee invented to warm tourists after a freezing ferry ride. San Francisco doesn't have one food identity. It has seven, layered on top of each other like the city's own geology.

Here's what you need to eat before you leave.

---

#### Mission Burrito

The Mission burrito isn't a burrito — it's an engineering project wrapped in foil. What separates it from every other burrito in America is the steamed flour tortilla, pliable as fabric, stretched around a precise architecture: rice first (controversial, but essential), then beans, then meat, then the fixings that make it yours. The foil isn't presentation — it's structural. Without it, the whole thing would collapse under its own ambition.

The first bite breaks through that steamed tortilla into warm, seasoned rice, and then you hit the carnitas — pork that's been braised until it surrenders, then crisped on a flattop until the edges turn almost candied. The salsa verde cuts through everything, tomatillo-bright and acidic enough to reset your palate before the next bite.

This burrito exists because of the Mission District's decades as a Mexican and Central American neighborhood, where taqueria culture developed its own San Francisco dialect. La Taqueria on Mission Street makes perhaps the purest version — they famously refuse to add rice, believing it dilutes the meat. El Farolito on 24th Street goes maximalist, piling everything including sour cream into a torpedo that requires two hands and zero dignity.

Go between 11 a.m. and 1 p.m. for the freshest tortillas. Expect to pay $12–$15 for a burrito that can legitimately serve as two meals.

#### Sourdough Bread

There's a reason San Francisco sourdough tastes like nothing else. The wild yeast strains native to this foggy peninsula — *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, named after the city itself — produce a tang that bakers elsewhere have spent decades trying to replicate. They can't. The microbes don't travel.

At Boudin Bakery on Fisherman's Wharf, they've been feeding the same sourdough starter since 1849. The bread comes out of the oven mahogany-crusted, with a crackle when you tear it that sounds like stepping on autumn leaves. The interior is a different creature entirely — chewy, almost elastic, with air pockets that catch butter and let it pool. The sourness isn't aggressive; it's background music, a gentle acidity that makes you want another piece before you've finished the first.

Buy a whole round loaf ($7–$10) and find a bench facing the bay. Tear pieces directly from the bread. The crust will scatter crumbs down your jacket, and you won't care.

#### Dungeness Crab

Dungeness season runs from November through June, and when it hits, the city loses its collective mind. The crabs arrive at Fisherman's Wharf still clicking their claws, and within hours they're tumbling into boiling water seasoned with bay leaves and lemon.

What comes out is sweet — genuinely sweet, not "seafood sweet" — with flesh that pulls from the shell in satisfying chunks. The body meat is richer, almost custard-like if the crab is fresh enough. The legs require work: cracking, picking, sucking the juice from the shells. This is not elegant eating. This is wearing a plastic bib at a wooden table overlooking the water, surrounded by tourists and locals who've stopped pretending there's a dignified way to do this.

The wharf stalls sell whole cracked crab for $25–$40 depending on size and season. Order it cold with a side of cocktail sauce, or hot with garlic butter that you'll be smelling on your fingers for hours. The best time is late morning, when the first crabs of the day hit the steam tables.

#### Cioppino

Italian fishermen invented cioppino in North Beach during the late 1800s, combining whatever the day's catch couldn't sell with tomatoes, wine, and garlic. It was poverty food that became luxury, a stew built on scraps that now costs $45 at white-tablecloth restaurants.

The best cioppino arrives in a cast-iron pot, the broth a brick-red from tomatoes cooked down to velvet. Swimming in it: Dungeness crab legs, clams still in their shells, mussels with beards intact, shrimp with their heads on staring at you, chunks of firm white fish that flake when your spoon touches them. The broth tastes like the ocean filtered through an Italian grandmother's kitchen — garlicky, slightly sweet from the tomato, with a wine-acid finish that begs for sourdough to soak it up.

Sotto Mare on Green Street serves an absurdly generous bowl, crab legs draped over the sides like the pot couldn't contain them. Cioppino's in North Beach is the old-school choice, red-checkered tablecloths and all. Budget $40–$60 per person with bread and wine.

#### Dim Sum

San Francisco's Chinatown is the oldest in North America, and the dim sum palaces here serve food that's survived five generations of aunties judging whether the har gow wrapper is translucent enough.

At Good Mong Kok Bakery on Stockton Street, the line starts before 8 a.m. The siu mai arrive still steaming, pork and shrimp dumplings with the meat practically bouncing inside the wrapper. The char siu bao — barbecue pork buns — split open to reveal a pocket of mahogany-glazed pork so sticky-sweet it could be dessert. Cheung fun, rolled rice noodle sheets filled with shrimp, arrive drenched in sweet soy sauce, slippery and substantial at once.

The larger dim sum halls — Hang Ah Tea Room, Lai Hong Lounge — do the full cart service experience, where aunties push carts between tables and you point at what you want. It's chaotic, it's loud, and it's the most honest way to eat. Point at the thing that looks good. Ask what's in it. Point at the next thing.

Dim sum runs $15–$25 per person if you pace yourself, which you won't. Go before 11 a.m. to beat the crowds.

---

#### Irish Coffee

The Buena Vista Cafe didn't invent Irish coffee — that was Shannon Airport in Ireland — but they perfected it in 1952, figuring out that lightly whipped cream, floated on top, would stay suspended instead of sinking. They've made over 40 million since.

The drink arrives in a small glass mug: hot coffee sweetened with two sugar cubes, a shot of Tullamore D.E.W. Irish whiskey, topped with cream so thick it looks like a cloud that got lost. The trick is drinking through the cream without stirring. The cold cream hits your lips first, then the hot coffee-whiskey mixture, and the temperature contrast is part of the point. The sweetness balances the whiskey burn; the cream smooths every edge.

Go late afternoon when the fog starts creeping back over the Marina. The counter seats face the bartenders making drinks in assembly-line fashion — pouring, stirring, floating cream — and watching them is half the experience. Each Irish coffee costs about $12, and you'll want two.

## How to Eat Like a Local

San Francisco eats early and often. Brunch culture here isn't Sunday-exclusive — it's a weekday religion, and dim sum is best before 11 a.m. when selection peaks. Dinner reservations at popular spots book two to three weeks out; use Resy or call directly.

Portions in the Mission are enormous. Order one burrito, split it, and still have room for churros from a street cart. At dim sum, order fewer things than you think — the dishes keep coming, and restraint early means room for the egg custard tarts later.

Tipping at restaurants runs 18–20 percent, calculated on the pre-tax total. At wharf stalls and bakeries, cash is often faster. Most importantly: dress in layers. You'll start the day in fleece, strip down to a t-shirt by noon, and need that fleece back by four o'clock when the fog returns and you're standing outside Bi-Rite Creamery waiting for honey lavender soft serve.
