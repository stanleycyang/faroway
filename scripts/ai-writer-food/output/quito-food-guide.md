# Quito Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The steam rises from a clay bowl of locro de papa at six in the morning, and you're standing in a market stall surrounded by women in embroidered shawls who've been cooking since before dawn. The soup is the color of sunshine, thick with cheese that strings from your spoon, and somewhere behind you a vendor is squeezing fresh naranjilla juice so aggressively tart it makes your eyes water. This is breakfast in Quito—and you haven't even found the good stuff yet.

## Why Quito's Food Deserves Your Full Attention

Quito sits at 2,850 meters above sea level, which means two things: you'll get winded walking uphill, and the food had to evolve to sustain people working at altitude. The result is a cuisine built on hearty, warming dishes that draw from both Indigenous Andean traditions and Spanish colonial influence—potatoes prepared seventeen different ways, slow-cooked meats, corn in forms you've never imagined, and soups that function as complete meals.

What makes eating here genuinely special is the geography. Within a few hours of the city, farmers grow everything from tropical fruit in the lowlands to quinoa in the highlands, and Quito's markets funnel all of it into one high-altitude capital. The food isn't trying to be modern or innovative—it's trying to be satisfying in the way your grandmother's cooking was satisfying, assuming your grandmother knew her way around a pig and had access to volcanic soil. The city hasn't been overtaken by international chains the way other South American capitals have, which means your best meal will almost always be cooked by someone who learned the recipe from their mother, who learned it from hers.

## The Dishes You Cannot Skip

**Hornado** is the dish that will ruin other roast pork for you forever. A whole pig, marinated in beer and spices, roasted until the skin shatters like glass and the meat falls apart at the suggestion of a fork. You'll find the best versions in Old Town, served with llapingachos (crispy potato cakes) and mote (giant corn kernels that pop between your teeth). The fat renders into something almost sweet, and the crispy skin makes a sound when you bite it that borders on inappropriate.

**Ceviche de camarón** arrives in a tall glass, pink with tomato and swimming with plump shrimp cured in lime and orange juice. It's sharper than Peruvian ceviche, more acidic, served with a mountain of popcorn for scooping and crunchy corn nuts called tostado. The citrus hits first, then a gentle heat from ají pepper builds at the back of your throat. Find it in the City Center, particularly near any market with a seafood section.

**Fritada** is pork fried in its own fat until the edges caramelize and the meat develops a crust that shatters against your teeth. It comes with the trinity of Ecuadorian sides: mote, llapingachos, and a curtido of pickled onions so pink they look artificial but are just stained with tomato juice. The Arts Quarter has cafeterías that serve this at lunch, packed with office workers who know exactly what they're doing.

**Seco de chivo** is goat stew, and before you wrinkle your nose, understand that the meat braises for hours in a sauce of naranjilla, beer, and cilantro until it's falling-apart tender with zero gaminess. The sauce is what you'll remember—tangy from the fruit, herbaceous, clinging to every shred of meat. Mop it with rice.

**Empanadas de viento** are "wind empanadas," named for how puffy they get when fried. Inside: just cheese, melting and stretching when you pull them apart. Outside: a dusting of sugar that sounds wrong until you taste the sweet-savory combination and order two more. Street vendors sell these in the Business District starting around 4 p.m., when the after-work crowd needs fuel.

## Street Food: Where the City Actually Eats

The real eating in Quito happens standing up or perched on plastic stools, and the markets are where you'll find it. Arrive hungry and early—the best stalls start running low by 1 p.m. Look for the longest lines of locals (not tourists, locals) and join them without knowing what you're ordering. The señora behind the counter will figure out what you need.

Night markets pop up in various neighborhoods, but the key hours are between 6 and 9 p.m. in the City Center. Watch for rolling carts selling salchipapas (french fries buried under sliced hot dogs and three kinds of sauce) and canguil (popcorn, served with ceviche or eaten alone as a snack).

Etiquette is simple: point at what you want if your Spanish fails, accept whatever salsa they hand you, and eat fast because seats turn over quickly. Tipping isn't expected at street stalls, but rounding up a few cents is appreciated. Bring small bills—nobody wants to break a $20 for your $2 breakfast.

## Where to Eat by Budget

**Under $25 (Budget):** This is where Quito shines brightest. For $5–8, you'll get an almuerzo (set lunch) that includes soup, a main course with meat and rice, juice, and sometimes a small dessert. Look for signs that say "almuerzo" in windows throughout Old Town—the more handwritten the sign, the better the food. These spots turn over fast at noon, so arrive by 12:15 or accept that the seco de pollo might be gone. At this price point, you're eating alongside taxi drivers, construction workers, and students, which is exactly where you want to be.

**$38–$48 (Mid-Range):** For a proper sit-down dinner with tablecloths and a menu you can contemplate, budget around $40 including a drink. Restaurants in the Arts Quarter specialize in elevated takes on traditional dishes—hornado with microgreens, ceviche with unexpected citrus, that sort of thing. The cooking is still Ecuadorian, just with better lighting and chairs that have backs. These spots take reservations, and you should make one on weekends.

**$60+ (Splurge):** High-end dining in Quito focuses on Amazonian and Andean ingredients prepared with technique—expect things like chontacuro (palm weevil larvae) if you're feeling brave, or tasting menus that take you through Ecuador's ecosystems course by course. The Business District has rooftop restaurants where you'll pay extra for the view, which is worth it exactly once. A proper splurge runs $80–120 with wine, and you should save it for a final-night celebration.

## Vegetarian and Vegan Realities

Let's be honest: traditional Quiteño food is built on pork fat and chicken stock. That said, you won't starve. Locro de papa can be made vegetarian if you ask (hold the chorizo), and many markets serve rice bowls with vegetables and avocado. Look for places advertising "comida vegetariana" in the Arts Quarter, where a small but genuine vegetarian scene has developed.

Vegan options require more intention. Llapingachos are often cooked in lard, so ask. Patacones (fried plantains) are usually safe, as is most fruit with chili and lime sold by street vendors. The Waterfront area has developed a handful of explicitly vegan restaurants catering to expats and health-conscious locals, with prices slightly higher than traditional spots. Don't expect the same depth of flavor—plant-based cooking here is newer and still finding its footing.

## Food Geography: Neighborhoods to Eat Your Way Through

**Old Town** is your ground zero for traditional cooking. The historic center's markets are where abuelas have been making the same recipes for decades. Come here for hornado, fritada, and locro early in the day before the tourist crowds swell.

**The City Center** concentrates seafood, oddly enough—cervicherías line certain blocks, all competing for the lunch crowd. This is also where you'll find the densest concentration of juice stands blending everything from mora (blackberry) to tomate de árbol (tree tomato).

**The Arts Quarter** skews younger and more experimental. Cafés here roast local coffee properly, and you'll find fusion spots trying things with quinoa and cacao. Evening crowds spill onto sidewalks, making it ideal for a progressive dinner across three or four spots.

For a market worth planning around, find the one in Old Town that operates daily but peaks on weekends. Three floors of produce, meat, prepared food, and heaving crowds of locals doing weekly shopping. Eat breakfast at the upstairs food stalls (the encebollado—fish soup for hangovers—is medicinal), then walk downstairs to buy fruit you've never seen before.

## How to Eat Like You Belong

Lunch is the main meal—2 p.m. is prime time, and restaurants fill fast. Dinner is lighter and later, often not until 8 p.m. Breakfast happens early and standing up, frequently consumed at market stalls by 7 a.m.

When dining at restaurants, wait to be seated even if the place looks casual. Greet your server respectfully with "buenas tardes" and don't snap your fingers unless you want to be ignored. Tipping runs around 10% at sit-down restaurants; service charges are sometimes included, so check the bill. At markets and street stalls, tipping isn't customary, but nobody will refuse a few extra coins.

Eat what's put in front of you. If a dish comes with a salsa you weren't expecting, try it—showing fear is worse than showing unfamiliarity. Compliment the cook, even through gestures and broken Spanish. The phrase "muy rico" (very delicious) will earn you seconds you didn't order.
