Miami hits different when you arrive hungry. Three days, one mission: eat through this city properly — from the Cuban coffee windows of Little Havana to the ceviche counters of the Waterfront, from 4 AM bakery runs to midnight stone crab claws. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Miami runs on its own schedule. Restaurants serve lunch from 11:30 AM to 3 PM, dinner rarely before 6 PM — but the ventanitas (walk-up coffee windows) open at dawn. Tip 18-20% at sit-down spots, a dollar or two at counters. Cards work almost everywhere, but carry cash for markets and food trucks. Reservations matter for dinner in the Arts Quarter and Waterfront neighborhoods, especially weekends. Book 2-3 days ahead for anywhere you've heard of.

## Day 1: Little Havana & The Heart of Cuban Miami

**Morning — Café con Leche and a Pastelito**

Start in the City Center's Cuban quarter before the heat sets in. Find a ventanita — those walk-up windows built into the sides of bakeries and restaurants — and order a café con leche with a guava pastelito. The coffee should arrive in a small plastic cup, sweet and strong enough to reset your entire nervous system. The pastelito, flaky and sticky with guava paste, costs almost nothing and tastes like the reason people moved here.

*Where*: City Center, near the main historic corridor
*What to order*: Café con leche ($2-3), guava and cheese pastelito ($1.50-2)
*Cost*: $5-8

**Afternoon — The Cubano That Ruins All Other Sandwiches**

Lunch is a Cuban sandwich, and you're going to be specific about it. The bread matters — it should be pressed until the crust shatters and the interior goes soft with butter and pork fat. Inside: roasted pork, ham, Swiss cheese, pickles, mustard. No lettuce. No tomato. This is not negotiable.

After lunch, walk the Old Town district and find the central market. Graze through the fruit stands — try mamey sapote if you've never had it, or a bag of cut mango with lime and chile salt. This is where locals shop, not perform for tourists.

*Where*: Old Town, then the central market
*What to order*: Cubano sandwich ($10-14), fresh tropical fruit ($4-6)
*Cost*: $18-25

**Evening — Lechón and the Classics**

Dinner is roasted pork, because Miami does this better than anywhere else in the continental U.S. Find a spot in the City Center that's been doing lechón for decades — the kind of place with fluorescent lights, paper napkins, and pork that's been slow-roasted until the skin crackles and the meat pulls apart with a fork. Order rice, black beans, and maduros (sweet plantains) on the side. A beer. Maybe flan for the table.

*Where*: City Center
*What to order*: Lechón asado with rice, beans, maduros ($22-32), flan ($6-8), beer ($5-7)
*Cost*: $35-50

**Day 1 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$60 · Mid ~$85 · Splurge ~$120

## Day 2: Waterfront Seafood & The Arts Quarter After Dark

**Morning — Venezuelan Arepas by the Water**

Shift neighborhoods. The Waterfront wakes up slower, so take your time with an arepa — that crispy-edged corn cake stuffed with anything from shredded beef to black beans and cheese. Find a Venezuelan spot with a line out the door. Order the reina pepiada (chicken and avocado) or the domino (black beans and white cheese). Pair it with fresh orange juice squeezed while you wait.

*Where*: Waterfront district
*What to order*: Arepa reina pepiada ($8-12), fresh juice ($4-6)
*Cost*: $15-22

**Afternoon — Ceviche, Markets, and a Cooking Class**

Lunch is ceviche, and the Waterfront delivers. Look for Peruvian spots doing leche de tigre properly — that citrus-chile marinade should wake up your whole face. Get the mixed seafood version with octopus, shrimp, and firm white fish.

If you want to go deeper, book a morning cooking class in the Arts Quarter. Several small operations teach you to make ceviche, empanadas, or Cuban mojo marinades in 2-3 hours. You'll leave with techniques that actually work at home.

Afterward, find the hidden cafe that locals swear by — there's always one tucked behind a gallery or down an unmarked alley in the Arts Quarter. Ask around. The best ones serve cortaditos and small bites without any signage.

*Where*: Waterfront for ceviche, Arts Quarter for exploration
*What to order*: Mixed ceviche ($16-24), cooking class ($60-90 optional), cortadito at hidden cafe ($3-5)
*Cost*: $25-40 without class · $85-130 with class

**Evening — Stone Crab and Splurge-Worthy Seafood**

This is your big dinner. The Arts Quarter has restaurants worth dressing up for, and stone crab season (October through May) is worth planning a trip around. If you're here in season, order claws by the pound — they arrive pre-cracked, cold, with mustard sauce. The meat is sweet, dense, and unlike any other crab you've had.

Off-season, pivot to whole grilled fish with mojo, or a Haitian-style red snapper if you can find it. End with tres leches cake, because Miami's version — soaked until it's almost pudding — deserves your attention.

*Where*: Arts Quarter
*What to order*: Stone crab claws or whole grilled fish ($45-85), sides ($12-18), tres leches ($10-14), drinks ($15-25)
*Cost*: $85-140

**Day 2 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$125 · Mid ~$165 · Splurge ~$290

## Day 3: The Greatest Hits & The Meal Worth Flying Home For

**Morning — The Breakfast That Locals Actually Eat**

Your last morning should feel like you live here. Head to a Business District diner — the kind of place with counter seating and a cook who's been working the same griddle for fifteen years. Order huevos rancheros or a full American breakfast with a side of tostones. Coffee refills are free and expected.

*Where*: Business District
*What to order*: Huevos rancheros or breakfast plate ($12-18), coffee ($3-4)
*Cost*: $18-25

**Afternoon — The Off-the-Beaten-Path Market and Haitian Griot**

This is grazing time. Find the off-the-beaten-path market in a local neighborhood — not the tourist-friendly one, the one where vendors are speaking Creole and Spanish and the produce looks like it came off a boat that morning.

Your lunch is griot — Haitian fried pork, marinated in citrus and scotch bonnet, then fried until the edges go crispy and dark. Eat it with pikliz (spicy pickled cabbage) and fried plantains. This is one of Miami's most underrated plates, hiding in plain sight.

Walk it off along the historic street that doubles as a photo spot. Buy a bag of something unfamiliar from a fruit vendor. Eat it on a bench.

*Where*: Local neighborhood market, then a walk through the historic center
*What to order*: Griot with pikliz and plantains ($12-16), market snacks ($5-10)
*Cost*: $20-30

**Evening — The Farewell Feast**

Your last meal should be the one you'll think about on the plane. Return to the Waterfront or find a rooftop in the Arts Quarter with a sunset view. This is where you order what Miami does best — maybe it's a return to stone crab, or a whole roasted fish for the table, or a tasting menu at somewhere you booked a week ago.

Get a cocktail made with local rum. Order dessert even if you're full. Tip well. Walk back along the water and remember why people build entire lives around eating in this city.

*Where*: Waterfront or Arts Quarter rooftop
*What to order*: Multi-course dinner or tasting menu ($65-150), cocktails ($14-20), dessert ($12-16)
*Cost*: $95-190

**Day 3 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$135 · Mid ~$180 · Splurge ~$250

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The ventanita rule**: If a Cuban coffee window has a crowd of older men arguing about baseball at 7 AM, the coffee is correct. Join them.
- **Lunch is the value play**: Many restaurants that charge $60+ for dinner offer the same kitchen's work for $18-25 at lunch. The Arts Quarter especially rewards afternoon visits.
- **Don't skip Haitian food**: Miami has one of the largest Haitian populations in the U.S. Griot, legume, and diri ak djon djon (mushroom rice) are essential eating — find them in the local neighborhoods, not the tourist zones.
- **Reservations by text**: Many smaller spots, especially in the Waterfront area, prefer text reservations over phone calls. Check their Instagram before you call.
