Three days in Medellín taught me that Paisas don't just eat — they gather, they linger, they make meals into events. The city's food scene sprawls from working-class comedores serving $3 bandejas to modernist tasting menus in El Poblado penthouses, and the best eating strategy is to hit both extremes and everything between. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Lunch is the main event here — expect restaurants packed from noon to 2pm and lighter evening meals. Most local spots are cash-only, so hit a casa de cambio (better rates than ATMs) and keep small bills. Tipping 10% is standard at sit-down restaurants but not expected at markets or street stalls. Reservations matter only at high-end spots on weekends. And please: it's Medellín, accent on the final syllable. Locals notice.

## Day 1: El Poblado & The Upscale Foundation

**Morning: The Arepa Start**

Begin in El Poblado, where bougainvillea spills over whitewashed walls and the coffee comes excellent. Skip the hotel breakfast and walk to any corner café for *arepa con hogao* — the corn flatbread topped with that slow-cooked tomato-onion sauce that tastes like someone's grandmother made it, because someone's grandmother probably did make the original recipe. Pair it with a *tinto* (black coffee) or a *café con leche*. This isn't breakfast to Instagram; it's breakfast to understand why Colombians eat arepas daily without getting bored. 

**Cost: $4–$7**

**Afternoon: Market Education & Proper Lunch**

Take the metro south to Sabaneta, a local neighborhood that most tourists skip entirely. This is where Medellín eats lunch when Medellín wants lunch done right. Find a family-run corrientazo spot (the term for a set lunch menu) and order *bandeja paisa* — the definitive Antioquian meal that arrives as a small mountain: red beans, white rice, crunchy chicharrón, a fried egg with runny yolk, sweet plantain, ground beef, chorizo, avocado, and an arepa on the side. It's absurd and perfect and costs what you'd pay for a sad airport sandwich back home. Eat slowly. You won't need a snack later.

After lunch, walk off the beans by exploring Sabaneta's small central plaza. Grab *mazamorra* from a street vendor — this corn-and-milk dessert drink is a childhood staple here, served cold in a plastic cup with a sprinkle of sugar. It tastes like nostalgia for a childhood you didn't have.

**Cost: $8–$14**

**Evening: El Poblado Polish**

Return to El Poblado for dinner as the valley lights begin flickering on. This neighborhood has the city's best upscale options, and tonight is about seeing what modern Colombian cooking can do. Look for restaurants doing elevated versions of traditional dishes — sancocho reimagined, ceviche with Amazonian fish, tasting menus built around indigenous ingredients. Order whatever the chef is proudest of, and pair it with a cocktail made from *aguardiente*, the anise-flavored firewater that's Colombia's national spirit. The good bars here know how to tame it into something sippable.

**Cost: $35–$65**

**Day 1 Total: Budget ~$50 | Mid-range ~$70 | Splurge ~$120**

## Day 2: Centro & Laureles — The Local Circuit

**Morning: Downtown Grit**

Today you eat where Medellín has always eaten. Head to Centro early, when the streets around Plaza Botero fill with vendors and office workers grabbing breakfast on the move. This is empanada territory — those fried pastries stuffed with seasoned potatoes and shredded meat, sold from metal carts for pocket change. A proper Medellín breakfast here is two empanadas, a cup of *avena* (oat drink), and watching the city wake up beneath Botero's rotund bronze sculptures. Don't linger in Centro after dark, but mornings here have an energy you won't find in the expat neighborhoods.

**Cost: $3–$5**

**Afternoon: La Paloma & Laureles Wandering**

Take the metro across the river to Laureles, the authentic neighborhood that long-term expats eventually discover. Before you explore, make a pilgrimage to La Paloma Restaurante — a legendary family-run spot open since 1952 that serves what might be the city's best *sancocho*. This hearty chicken and root vegetable stew arrives in a bowl big enough to swim in, the broth golden and restorative, the yuca falling apart at a glance. The dining room hasn't changed much in decades. Neither has the recipe.

Spend the afternoon walking Laureles' tree-lined streets, stopping for fresh juice at a corner tienda and browsing the small bakeries turning out *pandebonos* (cheese bread) and *buñuelos* (fried cheese balls). This is a neighborhood for grazing.

**Cost: $12–$20**

**Evening: Parque El Periodista & Dinner**

As evening arrives, find your way to Parque El Periodista, where Laureles' bar scene comes alive without the tourist markup of El Poblado. The surrounding streets have affordable restaurants doing honest Antioquian cooking — look for places packed with Colombian families on a weeknight. Order a *sudado* (fish or chicken stewed in tomato sauce) or grilled trout from the nearby rivers, plus a cold Club Colombia beer. If you're feeling adventurous, end the night with a shot of straight aguardiente. The Paisas at the next table will approve.

**Cost: $18–$35**

**Day 2 Total: Budget ~$35 | Mid-range ~$55 | Splurge ~$90**

## Day 3: Envigado & The Farewell Feast

**Morning: The Quiet Suburb**

For your final morning, metro south to Envigado, the quieter suburb where digital nomads eventually settle and locals escape weekend crowds. Breakfast here is unhurried — find a corner café and order *calentado*, yesterday's rice and beans refried with egg and hogao on top. It's peasant food elevated by time and technique, the kind of dish that makes you wonder why you've been overcomplicating breakfast your whole life. Sip your coffee slowly. The pace here allows it.

**Cost: $5–$9**

**Afternoon: Market Snacking & Final Empanadas**

Head back toward the city center for one last market session. On Sundays, Mercado de San Alejo draws locals for vintage finds and handcrafts, but any day you can find fresh fruit stalls selling *lulo* juice (tart, citrusy, addictive) and vendors grilling *chorizos* over charcoal. This afternoon is about the street food you haven't tried yet — perhaps *morcilla* (blood sausage) if you're brave, or *patacones* (fried green plantain) with hogao if you want comfort. Buy a bag of tropical fruit to take back to your hotel: granadilla, maracuyá, or whatever looks ripest.

**Cost: $8–$15**

**Evening: The Meal Worth Ending On**

Your final dinner should be the one you'll remember. Return to El Poblado or splurge on a tasting menu at one of the restaurants redefining Colombian cuisine — places where chefs are foraging Amazonian ingredients and aging beef in coffee husks. Or, if fine dining isn't your farewell style, book a tango show with dinner (Medellín has one of the best tango cultures outside Buenos Aires) and eat while watching dancers move like they're arguing about something beautiful. Either way, end with a final aguardiente toast. You've earned it.

**Cost: $45–$85**

**Day 3 Total: Budget ~$60 | Mid-range ~$85 | Splurge ~$140**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Lunch is king.** Colombians eat their biggest meal midday, and restaurants price accordingly — a $25 dinner might be a $10 lunch special at the same spot. Plan your splurges for evening and your best local food for afternoon.

- **Learn "corrientazo."** This word means "set lunch" and it's how most working Medellín eats — soup, main, rice, beans, juice, and sometimes dessert for $3–$6. Point at what the locals are ordering and say "lo mismo."

- **Greet everyone.** Paisas are warm and expect it back. A simple "buenas" when you walk into any restaurant, market stall, or café goes a long way. Learning "muy rico" (delicious) will make grandmothers smile.

- **Cash at markets, card at restaurants.** Upscale El Poblado spots take credit cards fine, but anywhere with plastic stools instead of chairs probably doesn't. Keep 50,000-peso notes broken into smaller bills.
