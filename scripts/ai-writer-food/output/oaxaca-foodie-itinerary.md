Three days in Oaxaca is barely enough time to scratch the surface of Mexico's most essential food destination — but if you eat with intention, skip the tourist traps, and follow the smoke rising from comals across the city, you can taste the full range of what makes this place extraordinary. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I arrived, stomach empty and notebook ready.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Oaxacans eat late — breakfast around 9-10am, lunch (the main meal) between 2-4pm, dinner after 8pm. Markets close by late afternoon, so plan accordingly. Cash dominates everywhere except upscale restaurants; ATMs are reliable and metered taxis are cheap. Tipping 10-15% is standard at sit-down restaurants. For any cooking class or serious restaurant dinner, book 2-3 days ahead — Oaxaca's food scene is smaller than you'd expect, and the best spots fill fast.

## Day 1: El Centro — Markets, Mole, and Finding Your Bearings

### Morning

Start at Mercado Benito Juárez around 9am, when the market is fully awake but not yet crowded. Find a stall selling **memelas** — thick oval masa cakes pressed by hand, charred on a comal, and topped with black beans and a snowdrift of Oaxacan cheese that stretches when you pull it apart. The cheese here is made fresh that morning, probably within the hour. Pair it with a cup of **tejate**, the ancient Zapotec drink of ground maize and cacao served cold in a painted gourd bowl. The foam on top tastes like nothing you've ever had — floral, chocolatey, utterly alien to a gringo palate. Don't overthink it. Just drink.

At the market's southeast corner, buy a small bag of **chapulines** from one of the women selling them by the scoop. The toasted grasshoppers come in varying sizes and heat levels — ask for *pequeños con limón* (small, with lime) for your first time. They taste more like crispy chili than insect. Eat them now, as a snack, not a performance.

**Cost: 80-120 MXN (~$5-7)**

### Afternoon

Walk the ten minutes to **Mercado 20 de Noviembre** for lunch — this is the carnivore's paradise. Head to the smoky back section where the grills are literally built into the floor. Buy your meat raw from one vendor (tasajo, cecina, chorizo), then pay a few pesos to have it grilled on the communal fires while you sit at a plastic table. The women running the stalls will bring you fresh tortillas, salsa, and grilled onions. This is lunch as theater, except the theater is 500 years old.

After eating, walk north through **El Centro's** colonial streets toward the Zócalo. Stop at one of the chocolate mills on Calle Mina (Mayordomo is the famous one, but any will do) and watch them grind cacao with sugar and cinnamon into the paste that becomes Oaxacan hot chocolate. Buy 100 grams of chocolate de agua — you'll want it later.

**Cost: Lunch 100-150 MXN (~$6-9) · Chocolate 40-60 MXN (~$2-4)**

### Evening

For your first proper mole dinner, head to **La Olla** in El Centro — a family-run restaurant with a rooftop terrace overlooking the city. Order the **mole negro** over chicken. This is the dish Oaxaca is famous for, and it should ruin you for every other mole you'll ever eat. The sauce is almost black, made from over 30 ingredients including multiple dried chilies, chocolate, burned tortillas, and spices that take days to prepare. It's savory, slightly sweet, impossibly deep. Have it with a copa of young mezcal to cut through the richness.

**Cost: 250-400 MXN (~$15-24) with drinks**

**Day 1 total food spend:** Budget ~$28 (460 MXN) · Mid-range ~$40 (660 MXN) · Splurge ~$80 (1,320 MXN)

## Day 2: Jalatlaco and the Mezcal Route

### Morning

Walk to **Jalatlaco**, the Instagram-famous neighborhood that earns its reputation at breakfast time, when locals are buying stacks of fresh tortillas and tourists are still asleep. Find one of the small fondas serving **enfrijoladas** — soft tortillas bathed in pureed black beans, topped with crumbled cheese and crema. It's the perfect recovery meal if last night's mezcal hit harder than expected.

The streets here are painted in fading murals and lined with bougainvillea. Walk slowly. This is the most beautiful neighborhood in Oaxaca, and it's worth lingering before the day heats up.

**Cost: 60-100 MXN (~$4-6)**

### Afternoon

This is the day to leave the city. Take a colectivo (30 MXN) from the second-class bus station toward **San Marcos Tlapazola** and the family palenques (distilleries) scattered through the valley. These aren't fancy tourist operations — they're backyards where three generations have been producing mezcal the same way since before your grandparents were born. You'll taste espadín, tobalá, and whatever else is resting in clay pots, straight from the source.

Most tours include a late lunch of Oaxacan food prepared by the family — often **tasajo** (dried beef) with fresh tortillas and beans. If you're arranging your own visit, negotiate lunch as part of the experience. This is not the time to be shy.

**Cost: Transport 60 MXN (~$4) · Tour with lunch 300-500 MXN (~$18-30)**

### Evening

Return to the city for dinner at **El Destilado** in El Centro — a rooftop restaurant where the kitchen treats Oaxacan ingredients with modern precision but doesn't lose the soul. Order the tasting menu if you want to spend, or go à la carte with whatever local ingredient they're featuring. The mezcal cocktails here are excellent, but after an afternoon at the palenques, you might just want an Oaxacan craft beer.

**Cost: 400-800 MXN (~$24-48)**

**Day 2 total food spend:** Budget ~$50 (820 MXN) · Mid-range ~$75 (1,240 MXN) · Splurge ~$150 (2,480 MXN)

## Day 3: Xochimilco, the Friday Market, and the Final Feast

### Morning

If you're here on a Friday, start at **El Pochote organic market** — the gathering place for Oaxaca's best small producers, with zero tourist crowds. You'll find heirloom corn varieties, wild herbs, fresh-pressed juices, and prepared foods that put most restaurants to shame. This is where local chefs come to source ingredients. Eat your way through: a tamal here, a fresh-made salsa there, fruit you can't name.

If it's not Friday, walk to **Barrio del Xochimilco** at dawn and find a tortillería where locals are buying their morning stacks. Order a few hot off the comal and eat them with nothing but salt. This is the foundation of everything else you've eaten — and it deserves a moment of attention.

**Cost: 80-150 MXN (~$5-9)**

### Afternoon

This is your afternoon to eat the **tlayuda** properly. Head to one of the street vendors in El Centro or the edges of Xochimilco — not a restaurant, not a café, but a woman with a barrel grill who's been making tlayudas for decades. The giant tortilla should be lightly charred, spread with asiento (unrefined pork fat), black beans, Oaxacan string cheese, and whatever meat you choose (tasajo is traditional). It's the Oaxacan pizza, except better than any pizza, and it costs a fraction of what you'd pay at a sit-down spot.

After, walk off the calories through the **Calle García Vigil mural walk**, where local artists have covered every surface for several blocks. This is Oaxaca's creative soul, unpolished and real.

**Cost: 60-100 MXN (~$4-6)**

### Evening

End your three days at **Casa Oaxaca**, or if you want something less famous, **La Biznaga** in its beautiful courtyard setting. This is the meal worth splurging on — order the tasting menu that moves through multiple moles, or construct your own progression from appetizers (chapulines with guacamole, tetelas stuffed with beans) through a main of mole amarillo or coloradito. Finish with mezcal, because there's no better way to say goodbye.

**Cost: 500-1,200 MXN (~$30-72)**

**Day 3 total food spend:** Budget ~$39 (640 MXN) · Mid-range ~$55 (910 MXN) · Splurge ~$120 (1,980 MXN)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **The market price and the restaurant price are different worlds.** A tlayuda costs 50-80 MXN from a street vendor and 200-300 MXN at a tourist café. The street version is often better.

- **Mezcal at a proper mezcalería has nothing in common with the stuff you've had at bars back home.** Start at La Mezcaloteca if you want an education — they'll walk you through producers and agave varieties like a sommelier, and you'll never drink cheap mezcal again.

- **Take a cooking class early in your trip, not at the end.** You'll learn about ingredients you've never seen — hierba santa, pitiona, chepiche — and suddenly every market becomes legible.

- **Chapulines are a 2,000-year-old Zapotec snack, not a tourist gimmick.** Eat them because they're delicious, not because they're weird.
