Three days in Vancouver taught me this: the city eats like it lives — caught between Pacific waters and mountain peaks, shaped by Asian immigration and Indigenous tradition, casual on the surface but obsessively quality-driven underneath. I arrived with a notebook and left with stained pages, a stretched waistband, and opinions about which dim sum house steams their har gow to the exact moment of translucent perfection. Here's everything I wish someone had told me before I started.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Restaurants tip 15–20%, and it's expected — servers notice when you don't. Most places take cards, but a few Chinatown cash-only spots still exist. Dinner reservations matter at popular spots, especially Friday and Saturday. Weekend dim sum means lines by 10:30 AM; go weekday if possible. Vancouver eats late by Canadian standards — dinner reservations at 8 PM are normal. And yes, tip on the pre-tax amount if you're watching pennies, but tip.

## Day 1: Gastown & Downtown — The Introductions

### Morning: Start Where the City Started

Begin in Gastown, Vancouver's oldest neighborhood, where cobblestones and exposed brick set the scene for your first real Vancouver breakfast. Walk past the Steam Clock (it'll whistle at you every fifteen minutes) and find a seat at a brunch spot on Water Street. Order the smoked salmon bennies — this is a city built on Pacific salmon, and you should meet it immediately. The fish should be silky, lightly cured, draped over eggs with the yolks still running.

Pair it with good coffee. Vancouver's third-wave scene is serious, and you'll find pour-overs and cortados that rival any North American city. Budget $18–25 for brunch with coffee; splurge spots push $35 with fresh-squeezed juice.

**Cost:** $20–$35

### Afternoon: The Market & The Dog

Take the False Creek ferry or walk across to Granville Island Public Market. This is not a tourist trap — locals shop here, which is why the produce gleams and the fishmongers will tell you exactly which inlet their spot prawns came from. Don't eat a full lunch yet. Graze.

Hit the charcuterie counter for a few slices of duck prosciutto. Find the bakery stalls and grab a fresh cheese bun. Get oysters shucked to order at the seafood counter — $3–4 each, briny and cold, eaten standing while ferries churn past the window.

Then, and this is non-negotiable, track down a Japadog. These Japanese-style hot dogs with teriyaki sauce, seaweed flakes, and Japanese mayo sound gimmicky and taste transcendent. The Terimayo is the classic — sweet soy glaze, crispy onion strings, creamy Kewpie. You can find carts downtown near the art gallery or a stand on Robson Street. $8–10 for what might be the city's most satisfying cheap meal.

**Cost:** $25–$40

### Evening: Dinner in Yaletown

Cross into Yaletown as the sun drops. This converted warehouse district has become Vancouver's upscale dining room — exposed beams, reclaimed wood tables, and serious wine lists. Book ahead for dinner at one of the seafood-focused restaurants along Hamilton or Mainland streets.

Order the wild Pacific salmon, but ask how they're preparing it. Grilled with a miso glaze is common and usually excellent. If spot prawns are in season (May through June), get them — they should still be twitching when they arrive, and they taste like the ocean concentrated into something sweet.

For dessert, ask if they have a Nanaimo bar variation. This layered chocolate-custard-coconut square is a BC institution, and better restaurants have started doing elevated versions with house-made components. A proper dinner here runs $65–120 depending on wine choices.

**Cost:** $65–$120

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$110 | Mid ~$150 | Splurge ~$195

## Day 2: Chinatown & Richmond — The Reason You Came

### Morning: Dim Sum, Done Right

This is the day you've been waiting for. Vancouver's Cantonese food scene is arguably the best outside Hong Kong, and dim sum is where it shows. Head to Chinatown early — by 9:30 AM if possible — or venture to Richmond on the Canada Line for even more options.

Get a table and start pointing. Har gow (shrimp dumplings) are the test: the wrapper should be thin enough to see pink through, with exactly seven pleats. Siu mai (pork and shrimp dumplings) should be bouncy, not dense. Char siu bao (BBQ pork buns) — both baked and steamed — should hit the table hot. Add cheung fun (rice noodle rolls), turnip cakes, and chicken feet if you're feeling brave.

The rhythm matters: start with steamed, move to fried, finish with sweets like egg tarts. A serious dim sum session runs 8–12 dishes for two people and costs $30–50 per person at the good places. Don't rush. This is the main event.

**Cost:** $30–$50

### Afternoon: Chinatown Wander & the Hidden Garden

Walk off the dumplings through Chinatown's narrow streets. Stop at a Chinese bakery for a pineapple bun (it contains no pineapple — the name comes from the crackly topping pattern) and a cup of Hong Kong-style milk tea, thick with evaporated milk. Both together: under $6.

Then find the Dr. Sun Yat-Sen Classical Chinese Garden, tucked behind Chinatown's main drag. This Ming Dynasty-style garden is the most peaceful spot in the city — koi ponds, covered walkways, scholar's rocks arranged with intention. Admission is around $15, and it's worth every penny as a digestion break before round two.

For a late afternoon snack, find a BBQ shop with lacquered ducks hanging in the window. Get a small portion of roast duck or char siu (BBQ pork) over rice. $12–15, and the pork fat should be caramelized until it shatters slightly at the edges.

**Cost:** $25–$40

### Evening: Modern Asian in False Creek

For dinner, shift registers. Book at a contemporary Asian restaurant around Main Street or the Olympic Village area — somewhere doing creative work with the Cantonese and Japanese techniques that define Vancouver cooking. Expect tasting menus or shareable plates: maybe raw scallop with yuzu kosho, crispy pork belly with black vinegar, or a whole grilled fish with Sichuan peppercorn.

Omakase sushi is also an option — Vancouver has the best sushi outside Japan, and a proper omakase runs $80–180 depending on how many courses and how rare the fish. The salmon should be wild, the tuna should be flown in, and the rice should be warm and seasoned just so.

**Cost:** $80–$160

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$135 | Mid ~$180 | Splurge ~$250

## Day 3: Kitsilano & Commercial Drive — The Farewell Feast

### Morning: Beach Brunch in Kits

Your last morning belongs to Kitsilano, the beach neighborhood where yoga pants are business casual and everyone seems to have just finished a run. Find a seat at a café with mountain views and order something healthy to offset yesterday's pork fat intake — a grain bowl with poached eggs, avocado toast with local microgreens, or house-made granola with thick yogurt.

Or don't. Get the full Canadian breakfast: eggs, back bacon, toast, and a side of poutine because you're leaving tomorrow and regret nothing. The Quebec classic — fries, cheese curds, gravy — shows up everywhere in Vancouver, and breakfast poutine with a runny egg on top is a legitimate life choice.

Kitsilano brunch runs $20–35, and the coffee is uniformly good. Sit outside if the weather cooperates. The North Shore mountains across English Bay look better with caffeine in your system.

**Cost:** $20–$35

### Afternoon: Commercial Drive & Final Snacking

Take a bus or bike to Commercial Drive, the multicultural strip where Italian nonnas shop next to Ethiopian families and everyone seems to know each other. This is Vancouver's most democratic eating street — no dress codes, no pretension, just good food at real prices.

Start at an Italian café for espresso and a sfogliatella (shell-shaped pastry, ricotta filling, shattering layers). Walk south, stopping for Portuguese custard tarts, Salvadoran pupusas, or Vietnamese banh mi depending on what catches your nose.

For a late lunch, find a taqueria or Middle Eastern spot. A lamb shawarma plate with all the pickles and sauces, or fish tacos with proper cabbage slaw. This is where Vancouver's immigrant communities feed themselves, which means the portions are generous and the flavors aren't softened for tourist palates. $12–18 fills you completely.

**Cost:** $25–$40

### Evening: The Last Great Meal

Your final dinner should remind you why you came. Return to Gastown or Yaletown for a proper West Coast tasting menu — the kind of place where the server explains that the halibut was caught yesterday near Haida Gwaii and the vegetables came from a specific farm in the Fraser Valley.

Order the tasting menu if they offer one. Let the kitchen show off. Expect courses like smoked sablefish with dashi, venison with foraged mushrooms, or a butter-poached prawn that tastes like a concentrated ocean sunset. The wine pairings will feature BC wines you've never heard of from the Okanagan Valley — say yes.

Dessert should involve something local: huckleberries, Douglas fir tips, or a final Nanaimo bar reimagined. A meal like this runs $120–200 with wine, and it's worth it. This is Vancouver cooking at its peak — technical skill applied to extraordinary ingredients, served without fuss.

**Cost:** $120–$200

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$165 | Mid ~$220 | Splurge ~$275

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Dim sum timing matters.** Weekday mornings at 9:30 AM mean no wait and dishes coming fresh from the kitchen. Saturday at 11 AM means a 45-minute line and lukewarm har gow. Plan accordingly.

- **Ask about the salmon.** Wild Pacific salmon is seasonal (roughly May through October). Off-season, some restaurants serve farmed Atlantic salmon without mentioning it. Ask, and choose spots that are honest about sourcing.

- **Chinatown is changing.** Gentrification has claimed some of the old-school spots. Richmond, accessible via the Canada Line, now has deeper options. If you have time, the 25-minute train ride pays off in dumpling dividends.

- **Book the splurge dinner early.** Vancouver's best restaurants fill up 2–3 weeks ahead for weekend reservations. If you know your travel dates, book before you land.
