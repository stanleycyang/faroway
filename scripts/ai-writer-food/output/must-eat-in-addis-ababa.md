The first bite catches you off guard. You're sitting on a low wooden stool in a cramped Piazza restaurant at 7 AM, and a woman has just torn a piece of *injera* and placed it directly in your mouth — her fingers still warm from the food. This is *gursha*, the Ethiopian act of feeding someone with your hands, an offering of love and respect that makes the spiced lentils taste like more than just breakfast. This is how Addis Ababa feeds you: intimately, generously, and with flavors that rewire your understanding of what food can be.

#### Injera with Doro Wot

Let's start with the dish that defines Ethiopian cuisine, the one you'll eat a dozen times and never tire of. *Doro wot* is a chicken stew built on a foundation of caramelized onions — and when I say foundation, I mean someone has stood over a pot for an hour or more, coaxing those onions into a deep, almost burgundy sweetness before the *berbere* spice blend even enters the picture. The chicken pieces emerge tender, saturated with heat that builds slowly from somewhere behind your tongue before spreading across your whole palate. And nestled in every serving: hard-boiled eggs that have absorbed the crimson oil until they're stained through.

But the stew is only half the experience. The *injera* beneath it — that spongy, slightly sour flatbread made from teff flour — acts as your plate, your utensil, and your carbohydrate all at once. Its tangy fermentation balances the richness of the wot, and the way it tears in your hands, pliable and slightly cool against the hot stew, creates a textural conversation that forks and spoons simply cannot replicate.

This is Sunday food, celebration food. Families crowd into traditional restaurants called *yebunna bets* across the Bole and Kazanchis neighborhoods, sharing massive platters after church. Expect to pay $5–8 for a full portion that will defeat you.

#### Kitfo

If *doro wot* is the soul of Ethiopian cooking, *kitfo* is its dangerous, thrilling heart. This is raw beef — finely minced, almost fluffy in texture — mixed with *mitmita* (a fiercer, more aromatic cousin of berbere) and *niter kibbeh*, a spiced clarified butter that coats each particle of meat in warm, herbed fat. When it arrives at your table, often on a clay plate still warm from the kitchen, the butter is still liquid, pooling around the crimson mound like a moat.

You eat it with more injera, of course, scooping the cool meat with the sour bread, the contrast of temperatures and textures creating something almost electric. Some restaurants offer it *leb leb* (lightly warmed) or fully cooked, but the traditional preparation is raw, and that's how the old neighborhoods around Merkato prefer it.

*Kitfo* traces back to the Gurage people of southern Ethiopia, who developed it as celebratory food, a dish of abundance served at weddings and holidays. In Addis, it's become an any-night indulgence. The best versions I found were in small, no-frills spots around the Gurage community areas near Megenagna — $4–6 gets you enough to share with a friend who trusts you.

#### Tibs

When you need something sizzling, something that arrives at your table still crackling and popping, *tibs* answers. This is sautéed meat — usually beef or lamb — cooked fast and hot with onions, peppers, rosemary, and garlic until everything caramelizes at the edges while staying juicy within. The smell hits you before the plate does: char and herbs and that unmistakable scent of good meat meeting high heat.

There are variations everywhere. *Derek tibs* comes dry and well-done, almost crispy. *Lega tibs* stays rare, the meat still blushing pink in the center. Some versions arrive in a clay pot with hot coals underneath, keeping everything warm while you work through it. I became obsessed with a lamb version in a small restaurant off Bole Road where the cook would finish each order with a dramatic sizzle of rosemary-infused butter.

This is late-night food, post-drinks food, the thing you order at 11 PM when the day has earned a reward. Budget $4–7 depending on the cut.

#### Shiro

For all the meat-centric glory of Ethiopian cuisine, the dish I craved most often cost less than a dollar. *Shiro* is a humble stew of ground chickpeas or fava beans, simmered with onions, garlic, and berbere until it becomes thick, creamy, and impossibly satisfying. The texture sits somewhere between hummus and gravy, smooth enough to coat the injera but substantial enough to feel like a real meal.

During fasting periods — and Ethiopian Orthodox Christians observe over 200 fasting days per year, abstaining from animal products — *shiro* becomes the centerpiece of the table. But even on non-fasting days, it appears as part of the *beyaynetu*, the combination platter of vegetarian dishes that demonstrates just how unnecessary meat can be.

The best *shiro* I had was from a window-front stall near Merkato's spice market, where a woman ladled it from a giant pot into injera wraps for morning commuters. Seventy cents. Transformative.

#### Beyaynetu

Speaking of that vegetarian platter: the *beyaynetu* deserves its own moment. This is a rainbow of stewed vegetables and legumes arranged on a single large injera — golden lentils (*misir wot*), collard greens (*gomen*), spiced beets, yellow split peas (*kik alicha*), cabbage cooked with carrots and turmeric, and always that anchoring shiro in the center.

Every mound offers something different. You tear injera and grab a bit of this, a bit of that, building each bite according to your mood. Earthy lentils with sharp gomen. Sweet beets against the heat of the red misir. It's a choose-your-own-adventure of flavor, and it proves that vegetarian food, done right, needs no apologies.

Wednesday and Friday mornings are peak *beyaynetu* time — fasting days when local restaurants prepare their most elaborate vegetarian spreads. Find one near the Selassie Cathedral area and expect to pay $3–5 for more food than is reasonable.

#### Genfo

Breakfast in Addis can surprise you. *Genfo* appears as a pale mound of cooked barley or wheat flour, smooth and dense, shaped into a volcano-like dome with a crater in the center. That crater holds a pool of melted *niter kibbeh* mixed with berbere — a crimson lake of spiced butter that you scoop up with each finger-pinch of the bland dough.

The contrast is the point. The genfo itself tastes of almost nothing, a neutral carrier, but that spiced butter transforms every bite into something warming and complex. It's stick-to-your-ribs breakfast food, the kind that fuels taxi drivers and construction workers through a long morning. Street stalls near the main transit hubs serve it starting around 6 AM for under $1.

#### Firfir

What happens to yesterday's injera? It becomes *firfir* — torn pieces of the flatbread sautéed with berbere, onions, and either butter or oil until they absorb all that flavor while staying slightly chewy. Add scrambled eggs and it becomes *enkulal firfir*, a breakfast dish that tastes like comfort in its purest form.

The slightly sour fermented bread mellows as it cooks, and the berbere permeates every fiber. Eaten with a side of fresh, uncooked injera (yes, injera to scoop up your injera), it's a lesson in waste-nothing cooking and a genuinely delicious start to any morning. Most traditional breakfast spots serve it for $2–3.

#### Ethiopian Coffee Ceremony

You haven't understood Addis Ababa until you've sat through *buna*. Ethiopian coffee ceremony is not a quick caffeine fix — it's a ritual that unfolds over an hour or more, beginning with green beans roasted over charcoal in a small pan. The woman performing the ceremony will wave the smoking pan toward you so the aroma fills your lungs, smoky and sharp, preparing your senses for what comes next.

The roasted beans are ground by hand, brewed in a clay pot called a *jebena*, and served in tiny handleless cups. The first round, *abol*, is the strongest. The second and third rounds grow progressively milder as water is added to the same grounds. Popcorn or roasted barley often accompanies each cup.

This is Ethiopia's birthplace claim made tangible — coffee originated in these highlands, and drinking it here feels like tasting history. Coffee houses throughout Addis offer the ceremony for $1–2, but the best experiences happen in homes or small neighborhood spots where time truly stops.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Addis Ababa runs on its own clock. Breakfast happens early, lunch stretches past 2 PM, and dinner rarely begins before 8. Learn to eat with your right hand only — your left never touches communal food. When someone offers you *gursha*, accept graciously; refusing is an insult.

Sharing is assumed. Most dishes arrive on platters meant for the table, and the act of eating from a common plate builds bonds faster than conversation. Don't rush. Meals here are measured in hours, not minutes. And if you're invited to a coffee ceremony, clear your schedule — you'll be there for three rounds, minimum.
