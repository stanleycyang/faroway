# Amsterdam Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The first thing you'll smell isn't canal water or coffee—it's caramelized sugar hitting a warm waffle iron at a market stall, that burnt-honey sweetness mixing with cold morning air as you pass a street vendor pressing fresh stroopwafels. This is Amsterdam at 9 AM, and you haven't even had breakfast yet.

## Why Amsterdam Eats Different

Amsterdam's food story is one of collision and reinvention. For centuries, this port city pulled ingredients and techniques from everywhere Dutch ships sailed—Indonesia, Suriname, the Caribbean, West Africa. The result isn't fusion confusion but rather a city that treats Indonesian rijsttafel with the same reverence as raw herring, where a "traditional Dutch meal" might mean anything from stamppot (mashed potatoes with vegetables) to a table covered in thirty small Indonesian dishes.

The Dutch approach to eating is refreshingly practical. Lunch is often quick and unfussy—a broodje (sandwich) eaten standing at a counter. But dinner becomes an event, lingering for hours in candlelit restaurants or around platters meant for sharing. Brown cafés, the wood-paneled neighborhood bars stained dark from centuries of tobacco smoke, serve bitterballen and local beers to crowds who've been drinking there since their grandparents did.

What makes Amsterdam particularly compelling is its compactness. Within a twenty-minute bike ride, you can eat Surinamese roti in De Pijp, fresh herring from a Jordaan canal-side stall, and Michelin-starred Dutch cooking near the Museum Quarter. The city doesn't make you choose—it just asks that you come hungry.

## The Essential Eats: 7 Dishes You Cannot Skip

**Stroopwafel** — Yes, you've had them from a package. No, that doesn't count. A fresh stroopwafel from a market vendor arrives warm and pliable, the caramel syrup still molten between two thin waffle layers. The exterior has a gentle crispness that gives way to that gooey, cinnamon-tinged center. Find the best ones at Albert Cuyp Market in De Pijp, where vendors press them to order. €2-3 gets you the real thing.

**Bitterballen** — These golf-ball-sized orbs look innocent until you bite through the shatteringly crisp breadcrumb coating and hit the molten beef ragout inside. The interior is almost obscenely creamy, somewhere between gravy and velvet, aggressively savory and best dipped in sharp Dutch mustard. Every brown café in Jordaan serves them; they're the Dutch equivalent of bar peanuts, except substantially more dangerous to your shirt.

**Haring (Raw Herring)** — This is where you prove your commitment. At haringstal stands throughout the city, vendors will offer you a whole raw herring, traditionally eaten by holding it by the tail and lowering it into your mouth. The fish is brined, not truly raw, with a clean, oceanic sweetness cut by raw onions and pickles. The texture is silky, almost buttery. Locals eat them for breakfast. The stalls near Central Station and along the canals in Jordaan have served them for generations.

**Kibbeling** — Think of this as the Netherlands' answer to fish and chips, except better. Chunks of cod are battered and fried until the exterior is almost impossibly crunchy, the fish inside flaking apart in moist, tender pieces. It comes with garlic sauce (or sometimes ravigotte, a tangy herb mayo), and you'll eat it from a paper cone while walking along a canal. Find it at fish stalls throughout Albert Cuyp Market.

**Poffertjes** — These miniature pancakes, each the size of a silver dollar, arrive in small mountains dusted with powdered sugar and a melting pat of butter. They're yeasted, giving them a slightly spongy texture that regular pancakes can't match, and they taste faintly of buckwheat. Street vendors cook them on specialized cast-iron pans with dozens of small indentations. The experience of eating them—burning your fingers slightly, sugar dusting your coat—is half the pleasure.

**Indonesian Rijsttafel** — "Rice table" is a colonial-era invention, a Dutch amplification of Indonesian cooking into a parade of small dishes: rendang (slow-braised beef in coconut), satay with peanut sauce, gado-gado (vegetables in peanut dressing), sambal-spiked eggs, fried tempeh, and a dozen more plates circling a mound of rice. It's sharing food at its most theatrical, and De Pijp has become the city's Indonesian food headquarters over generations.

**Kaas (Dutch Cheese)** — You cannot leave without standing at a cheese counter and eating samples until you've found your perfect aged gouda. The younger versions are mild and creamy; the aged ones (look for "oude" or "extra belegen") develop that crystalline crunch and caramel-butterscotch intensity. Any proper cheese shop will let you taste before buying. The markets throughout the city, particularly around Dam Square, have stalls dedicated entirely to this obsession.

## Street Food: The Outdoor Eating Guide

Amsterdam's street food operates on a different schedule than you might expect. Markets typically run from around 9 AM to 5 PM, making them lunch destinations rather than evening affairs. Albert Cuyp Market in De Pijp stretches for several blocks of produce, flowers, clothes, and crucially, food stalls serving everything from Surinamese sandwiches to fresh stroopwafels.

The etiquette is simple: order, pay, step aside, eat while walking. Nobody expects you to find a seat. Stalls near Albert Cuyp rarely have menus in English, but pointing works perfectly—and most vendors speak enough English to guide you.

For a more modern street food experience, De Hallen in Oud-West transforms a former tram depot into an indoor food hall with individual vendors selling everything from Vietnamese bánh mì to smoked meats to cocktails. It's busier on evenings and weekends, and feels distinctly local compared to more tourist-focused areas.

Watch for FEBO, the automat-style fast food institution where you drop coins into slots and pull out hot kroketten (deep-fried meat ragout logs) and other fried snacks from heated glass compartments. It's not fine dining. It's 2 AM after the brown cafés close, and it's perfect.

## Where to Eat: Budget to Splurge

**Budget (under €30/meal)** — The best cheap eating in Amsterdam often involves standing. A herring at a canal-side stall runs €4-5. A massive Surinamese roti stuffed with chicken and potato at a De Pijp takeaway costs around €10 and constitutes two meals. The Indonesian toko (small shops) throughout the city sell nasi goreng and bami for under €12. At brown cafés, a portion of bitterballen with a local beer totals maybe €10 and counts as dinner if you eat enough of them.

**Mid-range (€50-90/meal)** — This is where Amsterdam shines. Neighborhood restaurants throughout Jordaan and De Pijp serve three-course Dutch and international menus in this range, often in canal-house settings with original architectural details. Indonesian restaurants offering full rijsttafel typically charge €35-50 per person for the complete experience. Seafood restaurants near the canals serve platters of kibbeling, mussels, and North Sea catches without the fine-dining formality.

**Splurge (€150+/meal)** — Amsterdam has earned multiple Michelin stars in recent years, with Dutch chefs reinterpreting local ingredients through contemporary techniques. The restaurants near the Museum Quarter and along the major canals house most of the city's high-end options, offering tasting menus that might include North Sea crab, Dutch veal, and cheese courses sourced from specific farms. At this level, reservations are essential, often weeks in advance.

## For Vegetarians & Vegans

Amsterdam is genuinely welcoming to plant-based eaters—this is a city that embraced vegetarian restaurants before they were fashionable. Indonesian food offers substantial options: tempeh dishes, vegetable curries, gado-gado, and tofu preparations appear on most rijsttafel menus. Many brown cafés now offer vegetarian bitterballen (usually mushroom-based), and the food halls scattered throughout the city dedicate multiple stalls to vegan cooking.

De Pijp and Jordaan have the highest concentration of dedicated vegetarian and vegan restaurants, serving everything from elaborate tasting menus to casual lunch spots. Market vendors at Albert Cuyp sell falafel and vegetable-focused snacks alongside the fish stalls. Ask specifically about cheese—traditional Dutch cheese uses animal rennet, but vegetarian versions exist if you request them.

## The Food Map: Eating by Neighborhood

**De Pijp** — This is Amsterdam's multicultural stomach. Albert Cuyp Market anchors the neighborhood with stalls representing dozens of cuisines. The streets surrounding it house Indonesian restaurants, Surinamese takeaways, Middle Eastern grills, and an increasingly hip café scene. Come here hungry and without a specific plan; the best meal is often the one you stumble into.

**Jordaan** — The photogenic canal neighborhood has its own eating identity: brown cafés serving bitterballen, herring stalls along the water, small restaurants tucked into former residential buildings. It's more traditionally Dutch than De Pijp, with cheese shops and specialty food stores occupying ground-floor retail spaces. Morning is quieter; evenings transform the neighborhood into one extended dinner party.

**NDSM Wharf** — Take the free ferry from Central Station to reach this former shipyard turned creative complex. The food here skews experimental and casual: food trucks, brewery taprooms, restaurants in repurposed industrial spaces. It's worth the trip on a sunny day when the waterfront terraces fill with locals escaping the tourist center.

## How to Eat Like You Belong

Tipping is genuinely optional—servers earn living wages and won't chase you down for a tip. That said, rounding up or adding 5-10% for good service is appreciated and increasingly common. Credit cards are accepted almost everywhere, though some smaller stalls prefer cash or Dutch payment apps.

Dinner happens later than you might expect—8 PM reservations are normal, and kitchens often stay open past 10 PM. Lunch is typically noon to 2 PM and tends toward quick bites rather than extended meals. The Dutch eat dinner as the main meal; lunch might just be a sandwich at your desk.

When eating herring, don't be shy about asking the vendor to cut it into pieces if you're not ready to lower a whole fish into your mouth. Nobody judges; tourists do this constantly. And in brown cafés, ordering happens at the bar—don't wait to be served at your table.
