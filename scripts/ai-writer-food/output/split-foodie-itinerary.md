Three days in Split, and I ate like it was my job — because it was. I tracked down the city's best peka, argued with fishmongers at 7 AM, and learned that the real Dalmatian cuisine hides in places tourists walk right past. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I started.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Split runs on Mediterranean time. Lunch stretches from 1–3 PM; dinner rarely starts before 8 PM. Most restaurants accept cards, but market vendors and konobas (taverns) prefer Croatian kuna in cash. Tipping isn't obligatory — round up or leave 10% for good service. Reservations matter for dinner at popular spots, especially along the Riva waterfront. Learn "hvala" (thank you) and use it constantly.

## Day 1: Palace Walls & Market Stalls

**Theme: Old Town immersion — eating where emperors walked**

### Morning

Start inside Diocletian's Palace, where the old stones trap the smell of fresh bread and coffee. Find a café tucked into the palace basement — these vaulted Roman chambers now serve espresso alongside views of 1,700-year-old walls. Order a burek, the flaky pastry stuffed with cheese or meat that Croatians eat standing up, usually before 9 AM. The cheese version (burek sa sirom) runs about €2–3 and pairs perfectly with a thick Croatian coffee. Don't sit at a table with a harbor view; sit where the locals stand at the counter, eating fast before work.

**Cost: €4–6**

### Afternoon

Walk five minutes north to Pazar, Split's Green Market, which sprawls just outside the palace walls. This is where Split actually eats. Farmers from the surrounding hills sell tomatoes that smell like summer, figs wrapped in paper, and olive oil in recycled water bottles. Buy a bag of dried figs and some smoked cheese to snack on — you'll need fuel.

For lunch, find a konoba in the Old Town backstreets. Order a plate of pašticada, the Dalmatian beef stew braised in wine and prunes, served over gnocchi. This dish takes hours to cook properly, and you can taste when someone's cut corners. A good version will have meat that falls apart before your fork touches it. Pair it with a glass of local Plavac Mali red.

**Cost: €18–28**

### Evening

Dinner belongs to the Riva waterfront, but not at the tourist traps with laminated menus. Walk past them toward Manuš or the edges of the promenade where locals congregate. Tonight is about seafood — specifically, grilled brancin (sea bass) or orada (sea bream). The fish comes whole, simply prepared with olive oil, garlic, and chard. Ask what came in that morning.

At a mid-range waterfront restaurant, expect to pay €25–40 for fish by weight, a side of blitva (chard with potatoes), bread, and wine. If you're splurging, order a seafood platter to share — langoustines, mussels, and whatever the boat brought in.

**Cost: €35–70**

**Budget: ~€57** | **Mid-range: ~€75** | **Splurge: ~€104**

## Day 2: Beyond the Walls

**Theme: Neighborhood eating in Varoš and the Arts Quarter**

### Morning

Leave the tourist center early and climb into Varoš, the old fishermen's neighborhood that clings to Marjan Hill. The streets narrow to shoulder-width, laundry hangs between windows, and the tourists thin out. Find a bakery serving fresh-from-the-oven soparnik — a thin, savory pie filled with Swiss chard, onions, and garlic, traditionally made in a wood-fired oven. This is peasant food that became a protected cultural heritage item. Eat it warm, standing on the street, for about €3–4.

Follow with coffee at a neighborhood café where you'll be the only non-local. Order a rakija — the Croatian grape brandy — if you need courage for the morning climb up Marjan.

**Cost: €5–8**

### Afternoon

Descend from Marjan into the Arts Quarter near Pjaca square. Lunch should be crni rižot — black risotto stained with cuttlefish ink. When done right, it's briny and almost sweet, the rice still slightly firm. Bad versions taste like the sea after a storm; good versions taste like the sea on a calm day. The ink turns your teeth and lips black. Embrace it. A proper portion runs €12–18.

After lunch, walk to Bokeria, a hidden food hall that locals actually use. It's split between a wine bar, a fresh pasta counter, and a market selling Croatian specialties. Buy some Pag cheese and a jar of local honey to take home. This is also where you'll find cooking classes if you want to learn peka yourself — the slow-cooked meat and vegetable dish prepared under a bell-shaped lid covered in embers.

**Cost: €20–35**

### Evening

Tonight, leave the waterfront behind. Eat in the Business District near the ferry terminal, where restaurants serve workers and residents rather than cruise passengers. Order peka — lamb or octopus, ideally — but call ahead by noon to request it. This dish takes 2–3 hours under the coals, and no one's making it on demand.

A peka dinner for two with wine will run €50–80 at a proper konoba. The octopus version arrives purple and tender, surrounded by potatoes that absorbed hours of juice. The lamb falls off the bone. Either version comes with the smell of woodsmoke and the satisfaction of eating something that can't be rushed.

**Cost: €40–80**

**Budget: ~€65** | **Mid-range: ~€90** | **Splurge: ~€123**

## Day 3: The Greatest Hits

**Theme: Farewell feast — one last lap through Split's best**

### Morning

Return to the Old Town for a final breakfast, but do it properly this time. Find a café in the morning shadow of the Cathedral of Saint Domnius and order a fritule — small, doughnut-like fritters dusted with powdered sugar. They're traditionally Christmas food, but Split serves them year-round. Get them fresh, still warm, with a macchiato on the side. The cathedral bells might ring while you eat. Let them.

If you skipped the fish market yesterday, go now. The Pazar market has a fish section that opens at dawn. Even if you can't cook, walk through and watch the transaction between fishermen and restaurateurs. Point at what interests you, and someone will tell you what it is. Buy a small bag of fried anchovies to eat immediately — they cost almost nothing and taste like the Adriatic concentrated.

**Cost: €6–10**

### Afternoon

Your final lunch should be the dish Split does better than anywhere: whole grilled fish, simply prepared. Walk to the Waterfront edge near Bačvice beach, where the konobas serve workers from the ferry terminal and locals who know better than to eat in the palace. Order whatever's freshest — the waiter will tell you. Get the blitva on the side. Drink a cold Ožujsko beer. Sit in the sun.

Lunch here runs €20–30 for fish, sides, and a drink. The fish comes filleted tableside if you ask, though watching the waiter work is half the experience.

**Cost: €20–35**

### Evening

End with the best dinner Split offers. Return to the Old Town — you've earned the Riva at sunset now — and book a table at one of the serious restaurants tucked into the palace walls. This is splurge territory: start with oysters from Ston, then a pasta course with lobster, then grilled Adriatic prawns that cost more than your breakfast budget for the trip. Drink a serious Croatian white — Pošip or Grk from Korčula.

A meal like this runs €70–120 per person, but you'll remember it longer than most things you spend money on. If that's outside your budget, order a final pašticada instead, toast Split with a glass of Plavac Mali, and walk home through the palace at midnight, when the stones still hold the day's heat.

**Cost: €40–120**

**Budget: ~€66** | **Mid-range: ~€95** | **Splurge: ~€165**

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Order fish by weight, but ask first.** Prices are per kilogram. A 400g fish at €50/kg is €20 — reasonable. A 900g fish at €70/kg is €63 — maybe not what you planned. Confirm before they grill it.

- **Peka requires patience.** If a restaurant offers peka without advance notice, it's reheated. Real peka takes 2+ hours. Call by noon for dinner, or ask what time tomorrow works.

- **The Green Market peaks at 8 AM.** By noon, the best produce is gone and the energy's dead. Go early, eat figs for breakfast, talk to the farmers even if you don't speak Croatian.

- **Tip in cash even when paying by card.** Servers often don't see card tips. A few euros on the table goes directly to the person who fed you well.
