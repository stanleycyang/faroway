Three days in Samarkand taught me this: the city feeds you like a generous grandmother who won't take no for an answer. Plov at 10 AM? Of course. Another round of samsa before dinner? You'll manage. This is an itinerary for eating with purpose — the one I wish someone had handed me before I arrived hungry and overwhelmed at Siab Bazaar with no plan and too many options.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Samarkand runs on cash — Uzbek som, specifically. Cards work at upscale restaurants but nowhere else, so hit an ATM early. Lunch is the main event here, often starting around noon and stretching lazily. Dinner comes late, rarely before 7 PM. Tipping isn't expected at local spots but 10% is appreciated at sit-down restaurants. No reservations needed except for the fanciest places. Learn "rahmat" (thank you) — it opens doors.

## Day 1: The Old Town Initiation

**Morning — Siab Bazaar & First Bread**

Start where Samarkand starts every morning: at the bread. Walk to Old Town before 9 AM when Siab Bazaar is still shaking off sleep. Find a tandoor baker — you'll smell them before you see them — and buy non straight from the clay oven. The bread comes out blistered and impossibly fragrant, stamped with geometric patterns that date back centuries. Tear it while it's warm. This isn't breakfast; it's orientation.

Pair it with a bowl of mastava from one of the chaikhanas lining the bazaar's edge. This rice soup with meat and vegetables costs almost nothing and sits in your stomach like a warm anchor. You'll need it.

*Estimated cost: $3–$5*

**Afternoon — Plov Center & Market Grazing**

By noon, make your way to the plov centers clustered in Old Town. Here's what I learned too late: plov isn't lunch, it's an event. The dish — rice cooked in meat fat with carrots, chickpeas, garlic, and lamb — gets prepared in massive cast-iron kazans over open flames. Each neighborhood argues theirs is best. They're all right.

Arrive before 1 PM. Seriously. The good plov sells out. Point to the kazan, hold up one finger, and find a spot at a communal table. The rice should glisten, the meat should fall apart, and the bottom layer — the crispy, caramelized part called "qazmaq" — should make you briefly reconsider your life choices. Someone might offer you vodka. This is normal.

After, walk off the heaviness through the bazaar properly. This is grazing territory: dried apricots, walnuts, chunks of crystallized sugar, rounds of kurt (dried yogurt balls that taste like salty, funky cheese). Buy small amounts of everything. The vendors will let you taste first.

*Estimated cost: $8–$15*

**Evening — Old Town Courtyard Dinner**

For dinner, find a courtyard restaurant in Old Town where the tables sit under grapevines and the menu doesn't try too hard. Order shashlik — lamb skewers grilled over charcoal until the fat renders and the edges char. Get the lamb, not the beef. Add a plate of achuchuk (tomato-onion salad) and more bread for scooping.

The mid-range spots here serve massive portions for $15–20 per person with drinks. The splurge version adds a bottle of Uzbek wine and maybe some manti (steamed dumplings) to start. Either way, eat slowly. The courtyard cools down as the sun sets, and no one rushes you.

*Estimated cost: $15–$35*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$26 · Mid ~$45 · Splurge ~$55

## Day 2: Beyond the Tourist Center

**Morning — Local Neighborhood Breakfast**

Skip Old Town today. Head to the residential neighborhoods where Samarkand actually lives — the streets behind the main avenues where laundry hangs between Soviet-era apartment blocks. Find a neighborhood chaikhana (teahouse) that looks like it hasn't changed since 1985. You want the one full of older men playing backgammon at 8 AM.

Order non with kaymak — thick clotted cream spread on warm bread. Add honey if they have it. Drink green tea from a pot that seems to refill itself. This breakfast costs under $4 and buys you an hour of watching Samarkand wake up properly.

*Estimated cost: $3–$5*

**Afternoon — Cooking Class & Lunch Combined**

Book a home cooking class for midday. Several families in the Arts Quarter and surrounding neighborhoods run these — your hotel can arrange it, or ask around. You'll spend three hours learning to make plov or manti from someone's mother or grandmother. This isn't a polished cooking school experience. The kitchen will be small. The instructions will be vague. You'll mess up the dough. It's perfect.

The meal you cook becomes lunch, eaten at the family table with more food than you helped make appearing from somewhere. This is the hidden gem everyone talks about but few actually do — learning through eating, eating through learning.

*Estimated cost: $25–$40 (including everything)*

**Evening — Central District Modern**

Dinner tonight moves upscale. The Business District and areas around the main avenues have restaurants that take the old recipes and present them with tablecloths and wine lists. This is where you try lagman — hand-pulled noodles in a spiced lamb broth — done with refinement rather than rustic charm. The noodles should be chewy, the broth should stain your lips with chili oil, and the vegetables should still have bite.

Add an order of chuchvara (tiny dumplings in broth) if you have room. You probably don't, but order them anyway. At the nicer places, expect $25–40 per person with drinks. The service is slower, the portions more restrained, and the experience worth it for contrast.

*Estimated cost: $25–$50*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$53 · Mid ~$70 · Splurge ~$95

## Day 3: The Farewell Feast

**Morning — Tea & Pastry Ritual**

Your last morning deserves ceremony. Find the hidden café tucked in one of the quieter alleys — every local knows a different one, all of them correct. Order a full tea service: pot of green tea, small sweets, maybe some halva if they have it.

Then add samsa — the flaky, triangular pastries stuffed with spiced lamb and onion, baked in a tandoor until the crust shatters. The best ones come out mid-morning when the ovens hit peak temperature. Eat two. You're leaving tomorrow; restraint no longer applies.

*Estimated cost: $5–$8*

**Afternoon — Market Farewell Tour**

Return to Siab Bazaar or explore the off-the-beaten markets in neighborhoods tourists rarely visit. This isn't about finding lunch — it's about assembling provisions and eating standing up.

Work your way through the market systematically: fresh suzma (strained yogurt) eaten with a spoon right there, more dried fruits for the road, a final round of non to take with you. Stop at the prepared food section for norin — cold noodles with horse meat, thinly sliced, that sounds strange and tastes addictive. Eat it at the counter, standing, while the vendor watches to make sure you like it.

*Estimated cost: $8–$15*

**Evening — The Meal Worth Ending On**

Dinner is the finale. Go back to Old Town or find the restaurant everyone mentioned but you hadn't tried yet. Order extravagantly: shashlik, manti, plov (again, yes), achuchuk, bread, and whatever they recommend that you haven't had. Order Uzbek wine. Order more bread.

This is Samarkand food at its most generous — not refined, not complicated, just abundant and deeply satisfying. The spices are warm, the meat is rich, and the bread never stops coming. Spend $40–60 per person if you want to do this properly. You'll waddle out. It's the only appropriate exit.

*Estimated cost: $30–$60*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~$43 · Mid ~$58 · Splurge ~$83

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Plov timing is everything.** The massive kazans are filled once, usually around 11 AM. By 2 PM, the good stuff is gone. Plan your schedule around this, not the other way around.
- **Bring wet wipes.** You'll eat with your hands more than you expect, and many local spots don't have napkins. Dignity requires preparation.
- **Tea is never refused.** If someone offers tea, accept it. This isn't about thirst — it's how Samarkand says "welcome" and "stay awhile" and "tell me about yourself."
- **The bread is the utensil.** Forks exist, but tearing off bread and using it to grab meat, scoop rice, or mop up sauce is correct. Watch the locals and follow.
