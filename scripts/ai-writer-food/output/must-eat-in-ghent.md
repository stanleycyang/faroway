The first bite catches you off guard. It's 10 AM on a grey Wednesday in Ghent, and you're standing outside a bakery on Groentenmarkt, holding a *mastelle* so warm it's still steaming through its paper bag. The anise hits first—sharp, almost medicinal—then gives way to something sweeter, softer, the bread's interior yielding like brioche but denser, more purposeful. You didn't expect to fall in love this early in the day.

But that's Ghent. This medieval Flemish city doesn't announce its culinary brilliance with Michelin fanfare or Instagram-ready plating. It feeds you the way a grandmother feeds you: persistently, generously, with dishes that have been made the same way for centuries because why would you change perfection?

I spent three weeks eating through Ghent's canals, cobblestones, and hidden courtyards. What follows is what you need to put in your mouth before you leave.

---

#### Waterzooi

There's a reason Ghent claims this dish as its own, despite what Brussels might tell you. Waterzooi is liquid comfort—a cream-based stew that started with fish pulled from the Lys River and evolved into chicken when the waters grew too polluted for eating in the 19th century. The name literally means "watery mess," which is the most Belgian underselling of all time.

The bowl arrives pale gold, almost luminous, with chunks of chicken thigh so tender they're falling apart before your spoon reaches them. Leeks, carrots, and celery float in the broth like soft confetti. But the magic is the sauce itself: enriched with egg yolks and cream at the last moment, silky without being heavy, tasting somehow of both summer gardens and winter blankets. You'll want to mop it up with crusty bread until the bowl looks like you've licked it clean. You might.

Find it in the traditional restaurants around Patershol, Ghent's oldest neighborhood, where stone walls hold centuries of cooking smells. Lunch is the right time. Expect to pay €18–24 for a proper portion.

#### Gentse Neuzen

These aren't pretty. Cone-shaped candies the color of a raspberry bruise, with a powdery exterior that looks like they've been rolling around someone's pocket. But put one in your mouth and understand: this is what childhood tastes like in Ghent.

*Gentse neuzen*—literally "Ghent noses"—are violet-flavored gummies made with real arabica gum, which gives them a texture that's neither gummy bear nor hard candy but something in between. The violet flavor is assertive, floral, almost soapy on first hit, then mellows into something genuinely addictive. They were invented in the 19th century by a local confectioner, and the recipe has barely changed.

You'll find them at specialty candy shops throughout the city center, often sold by weight in paper bags. A small bag costs around €3–4. Eat them slowly. One at a time. Let them dissolve.

---

#### Stoofvlees met Friet

Let's talk about why Belgian fries are better than everyone else's fries. Double-fried in beef tallow. Cut thicker. The exterior shatters when you bite, and the interior is fluffy, almost creamy. You already knew this.

But stoofvlees is the dish that turns those fries into a vehicle for something greater. It's a Flemish beef stew, braised for hours in dark beer—often a local bruin—with onions melting into the sauce until they're indistinguishable from the meat. The gravy is sweet and bitter and deep, coating each chunk of beef like a second skin. You dip your fries into it. You order more fries. You dip again. The combination of crispy-hot potato and rich, beer-dark meat sauce is aggressively satisfying.

The frituren (fry shops) around Sint-Pietersplein serve versions of this, as do traditional eateries in the student quarter. It's dinner food, hearty and unsubtle. Around €12–16 with a proper serving of fries.

#### Cuberdons

Similar to neuzen but weirder, more divisive, more unforgettable. Cuberdons are cone-shaped candies with a hard purple shell that cracks to reveal a liquid center—genuinely liquid, like the world's strangest cough syrup, but in a good way. The flavor is raspberry, but not the raspberry you know. Darker. More mysterious.

They don't travel well—the filling crystallizes after a few days—so eating them in Ghent is part of the experience. There's a famous rivalry between two cuberdon vendors at Groentenmarkt; their stalls sit across from each other, and each claims to be the original. Pick a side if you want, but they both taste like sticky, confusing joy.

About €4 for a small bag. Eat them within two days, at room temperature. They're at their best slightly soft.

---

#### Gentse Mokken

You'll see these in every bakery window: small, golden-brown cakes shaped like coins, their tops glossy with sugar. *Gentse mokken* are deceptively simple—just flour, butter, sugar, eggs, and a whisper of cinnamon—but the texture is unlike anything else. Dense yet tender, slightly chewy at the edges, with a caramelized sweetness from the sugar syrup that coats them after baking.

The origin story is murky, but these have been made in Ghent since at least the 18th century. They were festival food, sold at fairs and markets, meant to be eaten while walking. That's still the best way: warm from the bakery, eaten on a canal bridge, watching the swans glide past.

Any traditional bakery in the old town will have them. €1–2 each. Morning coffee companion.

---

#### Tierenteyn Mustard

Not a dish. A condiment. But hear me out.

The Tierenteyn mustard shop on Groentenmarkt has been selling one product since 1790. One. They make a single variety of mustard—a coarse, brown-yellow paste with a nose-clearing heat that's somehow also rounded and complex. It's stored in ceramic pots, ladled out to order, and sold with the warning that it will not keep forever because there are no preservatives.

Spread it on a ham sandwich from the market next door. Add it to your stoofvlees. Put it on cheese. This mustard has more personality than most people you know.

A pot costs around €5–7 depending on size. Worth every cent.

---

#### Mattentaart

From the nearby town of Geraardsbergen but utterly embraced by Ghent, mattentaart is a small tart filled with *matten*—a type of curdled buttermilk cheese—sugar, eggs, and almond extract. The filling is custardy but lighter than custard, with a faint tang from the cheese and a whisper of bitter almond that haunts you for hours after.

These were originally Lenten food, meant to use up dairy before fasting. Now they're everyday afternoon snacks, found in bakeries across the city. The crust is thin, crisp, barely sweet—it exists to hold the filling and get out of the way.

€2–3 each. Best slightly warm, with very strong coffee.

---

#### Jenever

You will be offered this. Say yes.

Jenever is the grandfather of gin—a juniper-flavored spirit that Belgium and the Netherlands have been arguing over for centuries. In Ghent, it's served in tulip-shaped glasses, filled to the brim so you have to lean down and sip the first taste without lifting it. This is tradition. This is also hilarious to watch tourists attempt.

The flavor ranges from young (sharp, raw, assertive) to aged (smooth, almost whiskey-like, with notes of malt and oak). Some are infused with fruits or herbs—apple, vanilla, chocolate. The bars around Vrijdagmarkt specialize in this, with wall-length selections. Start with something old and work your way stranger.

€3–6 per glass, depending on age and rarity. Pace yourself.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Ghent eats late by some standards and early by others. Lunch is serious business here—restaurants fill between noon and 1:30 PM, and the best dishes often run out by 2. Dinner service starts around 7 PM, and rushing through a meal is considered vaguely insulting.

Portions are generous, so order less than you think you need. Bread often costs extra (a euro or two), and water is rarely free—order it still or sparkling, and accept this as the cost of civilization. Tipping is not expected but rounding up is appreciated.

Markets matter here. The Friday market at Vrijdagmarkt, the daily Groentenmarkt, the Sunday flower market at Kouter—these are where locals shop and graze. Bring cash, bring your appetite, and be willing to point at whatever looks good. The vendors will guide you.
