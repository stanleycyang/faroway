The steak arrives at 10 PM, and the entire restaurant turns to look. Three fingers thick, charred black on the outside, almost purple within, glistening with nothing but olive oil and coarse salt. The waiter doesn't ask how you'd like it cooked. In Florence, you don't get to choose.

I spent three weeks eating through this city, and I left five pounds heavier with zero regrets. Florence doesn't do trendy food, doesn't chase innovation, doesn't care what's happening in Copenhagen or Tokyo. It perfects what it's been making for five hundred years, and it expects you to show up hungry.

---

#### Bistecca alla Fiorentina

This is the dish that defines Florentine swagger. A T-bone from the Chianina cattle of the Valdichiana valley, aged at least two weeks, cut thick enough that you could use it as a doorstop. The grill master treats it simply: brutal heat, no seasoning until after, finished with Tuscan olive oil that tastes like fresh-cut grass and black pepper.

The first bite is warm on the outside, cool at the center — not cold, never cold, but that perfect *al sangue* temperature that makes carnivores close their eyes in reverence. The fat renders into something almost sweet. The meat itself has a mineral depth, an ironiness that pairs obscenely well with a glass of Brunello.

Bistecca exists because of geography: Tuscany has always raised these massive white cattle, originally for plowing fields, and Florentine butchers learned centuries ago that the less you do to this meat, the more it gives back. The English word "sirloin" may or may not come from a king knighting his beef, but bistecca needs no such mythology. It simply *is*.

Find it at any serious trattoria in the Oltrarno or Santo Spirito neighborhoods — places where the menu is handwritten and the waiters don't smile until your second visit. Expect to pay €45–70 per kilo, shared between two people. Order it alone and they'll either laugh or refuse.

#### Lampredotto

If bistecca is Florence's showpiece, lampredotto is its soul. A sandwich made from the fourth stomach of a cow — specifically the abomasum, for those keeping track — slow-simmered in broth with tomatoes, onion, parsley, and celery until it becomes something tender and deeply savory.

You order it from a cart, always a cart, the kind that sets up near Sant'Ambrogio market or outside the Mercato Centrale. The *lampredottaio* fishes out the stomach with tongs, chops it on a wooden board, stuffs it into a crusty roll, and asks one question: *bagnato?* Say yes. This means he dunks the top half of the bread into the simmering broth until it soaks through, soft and dripping.

The first bite is hot broth, then salt, then that unmistakable offal richness — not funky, not gamey, just deeply meaty in a way that regular beef never achieves. Add the green sauce (salsa verde) and the spicy red sauce (piccante). The combination makes you understand why Florentine workers have been eating this for lunch since the Renaissance.

Lampredotto costs €4–5. It is the best meal-per-euro ratio in all of Italy.

#### Ribollita

This is poverty cooking elevated to art. Ribollita means "reboiled" — a soup made from yesterday's bread, yesterday's vegetables, whatever the Tuscan farm had lying around. Black cabbage (cavolo nero), cannellini beans, carrots, celery, onions, tomatoes, stale country bread, and enough olive oil that the surface shimmers green.

The texture is what gets you. It's not really soup. It's more like eating a savory bread pudding that's collapsed into something creamy and thick. Each spoonful has multiple lives: the silky beans, the dark bitter greens, the bread that's dissolved into the broth and the bread that's retained just enough chew.

Ribollita was born from necessity — Tuscan farmers couldn't afford to waste anything, so leftover minestrone became the next day's meal, reboiled with stale bread until it became something new. Now it's served in the finest trattorias in the city, always better in October when the cavolo nero comes into season.

Order it at Trattoria Mario near San Lorenzo market, where you'll share a table with strangers and pay €8 for a bowl that could anchor you through a full day of museum-walking. Best at lunch, served hot enough to steam your glasses.

#### Pappardelle al Cinghiale

The pasta itself is a ribbon, wide as two fingers, with enough surface area to hold serious sauce. And this sauce is serious: wild boar braised for hours in red wine, tomatoes, rosemary, juniper berries, and bay leaves until the meat falls into shreds that cling to every centimeter of pasta.

The first forkful tastes like the Tuscan hills — gamey and dark, sweetened by slow cooking, with a wine-soaked richness that coats your mouth. Wild boar tastes nothing like pork. It's leaner, more intense, with an almost iron-like depth from years of rooting through chestnut forests.

This dish exists because Tuscany is lousy with wild boar. They're pests, really, tearing up vineyards and olive groves. So Tuscans learned to hunt them, and then to cook them low and slow until the tough meat turned silky. The pappardelle — egg pasta cut wide to match the rustic sauce — completes the marriage.

Every trattoria in the Oltrarno serves a version. The good ones taste like autumn in a bowl. Expect €12–16 for a generous plate that will ruin you for lesser ragùs forever.

#### Schiacciata

Imagine focaccia's cooler Florentine cousin — thinner, crispier, dimpled with fingers and drizzled with enough olive oil to pool in the little craters. Plain schiacciata is eaten warm from bakeries in the morning, torn with your hands, almost unsettlingly addictive in its simplicity.

But then there's schiacciata stuffed: split horizontally and filled with mortadella, or fresh mozzarella and tomato, or prosciutto crudo that drapes over the bread in translucent pink sheets. The bread stays crisp on the outside, slightly chewy within, providing structure for whatever fills it.

The name means "squashed" — a reference to how bakers press the dough flat before baking. It dates back to Etruscan times, making it older than Florence itself. During grape harvest, bakers make schiacciata all'uva, studded with wine grapes, sugar scattered on top, served as dessert.

Buy it from Antico Forno in the Mercato Centrale or any neighborhood *forno* (bakery) by 9 AM, when it's still warm. A plain piece costs €2–3; stuffed versions run €4–6. Eat it standing on the street like everyone else.

#### Gelato

Florence invented this. In the 16th century, architect Bernardo Buontalenti created frozen dessert for the Medici court, and the city has never stopped perfecting it. Real Florentine gelato uses less air than ice cream, served at a slightly warmer temperature, which makes the flavors hit harder and the texture denser.

The first lick of proper *crema* — egg custard base — tastes like frozen pastry cream, impossibly smooth. Pistachio should be muted green, not neon (that's a sign of artificial color). Chocolate should be dark and almost bitter. Fruit flavors should taste like actual fruit, bright and clean.

Skip any shop where the gelato is piled in dramatic peaks — real gelato is stored in covered metal containers to protect the texture. Look for *produzione propria* on the sign, meaning they make it in-house.

Gelateria della Passera in Oltrarno makes a ricotta and fig that will alter your brain chemistry. Vivoli near Santa Croce has been family-run since 1930. Expect €2.50–4 for a cone. Get two flavors. Get three. No one's judging.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Florentines eat late. Lunch runs from 1–2:30 PM; dinner rarely starts before 8:30 PM. If you walk into a restaurant at 6 PM, you'll be eating with tour groups. The meal structure matters: *primo* (pasta or soup), *secondo* (meat or fish), *contorno* (vegetables, ordered separately). You're not expected to order all three, but don't just order a pasta and ask for a table during dinner rush.

Aperitivo culture is real — between 6–8 PM, bars across the city serve Negronis and spritzes alongside free snacks that range from chips to full buffets. Santo Spirito piazza is the epicenter.

Bread comes without butter, without olive oil for dipping. It's unsalted (a tradition dating to medieval salt taxes) and meant to push food onto your fork or soak up sauce.
