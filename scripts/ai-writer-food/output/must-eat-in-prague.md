The steam rises from the bowl in lazy spirals, catching the late afternoon light filtering through the window of a Vinohrady cellar restaurant. I lift the first spoonful of kulajda to my lips — creamy, tangy, impossibly fragrant with dill — and the poached egg breaks, its golden yolk bleeding into the broth. Outside, Prague's church bells are ringing. I am not going anywhere.

Three weeks of eating through this city has taught me that Czech cuisine is not delicate. It is not minimalist. It is food built for cold winters and long walks across cobblestones, for beer halls where you sit elbow-to-elbow with strangers, for grandmothers who believe that hunger is a moral failing. This is the food that sustained a nation through empires and occupations, and it will sustain you too — generously, unapologetically, magnificently.

#### Svíčková na Smetaně

If the Czech Republic had a national dish worthy of state dinners and Sunday lunches alike, this is it. Svíčková is beef sirloin that has been marinated for days in a mixture of root vegetables — carrots, parsnips, celery root, onions — then braised until it yields to the gentle pressure of a fork. But the meat is almost secondary to the sauce: a velvet-smooth, cream-enriched gravy that carries the sweetness of the vegetables and a whisper of allspice. Cranberry compote sits alongside, its tartness cutting through the richness. Whipped cream — yes, whipped cream — crowns the plate, and it works in a way that shouldn't make sense but absolutely does.

The dumplings beneath are *houskové knedlíky*, bread dumplings sliced into thick rounds that exist solely to soak up every drop of that extraordinary sauce. Leave nothing behind. This dish dates back to the Austro-Hungarian era, when Czech cooks took the French technique of braising and made it their own — heavier, creamier, more satisfying.

Find it at traditional restaurants in Malá Strana or Old Town, ideally for a late lunch when you have nowhere else to be. Expect to pay around 220–320 CZK ($9–$14), and expect to need a walk afterward.

#### Vepřo-Knedlo-Zelo

Say it with me: *vepřo-knedlo-zelo*. Pork-dumpling-cabbage. The holy trinity of Czech comfort food, condensed into a single phrase that every Czech child learns before they can tie their shoes.

The pork is roasted until the fat renders and the skin crackles, the meat underneath staying tender and faintly sweet from caraway seeds. The dumplings here might be bread-based or potato-based — both have their devotees, and you'll develop your own preference. The sauerkraut is not the aggressive, vinegar-forward kind you might know; it's gentler, often cooked with a touch of sugar and studded with caraway.

Together, they create a plate that is impossible to eat elegantly and absolutely perfect on a gray Prague afternoon. This is peasant food elevated to art, the dish that Czech families serve on holidays and that appears on the menu of every hospoda (pub) in the city. The portions are enormous. The prices are not — around 180–250 CZK ($7–$10) at most neighborhood spots.

Žižkov is where I'd send you for the authentic experience: the kind of wood-paneled pub where locals drink Pilsner at noon and nobody speaks English.

#### Kulajda

I've had this soup three times in the past week, and I'm not done. Kulajda is a study in contrast: the broth is creamy yet acidic, warmed by white pepper, perfumed with enormous amounts of fresh dill. Potatoes break down into the liquid, thickening it. Wild mushrooms add earthiness, their flavor deepened by the soup's richness.

And then there's the poached egg, sitting in the center like a gift. Pierce it. Watch the yolk transform the bowl into something even more luxurious. This is a dish from the Bohemian countryside, a forager's soup made from what the forests provided. It tastes like autumn even in spring.

You'll find kulajda in traditional restaurants and at the Naplavka farmers market on Saturday mornings, where vendors ladle it into paper cups for hungry shoppers. It costs almost nothing — 60–100 CZK ($2.50–$4) — and it will make you understand why Czech people look forward to mushroom season the way others anticipate summer.

#### Smažený Sýr

Let's be honest: this is fried cheese. A thick slab of Edam or Hermelín (a Czech brie-style cheese), coated in breadcrumbs, and deep-fried until the exterior shatters and the interior turns molten. It comes with tartar sauce for dipping and often a small salad that exists mainly so you can pretend you're being healthy.

Smažený sýr is the food of late nights and beer-soaked conversations, the dish that appears on menus at traditional pubs and in the hands of people stumbling across Charles Bridge at midnight. It is gloriously, unashamedly indulgent. The Czech Republic is not a country that apologizes for calories.

Street vendors and hospody serve it for 120–180 CZK ($5–$7). The best versions use Hermelín, whose creamy interior becomes almost sauce-like when fried. Pair it with a cold Pilsner and watch the world improve.

#### Trdelník

You'll smell them before you see them — the sweet, yeasty aroma of dough spiraling around metal cylinders over open coals, caramelizing with cinnamon sugar. Trdelník is everywhere in Prague's tourist center, vendors hawking them from wooden stalls, the pastries emerging golden and crackling.

Here's the truth: trdelník is not historically Czech. It's a Slovak and Hungarian import that arrived in Prague's Old Town in the late 1990s and never left. Locals will tell you this with a mixture of amusement and mild irritation. But does it matter when you're biting through that crisp, sugar-coated exterior into the soft, warm dough inside? When the cinnamon hits your lips and the whole thing tastes like a hug?

Eat it plain or filled with ice cream, Nutella, or whipped cream (the tourist versions). Pay around 80–150 CZK ($3–$6) depending on toppings. And know that while you're eating a tourist snack, you're eating a delicious one.

#### Koláče

For something genuinely Czech, find a local bakery — a *pekárna* — in Vinohrady or Holešovice and look for koláče. These are round pastries with a soft, slightly sweet dough, their centers filled with fruit jams, poppy seed paste, sweetened tvaroh (a fresh cheese similar to quark), or combinations of all three.

The dough should be pillowy, yielding, barely sweet enough to let the fillings shine. The best koláče are made fresh each morning and sold out by noon. They cost almost nothing — 25–50 CZK ($1–$2) — and they're the breakfast of Czech grandmothers, eaten with coffee at kitchen tables across the country.

This is not a photogenic pastry. It is simply a good one.

---

#### Bramborák

A potato pancake, fried in oil until the edges turn lacy and bronze while the center stays dense and starchy. Bramborák is seasoned aggressively with garlic and marjoram, giving it an herbaceous punch that cuts through the richness. Some versions include bits of bacon or sausage; all versions are best eaten immediately, when the exterior still crackles.

Street food, pub food, market food — you'll find bramborák anywhere that ordinary people gather to eat. It costs around 50–80 CZK ($2–$3) and pairs magnificently with beer.

---

#### Czech Beer

You cannot eat in Prague without drinking in Prague, and you cannot drink in Prague without confronting the fact that Czechs consume more beer per capita than any nation on earth. This is not a statistic; it's a lifestyle.

Order *pivo* at any hospoda and you'll receive a half-liter of Pilsner — pale, golden, bitter in the way that makes you immediately want another sip. The foam should be thick, creamy, perfectly even. The beer should cost less than a bottle of water, often around 45–60 CZK ($2–$2.50), which tells you everything about Czech priorities.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the main meal in Prague. Restaurants offer *denní menu* (daily menus) from roughly 11 a.m. to 2 p.m. — two or three courses for a fraction of dinner prices. This is how locals eat well without spending much.

When drinking beer, never tilt the glass when toasting. Look your companions in the eyes, say *na zdraví*, and drink. Failing to make eye contact supposedly curses you with seven years of bad sex. I have not tested this, and neither should you.

At restaurants, tell the server the total you wish to pay rather than leaving money on the table. If your bill is 186 CZK, say "200" when handing over your cash. It's elegant, efficient, and expected.

Embrace the *hospoda* — the Czech pub — for what it is: a democratic institution where beer flows cheaply and the food is honest. You'll share tables with strangers. This is normal.
