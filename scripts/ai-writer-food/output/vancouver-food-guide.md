# Vancouver Food Guide: What to Eat, Where & How Much

The first thing that hits you isn't the ocean breeze rolling off English Bay—it's the smell of salmon smoking over alderwood, drifting from a waterfront restaurant while your ferry pulls into harbor. Then comes the sizzle of a Japadog vendor's grill on Robson Street, the clatter of dim sum carts in a Richmond banquet hall, and somewhere in Gastown, a chef is torching the top of a Nanaimo bar reinvented as crème brûlée. Vancouver doesn't just feed you; it ambushes your senses before you've even found your hotel.

## A City Where the Ocean Sets the Menu

Vancouver's food identity is written by geography. The Pacific Ocean delivers some of the world's finest wild salmon, spot prawns, and Dungeness crab directly to restaurant kitchens—sometimes within hours of being pulled from the water. The mountains surrounding the city create microclimates perfect for everything from Fraser Valley berries to Okanagan wine grapes. But what truly distinguishes Vancouver's table is its position as Canada's gateway to Asia.

More than 40 percent of Vancouver's population identifies as Asian, making this one of the most authentically diverse food cities in North America. The Cantonese immigrants who arrived during the gold rush built a Chinatown that still serves century-old recipes. Japanese immigrants brought techniques that evolved into what many consider the best sushi outside Tokyo. More recent waves from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Korea, and the Philippines have layered even more complexity onto the scene. Add Indigenous food traditions that predate European contact by thousands of years—think candied salmon, bannock, and foraged seaweed—and you have a city where a single block might offer you five different cuisines, each made by someone whose grandmother taught them the recipe.

## The Essential Bites: Seven Dishes You Cannot Skip

**Wild Pacific Salmon** — Forget everything you know about the pale farmed stuff. Wild sockeye runs orange-red with natural astaxanthin, tasting like the ocean crossed with clean mountain streams. The flesh flakes in thick, buttery sheets whether it's been grilled with maple glaze, cold-smoked into silky lox, or flash-seared for sushi. Find it everywhere, but the best preparations come from restaurants in Yaletown that source directly from local fishermen, or Indigenous-owned establishments serving traditional preparations.

**Sushi and Sashimi** — Vancouver's proximity to both Japan and prime fishing waters creates something special. The rice carries that perfect vinegar bite; the fish arrives so fresh it practically still has a heartbeat. Look for omakase counters in Gastown where chefs slice through tuna belly that melts on contact with your tongue, or neighborhood joints along Commercial Drive where $15 gets you a bento box that would cost triple in most cities.

**Dim Sum** — Sunday morning in Chinatown or Richmond means joining families for the ritual of small plates and endless tea. Har gow (shrimp dumplings) arrive translucent and plump, the wrapper so thin you can see pink through it. Char siu bao split open to reveal pork glazed in that sticky-sweet red sauce. The crunch of turnip cakes hitting the griddle, the wobble of silken tofu, the surprise of a custard bun's molten center—this is breakfast as theater.

**Japadog** — Born on Vancouver's streets, this Japanese-style hot dog sounds gimmicky until your first bite. Imagine a quality smoky dog wrapped in nori, topped with teriyaki sauce, Japanese mayo, and crispy fried onions. Some versions come with miso, others with wasabi mayo, but all of them deliver that perfect collision of snap, umami, and unexpected harmony. Street carts cluster downtown near Burrard Station.

**Poutine** — Canada's gift to late-night cravings: thick-cut fries drowning in rich brown gravy, topped with fresh cheese curds that squeak against your teeth before melting into everything. The best versions use duck fat for the fries and a gravy made from actual stock. You'll find elevated takes in Yaletown's upscale lounges and greasy perfection at Granville Street spots after midnight.

**Spot Prawns** — Available only from May to June, these local crustaceans are Vancouver's answer to lobster. Restaurants serve them still twitching, meant to be eaten within hours of harvest. The meat is sweet, firm, and bears no resemblance to frozen prawns. Kitsilano restaurants overlooking the water specialize in preparations so simple—just lemon, butter, maybe some garlic—that the prawn does all the talking.

**Nanaimo Bars** — This no-bake dessert originated up-island but belongs to all of British Columbia. Three layers: a coconut-graham-walnut base, a thick custard-flavored buttercream middle, and a dark chocolate top that cracks satisfyingly under your fork. They're sweet enough to make your teeth tingle, perfect with strong coffee. Granville Island Public Market sells dozens of variations, including mint and mocha versions.

## Street Food: Where the Real Eating Happens

Vancouver's street food culture centers on food trucks, night markets, and one legendary public market. The food truck scene clusters downtown during weekday lunch hours—follow the lines near the Vancouver Art Gallery and you'll find everything from Korean fried chicken to Filipino sisig. The trucks rotate, but locals track their favorites on social media.

Summer transforms Richmond into street food central with the Richmond Night Market, running Friday through Sunday from May to October. The scale is staggering: hundreds of stalls serving Taiwanese shaved ice, takoyaki (octopus balls), grilled lamb skewers, tornado potatoes, and bubble waffles. Arrive hungry around 7 PM, wear comfortable shoes, and bring cash—many vendors don't take cards for items under $10.

For year-round grazing, Granville Island Public Market operates daily. The trick is visiting on a weekday morning before cruise ship crowds arrive. Work your way through the aisles: oysters shucked to order at the seafood counter, still-warm donuts from Lee's, samples of smoked salmon that'll convert anyone into a fish person.

## Where to Eat at Every Budget

**Under $55 per meal** — This budget gets you some of Vancouver's most memorable eating. In Chinatown, $12 buys a plate of hand-pulled noodles swimming in chili oil that coats your lips in the best way. Commercial Drive's old-school Italian cafes serve sandwiches on ciabatta so crusty it shatters, stuffed with capicola and provolone, for under $15. Ramen shops across the city ladle out bowls of tonkotsu with eggs marinated until the yolks turn amber—$16 for something that would cost twice that in Tokyo. Hunt down the no-frills sushi counters in strip malls near Main Street; $40 feeds two people embarrassingly well.

**$83–$120 per meal** — This is Vancouver's sweet spot. Yaletown's converted warehouse restaurants serve Pacific Rim cuisine—think miso-glazed sablefish with bok choy, or duck confit spring rolls—in spaces with exposed brick and Edison bulbs. Gastown's historic buildings house gastropubs where local craft beer meets upscale pub fare: beer-braised short ribs, crispy pork belly with apple mostarda. This range also covers high-end dim sum in Richmond, where you're paying for the theatrical cart service and dishes you won't find at casual spots—abalone, bird's nest soup, Peking duck carved tableside.

**$150+ per meal** — Vancouver's splurge-worthy restaurants earn the price tag with hyper-local tasting menus that change with fishing seasons and foraged ingredients. Expect dishes like sea urchin harvested that morning, served on house-baked sourdough; venison from Vancouver Island paired with chanterelles gathered from North Shore forests; desserts featuring Douglas fir tips and huckleberries. Book these months in advance, especially during summer. January's Dine Out Vancouver offers a loophole: many of these restaurants participate with $25–$65 prix fixe menus.

## For the Plant-Forward Crowd

Vancouver makes vegetarian and vegan eating genuinely easy. The city's wellness culture and Asian culinary influences mean you'll find creative plant-based options beyond sad salads. Buddhist vegetarian restaurants in Chinatown have perfected mock meats for decades—their "duck" made from layered tofu skin fools omnivores. Kitsilano's health-focused cafes build grain bowls with massaged kale, roasted beets, tahini dressings, and enough protein to fuel a mountain hike.

Look for vegetarian versions of poutine using mushroom gravy and vegan cheese, surprisingly satisfying. Japanese restaurants typically offer vegetable rolls and inari (sweet tofu pockets). Indian spots along Main Street serve complete thalis with multiple vegetable curries, dal, rice, and fresh-baked naan. The city's food trucks include dedicated vegan options serving jackfruit tacos and coconut-based soft serve.

## Eating Your Way Through the Neighborhoods

**Chinatown** is where you eat dim sum—full stop. Arrive before 11 AM on weekends or face hour-long waits. Beyond dim sum, this neighborhood holds hole-in-the-wall noodle shops, Hong Kong-style cafes serving baked pork chop rice, and bakeries with egg tarts pulled steaming from the oven.

**Gastown** delivers Vancouver's most atmospheric dining. Cobblestone streets and heritage buildings contain cocktail-forward restaurants and farm-to-table concepts. This is date night territory.

**Yaletown** skews upscale: waterfront patios, wine bars, and the kind of restaurants where reservations matter. Come here when you want to spend more money than you probably should on beautifully plated food.

**Kitsilano** feeds the active and health-conscious—organic juice bars, acai bowl shops, and brunch spots with lines wrapping around the block on sunny mornings. The beach views make everything taste better.

**Commercial Drive** is Vancouver at its most eclectic. Italian nonnas still run espresso bars next to Ethiopian restaurants, Vietnamese sandwich shops, and taco counters run by actual Mexican families. This neighborhood eats global without pretension.

**Granville Island Public Market** deserves its own category. Plan to spend a morning: start with coffee and a pastry, sample your way through cheese mongers and charcuterie vendors, grab oysters and ceviche for lunch, leave with locally made chocolates.

## How to Eat Like You Belong Here

Canadians tip 15 to 20 percent on the pre-tax total—landing toward 18 percent is standard for good service. Unlike Americans, Canadians don't tip on takeout or counter service, though tip jars have become more common. Most restaurants build gratuity into the bill for groups over six.

Reservations matter for dinner at mid-range and up restaurants, especially Thursday through Saturday. Lunch is more casual; walk-ins usually work. Dim sum doesn't take reservations—you show up, grab a number, and wait.

Dinner service starts around 5:30 PM, with prime time hitting 7 to 8 PM. Unlike some Asian cities, late-night dining options thin out after 10 PM except on Granville Street. Brunch runs from 10 AM to 2 PM and is practically a religion in Kitsilano.

One last note: Vancouver restaurants acknowledge they operate on the unceded territories of the Musqueam, Squamish, and Tsleil-Waututh Nations. Some menus now incorporate Indigenous ingredients—look for dishes featuring salmon prepared in traditional methods, bannock, or foraged salal berries. Ordering these dishes supports Indigenous food sovereignty efforts.
