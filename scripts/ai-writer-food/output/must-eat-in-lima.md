The first spoonful of *leche de tigre* hits like a wave breaking against the Miraflores cliffs — cold, electric, impossibly alive. It's 11 AM at a cevichería in Surquillo, and the morning fog hasn't lifted, but this tiger's milk — the citrus cure left behind after the fish is gone — is already burning through the gray. A squeeze of lime, a punch of ají limo, and somewhere underneath, the clean sweetness of the Pacific. This is Lima in a glass, and you haven't even started eating yet.

## The Dishes That Define Lima

#### Ceviche

There's a reason Peruvians treat ceviche like a religion. The dish is deceptively simple: chunks of raw corvina or lenguado, "cooked" in nothing but lime juice, tossed with slivers of red onion, a scattering of cilantro, and sliced ají limo peppers that bloom with heat on the back of your tongue. But the magic is in the timing. Great ceviche is made moments before it reaches your table — the fish still firm, the citrus cure just barely penetrating the flesh. Each bite should snap with freshness, then melt into something silky.

The genius is in the accompaniments. A thick slice of sweet potato, its earthy sweetness cutting through the acid. Giant kernels of choclo corn, starchy and satisfying. And always, always, a small dish of that *leche de tigre* on the side — the liquor you're meant to drink straight, a hangover cure and appetite igniter rolled into one.

The dish traces back to pre-Columbian times, when coastal communities cured fish in fermented chicha corn beer. The Spanish brought limes; Japanese immigrants brought the quick-cure technique. Lima ceviche is a collision of histories on a single plate.

Eat it before 2 PM — that's the rule. After midday, the fish has been sitting too long, the cure has gone too far. Head to La Mar in Miraflores for the polished version ($18–25), or brave the stalls at Mercado de Surquillo, where a bowl runs you $4 and the fishermen's wives make it fresh while you watch.

#### Lomo Saltado

If ceviche is Lima's ocean, lomo saltado is its heartland collision — Chinese wok technique meets Peruvian beef, and the result is one of the most satisfying plates in South America. Strips of tenderloin, seared hard and fast over screaming heat, tossed with fat wedges of tomato, red onion cut into moons, and a splash of soy sauce that caramelizes against the wok. The smoke clings to every piece.

And then: french fries. Not on the side. In the stir-fry. Crispy batons that soak up the juices, going soft in places, staying crunchy in others. It shouldn't work. It works completely.

This is *chifa* — the culinary tradition born when Chinese immigrants arrived in Peru in the 19th century to work the guano mines and railroads. They brought soy sauce, ginger, and wok cooking. Peru gave them potatoes, ají peppers, and beef. Lomo saltado is the marriage certificate.

Find it everywhere, from white-tablecloth restaurants to hole-in-the-wall chifas in the Centro Histórico. The best versions come from places where the wok hasn't cooled in decades. Expect to pay $8–15 at a solid neighborhood spot. Order it *con bastante jugo* — with extra sauce — and drag every last fry through the puddle at the bottom of the plate.

#### Anticuchos

Night falls, and the smoke starts. Along the streets of Miraflores and Barranco, metal carts roll out, grills fired with charcoal, and the smell hits you from half a block away — caramelized, meaty, slightly charred. This is *anticucho* territory.

These are beef heart skewers, and before you recoil, understand: when cooked right, beef heart is the most tender, iron-rich, almost buttery meat you'll ever taste. Marinated overnight in vinegar, cumin, garlic, and ají panca — a dried pepper that stains everything a deep brick red — the hearts grill quickly, just until the outside chars and the inside stays pink. A brush of the marinade sizzles on top. You eat them standing up, right there by the cart.

The dish has roots in the Andes, where Indigenous communities ate all parts of the animal long before the Spanish arrived. Enslaved Africans and their descendants later made anticuchos their own, selling them on Lima's streets for centuries. Today, the best cart vendors — *anticucheras* — are still often women, passing down recipes through generations.

Pair your skewers with a boiled potato, a piece of choclo, and a smear of *ají* verde. Three skewers run about $3–4 from a street cart. Look for carts with a crowd. The smoke should be fragrant, not bitter. Your fingers will smell like charcoal for hours. This is correct.

#### Causa

Cold, bright, and architectural — causa is the dish that photographs best but tastes even better. Imagine a terrine made entirely of yellow potato, mashed with lime juice and ají amarillo until it turns the color of egg yolk. The potato is layered — a base, a filling of shredded chicken or crab or octopus with avocado, then another cap of that golden mash. Sometimes it's stacked into cylinders. Sometimes it's molded into elaborate shapes. Always, it's served cold.

The texture is impossibly smooth, almost creamy, with a gentle heat from the pepper and a brightness that keeps the richness in check. The fillings vary: *causa limeña* often comes with chicken salad bound in mayonnaise; seafood versions might layer tuna or pulpo with slices of ripe palta.

The name supposedly comes from the phrase *kausaq* in Quechua, meaning "that which gives life." During Peru's war of independence, women sold causa on the streets to raise money *for the cause* — hence the Spanish pun that stuck.

You'll find causa as an appetizer at almost any Peruvian restaurant, running $6–12 depending on the filling. The best versions use freshly cooked potatoes and just enough ají to wake up the palate without overwhelming the delicate seafood inside.

#### Ají de Gallina

This is comfort food for a city that rarely gets cold. Shredded chicken — historically made with old hens, gallinas, too tough for anything else — blanketed in a thick sauce the color of golden honey. The sauce is where the magic lives: ají amarillo peppers blended with walnuts, Parmesan cheese, milk-soaked bread, and a hit of evaporated milk that makes everything impossibly rich.

The flavor is warm, slightly nutty, with a lingering pepper hum that never turns aggressive. It's served over white rice, always, with a few black olives and a hard-boiled egg sliced on top. The dish has echoes of Spanish escabeche and Moorish nut sauces, filtered through centuries of Peruvian kitchens.

Find it at any *menú* restaurant — the lunch spots serving three-course meals to office workers and taxi drivers. Order the *menú del día* around noon, and there's a good chance ají de gallina is on the rotation. A full lunch with soup, main, and drink runs $3–5. At sit-down restaurants, the plate costs $8–12 and arrives looking almost too pretty to disturb. Disturb it anyway.

#### Picarones

The sweet finish. Picarones are doughnuts, technically, but unlike any you've had — made from mashed sweet potato and squash, formed into wobbly rings, and fried until they puff into golden, craggy clouds. They shatter on the outside, turn tender and almost custardy within.

But the syrup. *Chancaca* — raw cane sugar cooked down with cinnamon, cloves, and orange peel until it thickens into something dark, sticky, and dangerously close to molasses. It pools on the plate, and you drag each piece of picarón through it, letting the syrup seep into every crevice.

The dish dates back to colonial Lima, when enslaved people and free Black communities adapted Spanish *buñuelos* using local sweet potatoes. The fryers still set up on corners after dark, especially near the Plaza Mayor and along the Barranco waterfront. A plate of three or four runs $2–3. Watch them pull the rings from the oil, golden and steaming, and know that you'll burn your tongue. It's worth it.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is sacred in Lima. The biggest meal happens between noon and 3 PM, and the whole city slows down for it. Chase the *menú* — a set lunch offered at family restaurants and hole-in-the-wall spots that includes soup, a main, and a drink for a handful of soles. This is where you eat like a Limeño, surrounded by construction workers and accountants and abuelitas who've been coming for decades.

Share everything. Peruvians order plates for the table, not the individual. A ceviche to start, a lomo saltado to split, a causa to pass around. Asking for separate checks marks you as a tourist faster than your accent.

And never, ever trust ceviche after 2 PM or from a place without a crowd. The fish should have arrived that morning. When in doubt, follow the locals to the stalls they trust.
