Three days in Dubrovnik with a singular mission: eat everything worth eating. I walked the marble streets until my feet ached, ate black risotto until my teeth stained purple, and tracked down the octopus peka that locals actually travel across town for. This is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I started.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Croatians eat late — lunch rarely before 1 PM, dinner often at 9 PM. Most restaurants accept cards, but carry cash for market vendors and konobas (taverns) in the port area. Tipping isn't obligatory, but leaving 10% for good service is appreciated. Book dinner reservations for Old Town restaurants at least a day ahead during peak season — walk-ins after 7 PM are a gamble you'll lose. Tap water is excellent; drink it freely.

## Day 1: The Old Town Initiation

**Morning — Gundulićeva Poljana Market**

Skip the hotel breakfast. By 8 AM, the market at Gundulićeva Poljana in Old Town is alive with vendors selling figs, cherries, and homemade sheep's cheese wrapped in wax paper. Buy a wedge of Paški sir (aged sheep's cheese from Pag Island) and some dried figs — about €8 total — and eat them on the stone steps while the square wakes up. The cheese is salty, crystalline, perfect with the jammy sweetness of the figs.

For coffee, walk two minutes to one of the cafes lining the square. Order a kava (espresso) and a burek — the flaky phyllo pastry filled with cheese or meat that's Croatia's answer to a breakfast sandwich. A coffee and burek will run you €4–5, and you'll understand why locals eat this standing at the counter.

*Cost: €12–15*

**Afternoon — Lunch in the Walled City**

By 1 PM, find a table at a konoba tucked into the alleys behind Stradun. This is where you order your first **buzara** — shrimp or mussels swimming in a garlicky bath of white wine, olive oil, and breadcrumbs. The sauce demands bread for soaking. Order a half-liter of house white from Pelješac peninsula — the local Pošip grape drinks like a leaner Burgundy.

After lunch, walk off the wine by climbing to the city walls. The afternoon light makes the terracotta rooftops glow, and you'll burn enough calories to justify what's coming at dinner. Don't rush this — the walls take about 90 minutes at a contemplative pace.

For a mid-afternoon snack, descend near Buža Gate and find a vendor selling fresh orange juice or a scoop of fig gelato. The combination of heat and stone radiates back at you; something cold is mandatory.

*Cost: €35–50*

**Evening — Seafood at the Old Port**

Dinner at a terrace restaurant overlooking the Old Port. This is the night for **crni rižot** — black risotto stained midnight purple with squid ink and studded with tender cuttlefish. The good versions are creamy without being gluey, briny without tasting like an aquarium.

Start with a plate of those famous **Ston oysters**, harvested from the bay about an hour north. They're smaller than French oysters, brinier, served simply with lemon. Six oysters, the risotto, a bottle of Plavac Mali red, and you're looking at €60–80 depending on the restaurant's view tax.

*Cost: €60–85*

**Day 1 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€110 | Mid-range ~€150 | Splurge ~€250

## Day 2: The Port and the Peka

**Morning — Gruž Market**

Leave the cruise ship crowds to the Old Town and head to Gruž, the working port area where locals actually shop. The market here is bigger, cheaper, and entirely unconcerned with tourists. Arrive by 9 AM and wander the stalls: pyramids of tomatoes, bundles of wild herbs, honey from Mljet, olive oil sold from plastic jugs.

Breakfast is simple: find the stand selling warm bread and pair it with prosciutto from the Dalmatian hinterland — drier and more intensely flavored than Italian prosciutto. Add a paper bag of maraschino cherries if they're in season. Total damage: €10.

*Cost: €10–12*

**Afternoon — The Peka Hunt**

This is the day you eat **peka**, and it requires planning. Peka — lamb, veal, or octopus slow-roasted for hours under an iron bell with potatoes and vegetables — isn't fast food. You need to call ahead, usually by 10 AM the same day.

Head to Lapad, the beach peninsula northwest of Old Town, where family-run konobas specialize in this dish. The octopus version is transcendent: tentacles turned impossibly tender, potatoes soaked in olive oil and octopus juices, everything caramelized and smoky from the wood fire.

This is a meal for two or more — order one peka (about €60–70), a salad of tomatoes and local olive oil, and let the afternoon extend. You're not going anywhere for a while.

After lunch, walk the Lapad promenade along the water. There's a rhythm here that Old Town doesn't have — locals swimming, kids jumping off rocks, grandmothers watching everything from benches.

*Cost: €70–90 (split for two)*

**Evening — Ploče and Pašticada**

Return east toward Ploče, the upscale neighborhood just outside the city walls, where restaurants serve with a bit more polish. Tonight is for **pašticada**, the Dalmatian Sunday-dinner dish: beef braised for hours in wine, prunes, and bacon, served over homemade gnocchi. It's rich, sweet-savory, the kind of dish grandmothers are famous for.

Find a restaurant with a terrace looking back at the walls, lit golden after dark. Order the pašticada, a bottle of Dingač (the serious red wine from Pelješac), and finish with **rozata** — Dubrovnik's version of crème caramel, scented with rose liqueur. This is the dessert to order everywhere; the good ones have a bitter caramel edge.

*Cost: €55–80*

**Day 2 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€135 | Mid-range ~€190 | Splurge ~€320

## Day 3: Islands and the Final Feast

**Morning — Lokrum Island Picnic**

Take the first ferry to Lokrum Island (departures start at 9 AM from the Old Port, €20 round trip). Before you board, stop at the bakery near Pile Gate for a bag of kroštule — the twisted fried dough dusted with powdered sugar that's Dubrovnik's answer to the doughnut.

Pack a simple picnic from the market: cheese, cured meat, bread, a bottle of wine. Lokrum has no restaurants, just rabbits, peacocks, and the ruins of a Benedictine monastery. Find a spot along the rocky shore, swim in the salt lake, eat your picnic. This is the quietest meal you'll have all trip.

*Cost: €25–35 (including ferry)*

**Afternoon — Return and Recover**

Back in Old Town by early afternoon. Before dinner, squeeze in one more hidden meal: through a small door in the city walls near the Jesuit Stairs lies Buža Bar, clinging to the cliffs with nothing between you and the Adriatic. This isn't about food — order a cold Ožujsko beer and maybe some sardines if they're grilling them. It's about the view and the absurdity of a bar carved into the fortress walls.

*Cost: €15–20*

**Evening — The Farewell Dinner**

Your last dinner should be a recapitulation of everything Dubrovnik does well. Find a restaurant with a terrace somewhere between Ploče and Old Town — the view should be extravagant.

Start with oysters again, because you can. Then **buzara**, but this time with škampi (langoustines) — sweeter, more delicate than shrimp. For the main: if you didn't try the fish prepared "Dalmatian style" yet, this is your moment. Order a whole grilled brancin (sea bass) or orada (sea bream), served simply with olive oil, garlic, and chard. The fish should be so fresh it tastes like the sea without tasting fishy.

End with rozata once more, and a small glass of prošek — the sweet dessert wine that predates prosecco by centuries. The bill will sting — €100 or more — but you're eating what the Adriatic offers at its best.

*Cost: €100–140*

**Day 3 Total Food Spend:** Budget ~€140 | Mid-range ~€195 | Splurge ~€350

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Order peka by 10 AM.** Restaurants need notice to fire up the wood and slow-roast for hours. Call ahead or ask your hotel to arrange it.
- **Eat lunch as your big meal.** Dinner prices at the same restaurants run 20–30% higher, and you'll get better service at 1 PM than 8 PM.
- **Carry a phrase.** "Hvala" (HVAH-lah) means thank you. Use it liberally. Servers and market vendors soften immediately.
- **Avoid Stradun for meals.** The main marble street is for walking, not eating. One block into any alley and quality doubles while prices drop.
- **Watch for the wet marble.** Those polished limestone streets become skating rinks after rain or morning cleaning. Walk slowly, especially after wine.
