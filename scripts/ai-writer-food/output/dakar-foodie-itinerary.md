Three days in Dakar taught me that Senegalese food operates on its own clock and its own terms. Lunch is the main event — massive, communal, built around rice and fish and hours of slow cooking. Breakfast is quick and sweet. Dinner can be anything from grilled meat on the street to elegant French-influenced plates by the ocean. Plan accordingly, and you'll eat extraordinarily well.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Dakar runs on cash — bring CFA francs and don't expect cards outside upscale restaurants. Lunch happens between 1–3pm and is sacred; many spots close after the main service. Tipping isn't mandatory but rounding up is appreciated. Greet everyone — a simple "Salaam aleikum" before ordering opens doors. Dress modestly, especially in market areas. And yes, you'll eat with your right hand sometimes. Embrace it.

## Day 1: The Plateau and the Sea

The historic center is where Dakar's culinary identity makes the most sense — colonial architecture, ocean breezes, and the slow collision of French technique with Senegalese soul.

### Morning

Start in the Plateau with a Senegalese breakfast at a local café near the old post office. Order **café Touba** — a spiced coffee with hints of clove and selim pepper that'll rewire your brain if you're expecting French roast. Pair it with **fataya**, small fried pastries stuffed with spiced fish or meat. They're sold from glass cases in bakeries and from women with baskets near the cathedral. The combination is perfect: bitter, sweet, savory, oily, all before 9am.

*Where*: Any of the small coffee shops along Avenue Pompidou, or grab fataya from the vendors near Cathédrale du Souvenir Africain.
*Cost*: $3–5 for coffee and two fataya.

### Afternoon

Lunch is **thiéboudienne** — the national dish, and the meal Dakar does better than anywhere else on earth. This is fish (usually thiof, a local grouper) braised with tomatoes, tamarind, and a dense spice paste called rof, served over broken rice that's absorbed every bit of the cooking liquid. The vegetables matter: bitter eggplant, sweet potato, cassava, cabbage, all cooked until they surrender.

Walk to Marché Sandaga after eating — not for the food stalls, but for the sensory overload. Fabric sellers, phone vendors, the controlled chaos of Dakar's commercial heart. You won't eat here, but you'll understand something about the city's appetite.

*Where*: Look for a neighborhood **thiéboudienne** spot in the Plateau — many are unmarked, just follow the lunch crowd. The dish comes in two styles: *thiéboudienne rouge* (tomato-based) or *blanc* (without tomato). Get the rouge.
*Cost*: $6–10 for a massive portion meant for sharing.

### Evening

Dinner on the Waterfront as the sun drops over the Atlantic. This is where Dakar's French colonial past shows up on the plate — grilled fish with sophisticated sauces, properly dressed salads, wine lists. But the best kitchens here fold Senegalese ingredients into the framework: **yassa poulet** refined into something elegant, or fresh catch with a thiof-based sauce.

The Waterfront restaurants range from tourist-facing to genuinely excellent. Sit outside. Order grilled fish, whatever came in that day. Ask for **ataaya** (sweet mint tea, served in three rounds) after your meal — it's the proper way to end.

*Cost*: $25–45 for dinner with drinks at a mid-range Waterfront restaurant; $60+ if you're going upscale.

**Day 1 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$35 | Mid-range ~$60 | Splurge ~$110

## Day 2: Médina and the Neighborhood Table

Leave the French-influenced center behind. The Médina is where Dakar cooks for itself — market stalls, family-run lunch spots, and the kind of food that doesn't care whether you photograph it.

### Morning

The Médina wakes up with **thiakry** — sweet millet couscous mixed with yogurt, vanilla, and sometimes baobab fruit. It's breakfast and dessert combined, sold in plastic bags from women who've been making the same recipe for decades. Rich, creamy, subtly fermented. Eat it standing on a corner with the neighborhood.

Follow it with a small glass of **bissap** (hibiscus juice) or **bouye** (baobab juice) from any of the drink sellers. Both are aggressively sweet and perfect.

*Where*: Walk through the Médina's residential streets in the morning — thiakry sellers are everywhere near the smaller mosques.
*Cost*: $2–4 for thiakry and juice.

### Afternoon

This is cooking class territory if you want to learn rather than just eat. Several homes in the Médina offer hands-on sessions where you'll shop at the market, pound spices in a mortar, and learn the patience required for proper **mafé** — peanut sauce braised with lamb or beef until the oil rises and the sauce turns glossy.

If you'd rather eat than cook, find a neighborhood lunch spot serving mafé or **caldou** (a lighter fish-and-lime soup, delicate where thiéboudienne is bold). The hidden gems here don't have signs — look for plastic chairs, a woman in charge, and crowds between 1–2pm.

*Where*: Ask at your hotel for a cooking class recommendation, or wander until you find a lunch crowd.
*Cost*: Cooking class $30–50 including the meal; neighborhood lunch $5–8.

### Evening

Dinner in the Médina means grilled meat. As the sun sets, the **dibiteries** light their coals — these are lamb specialists, roasting whole legs and serving them chopped with raw onions, mustard, and bread. The smell alone will find you. Stand at the counter, order by pointing, eat with your hands. This is Dakar at its most direct.

For a sit-down alternative, seek out a spot serving **thiéré** (millet couscous with vegetables and meat) — it's what families eat on Friday nights, and some restaurants offer it later in the week.

*Cost*: Dibiterie meal $8–15; sit-down thiéré $12–20.

**Day 2 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$35 | Mid-range ~$55 | Splurge ~$85

## Day 3: The Best of What Dakar Does

Your final day should feel like a greatest hits — the dishes you'd miss most if you left tomorrow, eaten in the places that do them best.

### Morning

Return to **café Touba**, this time seeking out the best version you can find. The Arts Quarter near the Village des Arts has excellent options — small cafés where the coffee is brewed strong and the conversation is slow. Pair it with **accara** (black-eyed pea fritters) if you can find them fresh from the oil. Crispy outside, fluffy inside, perfect for dipping into spicy sauce.

This is also your morning for the **Marché Kermel** — a more manageable market than Sandaga, with beautiful produce and colonial-era architecture. Walk the aisles, buy fruit, watch Dakar shop.

*Where*: Café near Village des Arts, then walk to Marché Kermel in the Plateau.
*Cost*: $5–7 for coffee, accara, and fresh fruit.

### Afternoon

Your last proper lunch should be the dish you can't stop thinking about. For most people, that's thiéboudienne again — but the best version you can find, from a cook who's spent thirty years perfecting her rof paste. Ask around. Taxi drivers know. Hotel staff know. Everyone has an opinion about who makes the best.

Alternatively, hunt down **ceebu yapp** — the meat version of the national dish, with lamb instead of fish. It's richer, heavier, and eaten less frequently. Finding a great version feels like finding a secret.

Spend the late afternoon walking the Corniche — the coastal road with views of the Atlantic. Stop for **jus de gingembre** (ginger juice, fiery and sweet) from a street vendor. Let lunch settle.

*Where*: Ask locally for the best thiéboudienne — it's a conversation starter. The Corniche runs along the Waterfront.
*Cost*: $8–12 for lunch; $2 for juice.

### Evening

End with a meal worth remembering. The best tables in Dakar combine everything — impeccable grilled fish, Senegalese spicing, French service, Atlantic views. Splurge on a place in the Almadies district or along the Waterfront where the kitchen treats local ingredients with respect but isn't afraid of elegance.

Order **yassa poisson** (the fish version, onions caramelized for hours in lemon and mustard), or ask the chef what's best tonight. Finish with hibiscus sorbet or sweet millet if they have it. Drink Senegalese beer — La Gazelle is excellent — or Flag. Take your time. You've earned this.

*Cost*: $35–60 for a memorable final dinner; $80+ if you're doing it properly.

**Day 3 Total Food Spend**: Budget ~$50 | Mid-range ~$75 | Splurge ~$150

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Lunch is everything**: Plan your day around where you'll eat at 1pm. This is when the serious cooking happens.
- **Follow the smoke**: In the evening, dibiteries advertise themselves by smell. Walk toward grilling meat and you'll find the good stuff.
- **Eat with your right hand**: When sharing from a communal plate (and you will), only use your right hand. Watch locals for technique — there's an art to scooping rice efficiently.
- **Stay for the three teas**: If someone offers ataaya, don't leave after one glass. The ritual requires three rounds, each sweeter than the last. Refusing is rude; staying is the best way to understand Senegalese hospitality.
