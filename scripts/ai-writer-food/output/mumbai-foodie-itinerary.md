Three days in Mumbai taught me that this city doesn't just feed you — it overwhelms you with flavor at every turn, from the 5 AM chai wallahs to the midnight kebab stalls. My strategy was simple: eat constantly, walk everywhere possible, and never say no to something handed to me from a street cart. What follows is the itinerary I wish someone had handed me before I landed.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Mumbai eats late — lunch rarely happens before 1 PM, and dinner often starts at 9 PM or later. Most street food stalls are cash only (keep ₹100 and ₹500 notes handy), though restaurants accept cards. Tipping 10% is standard at sit-down places. Remove your shoes at any religious site, and use your right hand when eating with your fingers. Carry wet wipes — you'll need them after every vada pav.

## Day 1: South Mumbai — Where the City Began

### Morning: The Irani Café Ritual

Start in **Colaba** at one of the remaining Irani cafés, those Persian-influenced tea houses that defined 20th-century Mumbai. Order bun maska (buttered bread roll) with cutting chai — the half-cup portion that's meant to be consumed standing at the counter. The bread should be slightly sweet, the butter obscene in quantity. Follow it with akuri, the Parsi-style scrambled eggs cooked soft with onions, tomatoes, and green chilies. This is Mumbai's colonial-era breakfast, still served on marble tables under ceiling fans that have been spinning since independence.

**Where**: Café Mondegar or Olympia Coffee House, both walking distance from Gateway of India.
**Cost**: ₹150–250 (~$2–3)

### Afternoon: The Street Food Circuit

By noon, walk north through **Fort** toward Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus. This is where Mumbai's street food density reaches critical mass. Your mission: **Bhel Puri** from one of the vendors near CST station. Watch them assemble it — puffed rice, sev (crispy noodles), chopped onions, potatoes, raw mango, tamarind chutney, green chutney, all tossed in a metal bowl and served in a paper cone. The texture contrast is everything: crunchy, soft, tangy, sweet, spicy in every bite. Eat it fast before the puffed rice goes soggy.

Continue to **Crawford Market** for the sensory overload. This wholesale market sells everything from imported cheese to fighting roosters, but you're here for the fruit section and the chaos. Buy a fresh coconut (₹40), drink the water, then have the vendor hack it open so you can scoop out the flesh.

For a proper lunch, find a seat at Badshah Cold Drinks near Crawford Market and order their **Falooda** — yes, for lunch. This rose milk dessert drink layered with vermicelli, basil seeds, ice cream, and rose syrup is substantial enough to count as a meal. It's absurdly pink and absolutely delicious.

**Cost**: ₹300–450 (~$4–5) for the full afternoon circuit

### Evening: The Colaba Dinner

Return to **Colaba** as the evening crowds emerge. For dinner, you have a choice that defines your Mumbai experience. Budget-minded? Hit Bademiya, the legendary late-night kebab stall that sets up after dark near the Taj Hotel. The seekh kebabs wrapped in roomali roti are smoky, juicy, and cost almost nothing.

For a proper sit-down meal, book a table at Trishna in Fort for their butter garlic crab — a dish so famous it needs no description, just surrender. The crab comes swimming in garlic butter sauce that demands bread for soaking. This is splurge territory, but you'll remember it for years.

**Budget dinner**: ₹250–400 (~$3–5)
**Splurge dinner**: ₹2,500–4,000 (~$30–50)

**Day 1 Total**: Budget ~₹700 ($8) · Mid ~₹2,000 ($25) · Splurge ~₹4,500 ($55)

## Day 2: Bandra and the Western Suburbs

### Morning: The Keema Pav Pilgrimage

Take an Uber north to **Bandra**, Mumbai's most eclectic neighborhood — equal parts Bollywood wealth, old Catholic fishing villages, and cafe culture. Start at Olympia Coffee House (yes, another one — the chain spans the city) or Cafe Lucky for **Keema Pav**, the dish that Mumbai does better than anywhere else in India. Spiced minced mutton cooked until nearly dry, rich with ghee, topped with a fried egg, served with soft bread rolls for scooping. Order it "medium spicy" unless you've been training.

**Cost**: ₹180–300 (~$2–4)

### Afternoon: Hidden Bandra and Cooking Class Option

Walk off breakfast through Bandra's lanes. Stop at Bandstand Promenade to look at the Bollywood star homes across the water, then loop back toward Hill Road.

For a deeper dive, consider a cooking class — several home cooks in Bandra offer 3–4 hour sessions where you'll learn to make vada pav from scratch, grind your own masalas, and understand why every Mumbai household guards their chutney recipe. Expect to pay ₹3,000–5,000 ($35–60) for a quality class with market visit included.

If you skip the class, spend the afternoon eating your way through Bandra's street food. Find a **Bombay Sandwich** vendor near Linking Road — these aren't fancy, just white bread layered with cucumber, beetroot, potato, cheese, and absolutely drenched in green chutney, then pressed until crispy. For ₹50 you get something that shouldn't work but absolutely does.

Make time for **Khotachiwadi**, a hidden Portuguese-style heritage village tucked into Girgaon, technically south of Bandra but worth the detour today. The narrow lanes lined with wooden bungalows feel like stepping back 100 years. A few houses run small lunch services — if you see a sign, stop and eat.

**Cost**: ₹400–700 (~$5–8) for snacking, ₹3,000–5,000 ($35–60) for cooking class

### Evening: The Bandra Rooftop Scene

Bandra comes alive at night with rooftop lounges and live music venues. For dinner, find **Pav Bhaji** at one of the stalls near Pali Naka — this buttery vegetable mash served with toasted, butter-soaked bread rolls is Mumbai's comfort food masterpiece. The best versions use so much butter the bhaji practically glistens. Watch them mash and season it on the flat griddle, then serve it on steel plates still sizzling.

For a proper sit-down with views, head to one of Lower Parel's converted mill restaurants — High Street Phoenix area has several excellent options where you can order coastal Maharashtrian seafood while looking out over the city lights. Try bombil fry (Bombay duck, actually a fish) or prawn koliwada, the battered and fried prawns that fishermen in this city have been perfecting for generations.

**Street dinner**: ₹200–350 (~$2–4)
**Rooftop dinner**: ₹2,000–3,500 (~$25–40)

**Day 2 Total**: Budget ~₹780 ($9) · Mid ~₹2,500 ($30) · Splurge ~₹6,000 ($75)

## Day 3: The Greatest Hits Farewell

### Morning: Juhu Beach Dawn Patrol

Your final day starts at **Juhu Beach**, the long sandy strip where Mumbai families come to play and eat. Arrive by 7 AM to beat the heat and find the chai wallahs already set up. Order a cutting chai and sit on the sand to watch the morning walkers.

By 8 AM, the pav bhaji and chaat vendors fire up their griddles. This is your chance for a second round of bhel puri, this time the Juhu beach style — slightly sweeter, served with puri crisps instead of just puffed rice. Follow it with pani puri (golgappa), those hollow crispy spheres filled with spiced water that you eat in one explosive bite.

**Cost**: ₹150–250 (~$2–3)

### Afternoon: Sassoon Docks and South Mumbai Snacking

Head south to **Sassoon Docks**, Mumbai's working fishing harbor that most tourists skip. Arrive mid-morning to see the catch being sorted — pomfret, bombil, prawns, crabs — and the Koli fisherwomen negotiating prices. The docks now host a street art project, so the industrial chaos is punctuated by murals. No food here, but it's the most honest look at where Mumbai's seafood actually comes from.

Walk to nearby Banganga Tank in Malabar Hill for the visual contrast — an ancient freshwater tank surrounded by temples, completely peaceful despite being in the city center. Then descend to Marine Drive for an afternoon **Vada Pav** from one of the stalls near Chowpatty Beach.

This is it — the dish that defines Mumbai street food. A spiced potato fritter, deep-fried until crispy, stuffed into a pav roll with green and red chutney, sometimes with fried green chilies on the side. It costs ₹20–30 and delivers more satisfaction than meals costing fifty times that amount. Eat it standing, watching the Arabian Sea, preferably at golden hour.

**Cost**: ₹300–500 (~$4–6)

### Evening: The Final Feast

Your last dinner in Mumbai should match the city's ambition. Return to **Colaba** or head to the restaurant scene in Lower Parel for your finale.

For seafood, Mahesh Lunch Home (despite the name, excellent at dinner) serves coastal Karnataka-style fish that will recalibrate your expectations — try the butter garlic crab or the clam sukka.

For something special, Masala Library in Bandra-Kurla Complex offers a modernist take on Indian cuisine, with tasting menus that reinterpret street food through fine-dining technique. The pani puri here comes spherified; the vada pav is deconstructed. It sounds gimmicky but actually works, a celebration of the flavors you've been eating all trip.

Wherever you eat, end the night at Marine Drive watching the Queen's Necklace — that arc of lights along the bay — and processing three days of relentless, glorious eating.

**Final dinner**: Budget ₹500–800 (~$6–10) · Mid ₹2,000–3,000 (~$25–35) · Splurge ₹6,000–10,000 (~$75–120)

**Day 3 Total**: Budget ~₹950 ($11) · Mid ~₹3,000 ($35) · Splurge ~₹10,500 ($130)

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Timing matters**: Street food vendors have peak hours. Vada pav stalls are best mid-morning or evening; kebab carts don't appear until after 8 PM. Showing up at 3 PM means eating leftovers.

- **The right hand rule**: Use your right hand exclusively for eating, passing food, and paying. Left hand is considered unclean. This isn't optional at traditional restaurants.

- **Spice negotiation**: "Medium spicy" at a Mumbai street stall is still aggressive by most standards. Start there and adjust. Saying "kam mirchi" (less chili) won't offend anyone.

- **Monsoon strategy**: June through September, street food safety declines and flooding makes navigation miserable. If you must visit then, stick to established restaurants and bring waterproof shoes.
