The first bite catches you off guard. You're perched on a terrace in Oia, the Aegean glittering impossibly blue below, when a golden fritter arrives still crackling from the oil. You bite through the crisp shell and suddenly your mouth floods with something you weren't expecting: pure, concentrated tomato — sweeter and more intense than any tomato you've ever tasted — mixed with fresh mint and the faint burn of onion. This is tomatokeftedes, and this is the moment you understand that Santorini's food isn't just Greek food. It's something the volcano made.

## The Dishes That Define Santorini

#### Tomatokeftedes — The Fritter That Tastes Like Sunshine

These aren't tomato fritters in the way you might imagine them. Santorini's cherry tomatoes — small, wrinkled, almost ugly little things — have adapted to the island's volcanic soil and near-total lack of rain by concentrating their sugars to an almost jam-like intensity. When local cooks grate them into a batter with fresh mint, onion, and a whisper of flour, then drop spoonfuls into sizzling olive oil, something magical happens. The exterior shatters. The interior stays soft and sweet-savory, with that herbal brightness cutting through the richness.

Every taverna serves them, but the best versions come from places where the cook's grandmother probably made them the same way. Look for them at seaside spots in Amoudi Bay or at family-run places in Megalochori, where tourists rarely venture. They arrive hot, usually four or five to a plate, sometimes with a cooling dollop of tzatziki alongside. Around €7–10 for a portion that should be an appetizer but often becomes the thing you can't stop eating.

#### Fava — The Humble Puree That Steals the Show

Forget everything you know about split pea soup. Santorini's fava is made from a specific yellow lentil grown only on this island, and when it's cooked down and whipped with olive oil, the result is a puree so creamy and earthy it borders on decadent. It arrives in a shallow bowl, sunshine-yellow, with a pool of grassy local olive oil forming a moat around the edges. Raw onion rings and capers dot the surface. You drag bread through it and wonder how something so simple can taste so complete.

The dish dates back centuries — this is peasant food, sustenance food, the kind of thing that fed families through lean winters. But the volcanic soil gives these lentils a sweetness that elevates them beyond humble. The onion adds bite. The capers contribute tiny salt bombs. The olive oil ties everything together with that peppery, almost grassy finish that good Greek oil carries.

Find it everywhere, but pay attention to texture. The best versions are silky, almost mousse-like, never grainy. A good-sized portion runs €6–9, and it's the perfect thing to order while you're still deciding what else to eat.

#### Fresh Grilled Octopus — Tender Char Over Aegean Blue

You'll see them first hanging on lines outside tavernas, draped like purple-red laundry drying in the sun. This is part of the tenderizing process — the sun and salt air working on the flesh before it ever hits the grill. When it does, the char happens fast and hot, leaving the exterior slightly crispy while the inside stays impossibly tender.

A proper Santorini octopus arrives on a simple white plate: thick tentacles cut into chunks, glossy with olive oil, scattered with oregano, a lemon wedge waiting on the side. You squeeze the lemon, you bite, and the texture is what gets you — yielding but with just enough resistance, smoky from the grill, briny from the sea it came from that morning. This isn't rubbery calamari. This is octopus as it's meant to be eaten.

The best octopus comes from places with their own fishing boats or close relationships with local fishermen. Amoudi Bay, at the bottom of Oia's cliff, has several tavernas right on the water where the octopus was swimming hours before you eat it. Expect to pay €15–22 for a plate, more at sunset-view spots. Worth every euro.

#### Moussaka — Layers of Comfort Built for Sharing

This is the dish Greek grandmothers argue about. Every family has their version, their ratio of eggplant to meat to béchamel, their secret addition of cinnamon or nutmeg or a splash of red wine in the meat sauce. What arrives at your table is a golden-topped casserole, bubbling at the edges, the béchamel bronzed and slightly puffed from the oven.

You cut through the layers: that creamy top giving way to spiced ground meat, then soft slices of eggplant that have absorbed the meat juices and turned silky. It's rich in the way that makes you slow down, take smaller bites, actually taste. The eggplant brings an almost smoky sweetness. The meat is warm with cinnamon and allspice. The béchamel is there not for richness alone but to bind everything into something cohesive, something that feels like home even if you've never been here before.

Order it at traditional tavernas in Fira or Pyrgos village, where the kitchen has been making the same recipe for decades. It comes in individual portions (€12–16) but feeds like a meal meant for sharing with someone you like.

#### Souvlaki — The Perfect Meal in Your Hands

Late night in Fira, after the sunset crowds have thinned and the bars are filling up, this is what you want. A warm pita, soft and slightly charred from the grill, wrapped around chunks of pork or chicken that have been marinating in olive oil, lemon, and oregano before hitting the flames. Tzatziki — cool, garlicky, thick with cucumber — gets smeared inside. Tomatoes and onions add freshness. Maybe some fries get tucked in there too, because this is Greece and fries go inside the pita here.

You eat it standing, or walking, or sitting on a bench watching the lights of the caldera villages sparkle across the water. Juice drips down your wrist. The meat is slightly charred at the edges, juicy in the middle. The garlic from the tzatziki hits your nose with every bite. This is €3.50–5 depending on where you are, and it's arguably the best meal-to-euro ratio on the island.

Street souvlaki spots in Fira stay open late, feeding the post-bar crowd. Look for the places with lines of locals, not just tourists.

#### Grilled Sardines — Simple, Salty, Unforgettable

Small fish, big flavor. Sardines come off the grill whole, their skin blistered and salty, their flesh oily and rich in the way that makes you understand why people around the Mediterranean have been eating them for millennia. You eat them with your hands, pulling the meat away from the spine, squeezing lemon over each bite. A chunk of bread to wipe up the oil on the plate. A glass of Assyrtiko to cut through the richness.

This is lunch at a beach taverna in Kamari or Perissa — black sand under your feet, the sea right there, the fish probably caught within sight of where you're sitting. Around €10–14 for a plate that comes with a simple salad, and you'll want nothing else.

#### Assyrtiko Wine — Volcanic Terroir in a Glass

Not a food, but you cannot eat in Santorini without drinking what grows here. Assyrtiko grapes grow in basket-shaped vines close to the ground, protected from the wind, drawing minerals from volcanic soil. The wine is crisp and bone-dry, with a minerality that tastes like wet stone, like the island itself somehow made liquid. Notes of citrus, sometimes a hint of salinity, always that electric acidity that makes it perfect with seafood.

Visit Santo Wines or Venetsanos Winery for tastings with caldera views (€15–30 depending on the flight). But also just order it by the glass at dinner — €5–8 — and let it introduce you to every dish.

#### Baklava — The Sweet Finish

Layers upon layers of phyllo, each one brushed with butter, filled with crushed pistachios and walnuts, then drenched in honey syrup scented with orange blossom. The top shatters when you press your fork through. The bottom is soaked and syrupy. The nuts provide texture and an earthy richness that keeps the sweetness from being overwhelming.

Bakeries in Fira sell it by the piece (€3–5), wrapped in paper for you to eat on the go. Or order it at the end of a long meal, with Greek coffee, and let the sweetness linger as the bill comes.

## How to Eat Like a Local

Lunch is the big meal here — Greeks eat late, often not sitting down until 2 or 3 PM, and dinner might not happen until 9 or 10. Order multiple small plates to share rather than individual entrées; this is how the table works in Greece. Meze culture means the food comes when it's ready, not in courses, so don't be alarmed when dishes arrive in no particular order.

Bread comes automatically and is usually not free — it's added to the bill as a small cover charge, €1–2 per person. Tipping 5–10% is standard at sit-down restaurants, usually left in cash on the table even if you pay by card.

And book ahead for caldera-view restaurants, especially for dinner. A week in advance during peak season, at least a few days in shoulder months. The sunset tables go fast, but honestly? Lunch with that same view, when the light is softer and the crowds thinner, might be the better meal anyway.
