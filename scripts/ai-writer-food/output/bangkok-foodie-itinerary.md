Bangkok doesn't ease you into eating — it ambushes you. The moment you step outside, the smell of charcoal smoke and fish sauce hits, and suddenly you're standing at a plastic table at 9 AM wondering how a bowl of noodles can taste this good. I spent three days eating with the kind of focus usually reserved for religious devotion, and what follows is everything I learned: where to go, what to order, and how to move through this city without wasting a single meal.

## Before You Eat: Quick Notes

Thais eat constantly — there's no rigid breakfast-lunch-dinner structure, so embrace grazing. Most street stalls are cash only; carry small bills (20s and 100s). Tipping isn't expected at markets but 10% is appreciated at restaurants. Remove your shoes if a restaurant has floor seating. Download Grab for transport between neighborhoods — trying to walk Bangkok in the heat will destroy your appetite. Book any high-end dinner at least 48 hours ahead.

## Day 1: Old Bangkok and the Flavors That Built This City

### Morning: Rattanakosin

Start in the old city before the heat and crowds arrive. Near Wat Pho, you'll find vendors serving *jok* — Thai rice porridge — from carts that have been there for decades. This isn't the thin congee you might know; it's thick, warming, and topped with a slow-cooked egg, ginger, and crispy fried garlic. A bowl costs 40–50 baht ($1.20–$1.50). Pair it with *pa tong ko*, the Thai-Chinese fried dough sticks, for dipping.

After eating, walk to Wat Pho to see the reclining Buddha before 9 AM, when the tour buses arrive. You'll have worked up a second appetite by the time you're done.

**Morning spend**: $2–$5

### Afternoon: Chinatown (Yaowarat Road)

By noon, take a taxi or the MRT to Wat Mangkon station and surface into Chinatown. Yaowarat Road is overwhelming — that's the point. For lunch, find a shophouse serving **boat noodles** (*kuay teow reua*). These come in tiny bowls, rich and dark from pork blood and soy, each one just a few bites. You're meant to eat four or five. Look for places with a stack of empty bowls on the table — locals count them to tally your bill. Around 15–20 baht per bowl ($0.50–$0.60); you'll spend $3–$5 total and leave properly full.

Spend the afternoon wandering the narrow sois (alleys) branching off Yaowarat. This is snacking territory: roasted chestnuts, coconut pancakes, dried shrimp, and whatever catches your eye. Grab a Thai iced tea from a cart — it should be aggressively orange and tooth-achingly sweet. Budget $5–$8 for afternoon snacking.

**Afternoon spend**: $8–$13

### Evening: Chinatown After Dark

Stay in Yaowarat for dinner — the neighborhood transforms when the sun goes down. The street becomes one continuous outdoor restaurant. Find a stall serving **tom yum goong**, the hot and sour shrimp soup that defines Thai cooking. The best versions use river prawns the size of your hand, and the broth should make your lips tingle and your nose run. Expect to pay 150–250 baht ($4.50–$7.50) for a proper bowl.

For a sit-down option, try one of Chinatown's old-school seafood restaurants where you pick your fish from ice trays and they cook it your way. A meal with two or three dishes and a beer runs 400–800 baht ($12–$24) per person.

**Evening spend**: $15–$30

**Day 1 Total**: Budget ~$25 | Mid ~$50 | Splurge ~$90

## Day 2: Modern Bangkok and the Art of the Noodle

### Morning: Sukhumvit

Today you eat like a Bangkok office worker. Take the BTS to Phrom Phong or Thong Lo and find one of the shophouse restaurants serving **khao pad** — Thai fried rice. This isn't a side dish; it's an art form. Jasmine rice, wok-fried at volcanic heat with egg, garlic, and your choice of protein (crab is traditional and worth the splurge). A good plate comes with a wedge of lime, sliced cucumber, and a small bowl of fish sauce with chilies. Expect 60–120 baht ($1.80–$3.50).

**Morning spend**: $2–$5

### Afternoon: Cooking Class + Talat Noi

This is the day to learn, not just eat. Book a half-day cooking class that includes a market visit — the best ones take you to Khlong Toei Market, Bangkok's largest wet market, where you'll see ingredients you won't find back home. You'll learn to make classics like **pad Thai** and **som tum** (green papaya salad), and you'll eat everything you cook. Classes run 1,200–2,500 baht ($35–$75) depending on the operation.

After class, if you have energy, take a taxi to **Talat Noi**, the hidden street art quarter near Chinatown. This old neighborhood has Portuguese and Chinese roots, narrow lanes, and a few small cafes serving proper Thai coffee. It's quiet, strange, and feels like a secret. Grab an iced coffee and something sweet for $3–$5.

**Afternoon spend**: $40–$80

### Evening: Silom

Head to Silom for dinner. This business district empties out after dark, but the food stays excellent. Find a restaurant serving **khao soi** if you can — this Northern Thai curry noodle dish isn't native to Bangkok but the city does it well: coconut curry broth, egg noodles, crispy noodle topping, pickled mustard greens. It's rich, layered, and costs 80–150 baht ($2.50–$4.50) at a simple place.

For a splurge, book ahead at one of Silom's Thai fine-dining spots where they deconstruct street food into something theatrical. A tasting menu runs 2,500–4,500 baht ($75–$135), not including wine. You won't eat better pad Thai anywhere.

**Evening spend**: $10–$140

**Day 2 Total**: Budget ~$55 | Mid ~$95 | Splurge ~$225

## Day 3: The Local Side and a Proper Goodbye

### Morning: Thonburi and the Floating Market

Wake up early and cross the river to the quieter west bank. On weekends, **Khlong Lat Mayom floating market** is worth the trip — it's where Bangkok families go, not tourists. Boats sell noodles, grilled seafood, and coconut sweets directly from the water. Eat boat noodles again (they're different here, lighter), try grilled river prawns with a tamarind dipping sauce, and finish with coconut ice cream served in the shell. The whole morning of eating runs $8–$15.

If it's a weekday, explore the canal communities around the Artist House at Klong Bang Luang instead — traditional puppet shows happen in the afternoons, and the area has small local restaurants serving central Thai home cooking.

**Morning spend**: $8–$15

### Afternoon: Chatuchak Area

On weekends, Chatuchak Market is mandatory — 15,000 stalls across 35 acres. You're not here to shop; you're here to graze. Look for the food section (it's enormous) and try whatever has a line: coconut water straight from the shell, grilled pork skewers, mango sticky rice made fresh, Thai-style sausages. This is aggressive snacking. Budget $10–$20 and expect to walk out overfull.

On weekdays, head to the Or Tor Kor Market next to Chatuchak instead. It's an upscale wet market with the best produce in the city, ready-to-eat curries, and pristine tropical fruit. Buy a plate of **mango sticky rice** here — the mangoes are stupidly good, the sticky rice is warm, and the coconut cream is rich and salted. Around 100–150 baht ($3–$4.50).

**Afternoon spend**: $10–$25

### Evening: The Farewell Meal

Your last dinner should be the meal you'll remember. Two options:

**The romantic finale**: Book a riverside table at a restaurant along the Chao Phraya. Order a whole fried fish with green mango salad, a proper **tom yum goong**, and whatever curry they're known for. Watch the boats pass, watch Wat Arun light up across the water. This runs 1,500–3,500 baht ($45–$105) for two people.

**The street food victory lap**: Return to Yaowarat one more time. Eat everything you missed the first night. Get the oyster omelet. Get the grilled squid. Get another round of boat noodles. End with mango sticky rice from a cart. You'll spend $20–$30 and leave knowing you did this right.

**Evening spend**: $25–$105

**Day 3 Total**: Budget ~$45 | Mid ~$75 | Splurge ~$145

## Pro Tips for Food Travelers

- **Follow the smoke.** The best grilled meats and satays come from stalls with active charcoal — if it's not smoking, it's not turning over fast enough.
- **Eat where you see stacked plates.** High turnover means fresh food. If a noodle stall has no customers at noon, there's a reason.
- **Carry tissues and hand sanitizer.** Street stalls don't always have napkins, and you'll want to wipe down plastic stools in the heat.
- **Learn to say "mai phet" (not spicy) and "phet nit noi" (a little spicy).** Thai spicy can hospitalize the unprepared. There's no shame in asking.
